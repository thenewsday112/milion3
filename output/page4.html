Carne Guisada

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 1 boneless beef chuck roast (3 pounds), cut into 2-inch pieces
  • 3/4 teaspoon salt, divided
  • 1/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/2 teaspoon paprika
  • 2 tablespoons canola oil
  • 1 medium onion, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1 jalapeno pepper, seeded and chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 bottle (12 ounces) beer
  • 2 cups beef broth
  • 4 teaspoons Worcestershire sauce
  • 2 to 3 teaspoons crushed red pepper flakes
  • 2 teaspoons chili powder
  • 1-1/2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 2 large unpeeled red potatoes, chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon red wine vinegar
  • Tortillas, hot cooked rice, lime wedges and chopped fresh cilantro, optional
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. In a large bowl, toss beef and 1/4 teaspoon salt. In a separate large bowl, combine flour and paprika. Add beef, a few pieces at a time, and toss to coat.
  2. In a Dutch oven, heat oil over medium heat. Brown beef in batches, adding additional oil as necessary. Remove with a slotted spoon. In same pan, add onion; cook and stir until tender, 3-5 minutes. Stir in tomato paste, jalapeno and garlic; cook until fragrant and color starts to darken slightly. Add browned beef back to the pan. Add beer; bring to a boil, scraping the bottom to release browned bits. Simmer until reduced by half, about 5 minutes. Stir in broth, Worcestershire sauce, crushed red pepper flakes, chili powder, cumin, bay leaf and remaining 1/2 teaspoon salt; bring to a boil. Reduce heat; cover and simmer until meat is almost tender, about 1-1/2 hours.
  3. Add potatoes and red wine vinegar. Cover; simmer until meat and vegetables are tender, about 30 minutes longer.
  4. Discard bay leaf. Using the back of a spoon or ladle, slightly mash potatoes. If desired, shred pork slightly with 2 forks. Serve tortillas, rice, lime and cilantro.

Nutrition Facts

1 cup: 347 calories, 16g fat (5g saturated fat), 88mg cholesterol, 445mg sodium, 19g carbohydrate (2g sugars, 2g fiber), 29g protein.

Skillet Pizza with Cider Caramelized Onions & Pumpkin Ricotta.

This caramelized onion skillet pizza with pumpkin ricotta is delicious for the fall season! Apple cider caramelized onions are mixed with pumpkin ricotta cheese, mozzarella and fresh sage for the most autumn coded pizza ever!

It’s pizza Friday!

This pizza is made in a cast iron skillet and has a layer of apple cider caramelized onions, fresh sage and pumpkin ricotta cheese. It tastes like fall in a pizza!

And I am loving it. 

In the most fall-like combination ever, I’m combining BOTH apple cider and pumpkin!! This is the perfect day to enjoy it because come November 1, I will most likely be in full holiday mode. Stopping only for one or two days to fully celebrate Thanksgiving, of course.

I’ve been making apple cider caramelized onions since I first shared them in my second book almost ten years ago. Not only is the flavor fabulous, but the cider helps the onions cook and caramelize so much quicker than they normally would. This is great, because it’s definitely a high maintenance step for pizza.

I always look at it as: I can caramelize my onions while my pizza dough rises! Easy as that. 

The cast iron pizza method has been a favorite of mine for well over a decade. It’s easy and simple, but does require moving a little quickly. The only time I had a minor fiasco is when I dropped the cast iron skillet ON THE FLOOR a few weeks after having Max. I wanted to make us cast iron skillet pizza so badly but I was still trying to get my footing postpartum.

Not only did I drop the skillet, but the pizza came OUT of the skillet and landed cheese-side on the floor. Whomp whomp.

Other than that, a cast iron pizza is the best. It’s so easy to make. The method is so quick and easy that you may wonder if you’re doing it wrong!

The key? A super hot cast iron skillet and the broiler in your oven. 

Oh and one more thing: all of your toppings have to be out and ready to go. Similar to my grilled pizza method, just get everything ready and you’ll be golden. It will come together in minutes! 

First, I heat the cast iron skillet so it’s super hot. In a way, this sort of mimics a wood-fired pizza because the cast iron will create some of those golden, puffed burned bits. Once the skillet has been heating for a few minutes, I press my dough right in there. It’s the exact shape of the skillet, and it’s okay to take a few seconds to press it around the edges. 

Next, I build the pizza right there. Sauce, veggies, meats, cheese – everything. I build it right on the dough, while the dough is cooking in the skillet. This should take one to two minutes, max.

At this point, the edges of the dough will be bubbling up and you can see that it’s starting to cook! 

Then, you pop the entire skillet (it’s hot!!) into the oven, directly under the broiler. This should only be under the broiler for another one (maaaaaybe two) minutes. But you need to be watching it the entire time. Everything can burn quickly! (more…)

Every year during Thanksgiving, I feel compelled to make a turkey dinner because I love a good tradition. But as a Thai, I can't help but put some Thai twists on it for a little bolder and more complex flavours. In this recipe, turkey breast and thigh are marinated in a classic Thai meat marinade, then roasted on a bed of lemongrass and other Thai herbs. The herb-infused drippings get turned into our coconut gravy. It is sublime. If you still want a traditional vibe to your turkey dinner, but are getting tired of the same old, this recipe is for you! It can also be made gluten free.

Note: Over the years I have decided that my favourite way to roast turkey is to roast breast and thighs instead of roasting a whole turkey. This yields the juiciest, most flavourful meat, a short cooking time, easiest carving, and you can scale it up or down to suit the size of your party. And I think the serving is still just as beautiful! If you still want to roast a whole turkey, I will provide some guidance.

TL;DR - Recipe Overview

Here's a quick snapshot of what's involved, so you can plan accordingly!

The turkey: Turkey breasts and/or thighs are first marinated; I will explain why I prefer roasting parts rather than a whole turkey. They're roasted on a bed of lemongrass, galangal, lime leaves, and shallots.

The gravy: The marinade and the herb-infused drippings are added into the gravy, which is then enriched with coconut milk.

How long it takes: Give the meat at least 3 hours to marinate and up to overnight. Roasting takes only about 45 minutes, plus 15-20 mins resting time. The gravy can be started while the turkey is roasting, and finished during resting.

What it can be served with: This will still work perfectly with your traditional sides - mashed potatoes, stuffing, salads, roasted veg, etc. I will provide some Thai side ideas below.

Optional flair: To make it a little more Thai, serve with the classic Thai dipping sauce for meat, nam jim jaew.

Why You Should Roast Turkey Parts Instead of a Whole Bird

I know there’s nothing more impressive than a whole roasted turkey in the middle of the Thanksgiving table, but let me try to convince you that if you prioritize flavour, juiciness AND practicality, you should roast turkey parts instead.

The Problem with Roasting a Whole Turkey

  • It’s nearly impossible to cook evenly. The bird is so big that the outside usually overcooks before the inside is done, or the breasts are dry by the time the thighs are cooked through. You can try to keep it juicy by brining or dry-brining, but that adds a long, cumbersome step and requires a lot of fridge space.
  • It’s hard to marinate. This recipe is so flavourful because of the Thai marinade. To marinate a whole bird, you’d need a giant bucket of marinade, which is impractical and wasteful.
  • It’s pretty stressful! Cooking a whole turkey is basically a whole-afternoon ordeal, and we all know the nervous moment at the end when you’re not sure whether you’re about to cut into a bird that’s raw inside or completely dry. Turkey parts take a fraction of the time and are so much easier to temp properly. Most importantly, you can take the breast and thigh out at different times, so each part cooks to its optimal doneness!

Roasting turkey parts solves all of these issues. And while you don’t get the classic whole-bird presentation, I think turkey breast and thigh on a platter of lemongrass is just as beautiful and impressive!

Can I Use This Recipe for a Spatchcocked Turkey?

If you REALLY want a whole bird, a good compromise is to spatchcock your turkey (or, ideally, have a butcher do it), which helps it cook more evenly. Marinating will still be a challenge, but it’s more doable than with a round bird.

If you have experience spatchcocking, you can use this recipe’s marinade, the herb bed, and the gravy, but you’ll need to consult another trusted recipe, created specifically for spatchcocked turkey, for the cooking time and temperature.

Keep in mind that this marinade contains sugar, so the turkey will brown faster than usual. If you’re extending the cooking time, you’ll likely need to lower the temperature.

Important Ingredient Notes

Here are all the important notes on ingredients, including substitutions. Amounts are in the full recipe card below!

Turkey

  • Turkey breast and/or thigh. I chose to use one boneless breast and one bone-in thigh so I have both dark and white meat to choose from, just like a whole turkey! If you have a strong preference, you can go with 2 breasts or 4 thighs. Roasting in parts also allows you to scale up according to the number of people, so you can do 2 breasts and 2 thighs if you've got a larger party. You can even add 2 drumsticks and you'll basically have a whole turkey, but much easier to roast!
  • Lemongrass. You want whole stalks of lemongrass as these will form the "bed" for your turkey to roast on. Frozen whole lemongrass works fine for this.
  • Makrut lime leaves. These will add a beautiful, citrusy aroma to the gravy which will help make the meal feel fresher. Fresh or frozen work well.
  • Galangal. Optional, but adds lovely flavour if you can find it! Frozen is fine.
  • Ginger. I don't normally add ginger to the "Thai trinity" that is the 3 previous herbs, but since this isn't meant to be traditional, I think it works quite well!
  • Shallots. Red onions or regular onions can also work. These will soften and naturally sweeten the drippings.

Marinade

  • White and black peppercorns. I like to use both black and white as they both have different flavours, but you can choose one or the other.
  • Garlic
  • Cilantro stems, or cilantro roots. Traditionally we would use cilantro roots for marinades as they are very aromatic and hold up well in pastes. But cilantro stems work great as a substitute.
  • Fish sauce. Use good quality fish sauce such as Squid, Megachef or Red Boat. You can read more about how to choose good fish sauce here.
  • Soy sauce. I use Thai soy sauce, but any kind of regular or light soy sauce you have will work. To make this gluten free, use gluten free soy sauce.
  • Black or dark soy sauce. This is added for colour, so the turkey will darken beautifully. You do not have to add it. If making gluten free, you can omit, or use GF dark soy sauce (though I have not seen that yet!)
  • Palm sugar. The sweetness will balance the saltiness of the soy sauce and fish sauce. You can sub an equal amount of brown sugar.

Gravy

  • Chicken stock, or turkey stock, of course! The most important thing is to make sure your stock is unsalted or low sodium, as we will be adding soy sauce and fish sauce into it. Store-bought is fine, but if you wanna DIY here's my recipe for an Asian style chicken stock.
  • Coconut milk. This will add a little creaminess and coconut aroma to our gravy. My favourite brand is Aroy-D in UHT paper carton. It is leaps and bounds better than canned coconut milk!! Read more about that in my Ultimate Guide to Coconut Milk.
  • Cornstarch. Gravy thickener. You can use all-purpose flour instead if you wish.
  • Lime juice or lemon juice (optional). You may or may not need this, but I find that if the gravy tastes a little flat or rich, a little squeeze of lime or lemon can sharpen the flavours and make it so much more palatable. You can decide whether to add it at the end when you are doing final taste adjustment for the gravy.
  • Black or dark soy sauce (optional). If you want your gravy a little darker, which always makes it look more delicious, an extra glug of dark or black soy sauce does the trick!

How to Make - A Bird's Eye View

Here's a bird's eye view of the process. These are NOT the full instructions, they're here to give you a visual guide of what you'll be doing. When you're ready to cook, cook from the full recipe card with detailed instructions, and you can also watch the video tutorial where I talk you through how I did it!

  1. Make the marinade: Pound peppercorns in a mortar and pestle until fine. Add the garlic and cilantro stems and pound into a fine paste. You can also finely mince the cilantro stems, grate the garlic, and use a pepper grinder.
  1. Add the palm sugar and pound until mostly dissolved, then add fish sauce, soy sauce, black/dark soy sauce and water, stir to combine.
  1. Place the turkey breast and thigh into a flat dish just large enough to hold them (they can be in 2 separate dishes, which is what I did). Or you can put them into zip top bags. Pour the marinade over the turkey, putting about ⅔ of it for the breast and ⅓ for the thigh. Flip the turkey over to thoroughly coat and then marinate for at least 3 hours and up to overnight.
    On roasting day: Pull the turkey from the fridge 2-3 hours before roasting so it is not fridge cold going into the oven. Preheat the oven to 450°F (230°C).
  1. Prepare the roasting pan: Cut the lemongrass stalks in half then smash them with a pestle or the back of a knife until the stalks are split open. Place them on the roasting pan, spreading them out enough to create a bed for your turkey. Scatter the galangal, lime leaves, shallots and ginger all around the herb bed.
  1. Place the turkey on the herbs (don't discard the marinade!) and then place into the oven on the middle rack. Pour water into the pan so it's about ¼ inch deep. Roast for 35 - 45 minutes, depending on the size; check at 30 mins. You want the internal temp of the breast to be 145-150°F (63°C), and the thigh should reach at least 170°F (77°C). Keep an eye on the water and don't let it dry or the drippings could burn. Add a little more water as needed!
    *You will likely need to remove the breast before the thigh. If they are browning too quickly, reduce the heat to 400°F (200°C).
  1. Once the turkey is done, place the meat on a plate to rest, then take all the herbs and arrange them on a serving platter (this is just for presentation, do not eat the herbs!). Put the turkey on top of the herbs and allow to rest for 20 mins before slicing.
  1. This is the liquid I had left in the roasting pan. Mine is dark because I accidentally let the water dry up halfway through, so the drippings browned onto the pan and I deglazed it with extra water. If yours is not brown that is okay!
  1. For the gravy: In a pot, add the chicken stock and the reserved turkey marinade and bring to a boil to cook the raw turkey juices in the marinade. Pour the turkey drippings into the gravy pot (carefully!).
  1. Add the coconut milk and bring to a boil. Then taste for seasoning. If it tastes bland, reduce until the seasoning tastes right. If it is too salty, add more unsalted chicken stock or coconut milk to dilute. Once the seasoning is right, dissolve the cornstarch with a bit of water, then pour about half of the slurry into the gravy while you whisk until it comes to a boil. If you want it thicker, add more slurry. Do a final taste and add a squeeze of lime or lemon juice if you feel like it needs it.
  1. Make sure the turkey has rested for at least 20 minutes before slicing, then slice and serve with your sides of choice!

What to Serve with Thai-Style Gravy

Because the Thai flavours used here are subtle, any of your traditional Thanksgiving sides will work with this turkey, from mashed potatoes and stuffing to roasted veggies. But if you want to keep the Thai theme going, you can do what I did!

I served mine with my 5-spice roasted cauliflower and som tum pla ra, a spicy, umami-packed green papaya salad that brings a fresh crunch to balance the rich gravy. For this som tum I used store-bought broccoli slaw to make it more convenient since I was already making so many other things, and it worked fantastically as a papaya substitute. I also had mashed potatoes, which worked well with those two dishes.

For something even more Thai, serve with nam jim jaew, the dipping sauce Thai people must have whenever we serve grilled or roasted meats. Serve it on the side for anyone who wants something spicy and bold!

Thai Marinated Roast Turkey with Lemongrass Coconut Gravy

Turkey breast and thigh marinated in a classic Thai meat marinade, then roasted on a bed of lemongrass and other Thai herbs. The herb-infused drippings get turned into a rich coconut gravy. Can be made gluten free. Course Main Course Cuisine Thai Prep Time 30 minutes minutes Cook Time 1 hour hour Marinating 3 hours hours Total Time 4 hours hours 30 minutes minutes Servings 6 servings

Equipment

  • mortar and pestle
  • Meat thermometer very important in making sure the meat is cooked properly!

Ingredients

Turkey

  • 1 turkey breast boneless, skin on
  • 1 turkey thigh bone in, skin on, see note 1
  • 8 stalks lemongrass cut the stalks in half
  • 15 thin slices galangal
  • 10 makrut lime leaves roughly torn into chunks
  • 15 thin slices ginger optional
  • 2 heads shallots roughly chopped
  • 1-2 cups water

Marinade

  • ½ teaspoon white peppercorns
  • ½ teaspoon black peppercorns
  • 6 cloves garlic
  • 8 cilantro stems chopped, or use 3 cilantro roots
  • 1 tablespoon palm sugar finely chopped, or sub brown sugar
  • 3 tablespoon fish sauce
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce sub GF soy sauce if needed
  • 1 teaspoon black or dark soy sauce optional, for colour
  • ¼ cup water

Gravy

  • 1 cup chicken stock unsalted or low sodium
  • ¼ cup coconut milk
  • 3 tablespoon cornstarch or more as needed
  • A squeeze of lime juice or lemon juice may not need
  • Black or dark soy sauce optional for colour

Instructions

Marinate the turkey

  • Pound peppercorns in a mortar and pestle until fine. Add the garlic and cilantro stems and pound into a fine paste. Add the palm sugar and pound until mostly dissolved. Add fish sauce, soy sauce, black/dark soy sauce and water, stir to combine. ½ tsp white peppercorns, ½ tsp black peppercorns, 6 cloves garlic, 8 cilantro stems, 1 Tbsp palm sugar, 3 Tbsp fish sauce, 1 Tbsp soy sauce, 1 tsp black or dark soy sauce, ¼ cup water
  • Place the turkey breast and thigh into a flat dish just large enough to hold them (they can be in 2 separate dishes, that’s what I do), alternatively you can put them into separate zip top bags. Pour the marinade over the turkey, putting about ⅔ of it for the breast and ⅓ for the thigh. Flip the turkey over to thoroughly coat and then marinate for at least 3 hours and up to overnight. 1 turkey breast, 1 turkey thigh
    • Important: Make sure you flip the turkey halfway through your marinating time for even distribution. Also important: A 24-hour marinade is fine for the breast but too long for the thigh and it is a bit too salty, so if you’re doing an overnight marinade, remove the thigh from the marinade first thing in the morning.

Roast the turkey

  • Roasting day: Remove the turkey from the fridge 2-3 hours before roasting so it is not fridge cold going into the oven.
  • Preheat the oven to 450°F and place the rack so that the turkey will be in the middle of the oven.
    • I recommend using an oven thermometer to make sure you are at 450°F; most ovens are inaccurate, and if it’s too hot, the skin might burn too quickly. My oven, for example, has to be set at 430°F to achieve 450°F.
  • Cut the lemongrass stalks in half then smash them with a pestle or the back of a knife until the stalks are split open and bruised. Place the lemongrass on the baking sheet or roasting pan, spreading them out enough to create a bed for your turkey. Scatter the galangal, lime leaves, shallots and ginger all around the herb bed. 8 stalks lemongrass, 15 thin slices galangal, 10 makrut lime leaves, 15 thin slices ginger, 2 heads shallots
  • Remove the turkey pieces from the marinade and reserve the marinade as we will need it for the gravy. Place the turkey on top of the herb bed, skin side up, leaving plenty of space between the two pieces to allow air circulation. Place the roasting pan in the oven, and then pour the water into the roasting pan so there's about ¼ inch (half a centimeter) of water in the pan; this will extract flavours from the herbs and prevent the drippings from burning. How much water you need depends on how large your roasting pan is, so add as much as necessary. 1-2 cups water
  • Roast the turkey for 35 - 45 minutes, timing will depend entirely on how big your pieces are so I would check them at 30 minutes. You want the internal temp of your breast to be 145-150°F (63°C), and the thigh should reach at least 170°F (77°C). You will likely need to remove the breast before the thigh. If they are browning too quickly, reduce the heat to 400°F (200°C).
    • Important: Keep an eye on the roasting pan and add more water as needed to ensure there is always a shallow puddle of water to extract flavour from the herbs and prevent the drippings from burning (but don’t add a ton of water). If you accidentally let the water dry up and the drippings have browned (as I did in the process photos above), as long as it's not completely burnt, that’s totally okay, just deglaze with water. Your gravy will just be darker and probably a little more flavourful!

Make the gravy

  • While the turkey roasts, start the gravy. In a pot, add the chicken stock and the reserved marinade and bring to a boil to cook the raw turkey juices in the marinade. It will curdle a bit, this is fine, just whisk it and the curdles will break apart. Hold it here and wait for the turkey drippings. 1 cup chicken stock
  • Once the turkey is done, place the meat on a plate to rest, then take the lemongrass and all the herbs and arrange them on a serving platter.
  • Finish the gravy: Carefully pour the drippings from the roasting pan into the gravy pot, and add the coconut milk. If when you get here, you have collected some juices from the resting turkey, you can pour that in as well. If you want the colour to be darker, you can add a little more dark/black soy sauce; but not too much as you don't want to make it too salty. ¼ cup coconut milk, Black or dark soy sauce
  • Bring the gravy to a boil, then taste for seasoning. If it tastes bland, boil it for a while to reduce until the seasoning tastes right. If it is too salty, add more unsalted chicken stock or coconut milk to dilute.
  • Once the seasoning tastes right, dissolve the cornstarch with a bit of water, then pour about half of the slurry into the gravy while you whisk. Bring the gravy to a full boil, whisking constantly, and assess the thickness of the gravy. If you want it thicker, add more cornstarch slurry. 3 Tbsp cornstarch
  • Do a final taste of the gravy, and if it could use a little acidity to brighten, add a squeeze of lemon or lime. A squeeze of lime juice or lemon juice
  • Put the turkey on the herb bed on the serving platter, and there’s your beautiful turkey ready for the table! Make sure the turkey has rested for at least 20 minutes before slicing. Enjoy!

Food-Themed Fashion Is Starting to Feel Gluttonous

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Loewe | Loewe

There was a time, not too long ago, when a purse shaped like a baguette felt like a thrilling — even subversive — novelty. Now, we hardly bat an eye when we see that Moschino has made a trompe l’oeil celery purse, or that Big Bud Press is making a hot-dog-and-pretzel-print jumpsuit in collaboration with a Los Angeles bar. How did we get here? And has it all become a little too much? 

Fashion has long found inspiration from the culinary world, but the influx of food-fueled prints, graphics, and trompe l’oeil accessories that picked up steam during the pandemic have now reached a cultural tipping point. 

Today, there are truly countless examples of these food motifs and edible, wearable trompe l’oeil creations, at all price points. Loewe’s summer collection featured a $4,150 lambskin tomato clutch, while Walmart’s included a line of rotisserie chicken bag-like purses and charms starting at $5.97. As with many trends, this trajectory started in high fashion and worked its way down to mass-market ubiquity. In 2020, a chocolate chip cookie went down one of New York Fashion Week’s most buzzed-about fall runways. It protruded from a black purse by the designer Puppets and Puppets, a design that quickly went viral for its whimsical inclusion of a classic dessert on a high-end handbag. 

The then-emerging brand made food a central theme of its vision: Its cake-topped aviator caps, Ferrero Rocher heels, and beeswax-sculpted cheese hats — hyperrealistic, absurdist food designs capable of achieving instant social media virality. Prior to working in fashion, Puppets designer Carly Mark was working as an artist whose pieces revolved around candies and snacks. As she told Business of Fashion in 2023, the Puppets universe felt like the ideal pairing given what she considers food and fashion’s motivators: pleasure and money. 

View this post on Instagram

During the pandemic, the much-duped “strawberry dress” took over TikTok, and Tombolo Company found a hit on its hands with its vongole shirt, which debuted in 2019, but found peak popularity in 2021. The shirt appeared in Vogue in 2020, and InsideHook called it an “obvious bedfellow to day-drinks, rooftops and weekenders” — in other words, a status symbol. Katie Kimmel’s T-shirts made chicken-parm enthusiasm akin to being a devout sports fan, and in 2022, this very publication called “seafood kitsch” the look of the summer, as items like Rachel Antonoff’s super-popular seafood-tower dress flew off the digital shelves. 

Why was food so primed to become a motif in clothing at this point in culture? One could make the argument that it has roots in the millennial optimism years: Earlier in the 2000s and 2010s, the concept of “foodies” was new. Brands printed tongue-in-cheek food- and coffee-related motifs and slogans (a fervent love of avocados comes to mind) on hipster apparel, as the idea that “you are what you eat” developed growing fervor. That period was a key stepping stone in the evolution toward the far more elaborate and pervasive marketability of food-as-identity we see today. Content creator Cole Poska, who is a partner and creative director at Beyond Studios, tells Eater that “food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.” Poska’s own Instagram often reimagines restaurant merch just for fun; his designs for Cheesecake Factory apparel went viral, such as a hat that resembles the iconic brown bread. He points out that people will defend their favorite fast-food order like it’s a family member, and with the kind of loyalty that’s hard to manufacture. The same could be said about their martini order, or favorite taco spot; it’s all a way of telling others who we are through what we like.

View this post on Instagram

But the fatigue is undoubtedly here. In 2024, Bon Appétit asked “Why Does Every Dress Have Pasta and Cocktail Shrimp on It?” And as the novelty factor of a shrimp-print dress has worn off, the next question might be: Will there be any true longevity for these food-as-fashion moments? Our feeds overflow with products that are hurriedly designed for flash-in-the-pan viral trends and quickly destined for a landfill; arguably more so if the object in question prioritizes shock value and gimmicks over function and brand identity. 

This is undoubtedly all related to the accelerated speed of trend cycles and shortened consumer attention spans. However, as Leah Asmelash wrote last year for CNN, the rising costs of food also casts an increasingly sinister shadow over food-themed fashion. In 2026, a $425 cardigan emblazoned with oysters has waning originality compared to a few years ago; it falls into banality at best and the Hunger Games at worst; its wearer indicating that they’re financially comfortable and, more importantly, well-fed. In that light, beyond its role in identity, food has become more of a status symbol than ever. A sweater touting one’s love of caviar is shorthand for their access to and appreciation of luxury, culinary and otherwise. But sporting the mass-market Temu version? It’s more akin to wearing a faux Gucci belt.  

“Food brands have something most brands would kill for: decades of nostalgia, iconic identities, and consumers who are borderline obsessed with them.”

Cole Poska

If it’s possible to escape the sense of accelerated-trend-cycle disposability, it may have to be through a more subtle approach than, say, a bag that looks like a giant stick of butter. Sam Salad is the co-founder of Meals, a Los Angeles-based, workwear-inspired apparel company that, by its own description, “bakes ‘food culture’ into apparel, blurring the lines between the clothes we eat and the food we wear.” Utility is at the forefront of Meals, which offers a classic chef pant with a slightly fuller cut, in both basic colors and subdued aesthetic homages to blue cheese and watermelon. When Meals launched in 2019, Salad was selling premium denim along Venice’s Abbot Kinney Boulevard, a hotbed of Instagrammy food culture and trendy restaurants like Gjelina that helped inspire his own brand. “Design today has to translate across languages and platforms,” Salad tells Eater. 

Food is hardly a new bedfellow for fashion designers, even if today’s hyper-fast trend cycle has kicked it into overdrive. “Food is totally universal,” Salad says. “Issey Miyake once merchandised their pleats collection as little omakase sets.”  He cites that collection among other fashion-meets-food moments that he considers successful, such as the iconic Salvador Dalí- and Elsa Schiaparelli-designed lobster dress. At the time of its 1937 creation, the dress was a scandal: It assumed a conformity-defying sartorial stance and was fittingly worn by Wallace Simpson. Today, its highly devolved spawn are available on Shein for $13. 

As it stands, one clear way out of corny, food motif purgatory is for brands — fashion or food — to focus less on quantity and more on storytelling and quality. “We do a few things really well, and that’s enough,” Salad says, “Like In-N-Out [Burger].” 

Poska’s philosophy is similar. “I’d rather make one thing people talk about for a year than 20 things people forget about tomorrow,” he says. “The first person to make a candle look like a stick of butter is a genius. The hundredth person is just making a candle.”

Before the first kitchen timer goes off, international trade duties have already driven up the cost of your holiday dinner. Between a historic shortage in American cattle herds raising beef prices sky-high, tariffs on tinplate steel inflating the price of canned goods, and foreign trade duties hitting European cheeses, wines, and sweets, hosting a holiday meal in 2026 can feel like walking a tightrope. I’ve been seeing these price hikes firsthand while shopping, and it’s definitely made me rethink a few of my usual grocery picks.

And while we can’t control international trade policies, we can outsmart them at the grocery store!

Your Guide to a Budget-Friendly Holiday Feast

Hosting family shouldn’t mean taking on a mountain of stress or spending your whole paycheck on dinner. With a few smart, budget-savvy pivots, I’m here to help you put together a celebratory feast that tastes incredible, looks festive, and sidesteps the latest markups on the shelves. Alongside my favorite budget-saving swaps and shopping tips, we’ve also put together a complete holiday menu that feeds eight people for around $45!

TL;DR: The 4 Smartest Swaps for a Tariff-Proof Holiday Table

Instead of buying the exact same grocery list you’ve used over the holidays for years, we found making these four simple adjustments will help keep your receipt total down:

  1. Ditch the canned goods: Tariffs on tinplate steel and imported aluminum have turned these former budget heroes into surprise budget busters. Buying canned pumpkin puree, canned green beans, canned yams, and canned cranberry sauce means you’re paying a hefty markup just for the packaging. Stick to fresh, bagged, or frozen U.S. produce instead. It’s cheaper, avoids the packaging surcharge, and often tastes noticeably better.
  2. Rethink the centerpiece protein: Avoid expensive beef roasts and foreign seafood this holiday season, both of which are carrying markups right now. Focus on domestic whole turkeys, bone-in turkey breasts, pork roasts, or glazed holiday hams. These proteins rely on local agricultural supply chains and are largely insulated from foreign trade taxes. We found whole turkey for as little as $0.85 per pound at Walmart, making it a fantastic budget-friendly centerpiece!
  3. Keep your dairy and drinks domestic: European cheeses like French Brie and Italian Parmigiano-Reggiano, along with wine and imported European butters, are carrying heavy import taxes. The U.S. produces both dairy and wine, so if you’re avoiding tariffs, they make a great swap. Switch to Wisconsin aged cheddar, Vermont goat cheese, domestic sweet cream butter, and West Coast wines.
  4. Focus on seasonal fruit, grain, and spice desserts: High tariffs have sent the price of specialty chocolates soaring, but we’re not giving up dessert! Lean into fruit-forward desserts like a pear galette or an apple crumble. You could also try an egg-rich chess pie, or any number of bread-based desserts. Other great options include sweet potato pie or pecan pie using pantry grains and domestic sweeteners.

“Kicking the Can” at Thanksgiving

A stress-free Thanksgiving dinner often relies on convenience canned products like jellied cranberry sauce, condensed cream-of-mushroom soup bases, and pumpkin puree. But in 2026, paying a premium for steel packaging is the quickest way to run up your total at the register.

Aside from straight facts about how tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs are impacting our grocery carts this holiday season, let’s talk about yield, which applies to pre (and post–let’s hope!) tariff times, too. For example, if you want to give 8 people an honest half-cup portion of carrots, you cannot rely on the canned carrot’s label touting that 3.5 servings are ready to be enjoyed inside. You’d need at least 4 cans, yielding roughly 5 to 6 cups total; at $0.96 per can, that’s $3.84, and over a third of what you bought was salt water and packaging.

On the flip side, a 4 lb. bag of raw carrots yields a solid 14 to 16 generous side servings. Even after peeling and trimming the tips (about a 10% prep loss), you are left with roughly 57 to 58 oz of pure edible veg. At $4.77 for the 4 lb bag, you feed twice as many guests for roughly 40% less out of pocket than buying the 6 to 7 cans needed to match that yield, paying around 8.3 cents per edible ounce compared to over 13 cents for canned solids, while skipping the canned brine (and tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs) completely!

Here is how to ditch the cans and build a fresher table for less money with just a little bit of extra elbow grease:

Make a 15-Minute Fresh Cranberry Sauce with 3 Ingredients

A can of jellied cranberry sauce seems cheap until you compare its price per ounce to a simple bag of fresh, American-grown cranberries. Simmering whole cranberries on the stove with a little sugar, a splash of water, and fresh orange zest takes less than twenty minutes. It thickens naturally as it cools, costs less, and delivers a vibrant flavor that makes the canned jelly completely obsolete. Our easy three-ingredient cranberry sauce recipe (shown above) has become my go-to.

Only need a small batch of cranberry sauce? Put the rest of those fresh cranberries to good use and make my sugared cranberries. They’re a delicious, festive, budget-friendly treat that doubles as a gorgeous garnish for your turkey platter and holiday desserts.

Swap Out the Green Bean Casserole

Traditional green bean casserole often relies on canned french-cut green beans and canned cream soups…a double hit of packaging markups! Instead, grab two pounds of fresh green beans from the produce section or a couple bags of frozen from the freezer section. Sauté them in a skillet with a drizzle of olive oil or domestic butter, garlic, and a pinch of other ingredients you probably already have on hand. It’s crisp, colorful, and feels like something you’d order at a nice restaurant.

Elevate the Bread Basket

Instead of spending $8 to $12 on a small wedge of imported cheese to snack on before dinner, make the bread basket your showstopper instead. Bake a quick batch of warm homemade dinner rolls or a skillet of golden cornbread, and serve it alongside whipped domestic sweet cream butter drizzled with local honey and sea salt. They cost pennies per serving, actually fill people up, and feel deeply comforting. Short on time? Don’t feel bad about grabbing a bag of ready-made dinner rolls at the grocery store like we did this time. At only $1.52 for 12 dinner rolls, I have no regrets…just extra time to spend with my family!

Prep Ahead to Keep Your Sanity

Prepped carrots, celery, and potatoes in deli containers with cold water.

Admittedly, scratch-cooking fresh foods takes more prep than opening a row of cans with a can opener, but that doesn’t mean you need to be glued to the stove all day. Make your cranberry sauce two days in advance. Peel and chop your carrots, celery, and potatoes the day before and keep them refrigerated in water. Then on the holiday morning, you’re just assembling, roasting, and mashing, not stressing!

Christmas & Holiday Entertaining: Luxury Without the Luxury Tax

Christmas dinner and New Year’s Eve often lean toward luxury imports like Champagne, ornate cheese boards, and expensive cuts of beef and roasts. You can still create an indulgent spread without absorbing tariffs:

1. Build an American Charcuterie Board

Spot the savings! The imported Swiss Gruyère on the right costs $7.97 for 6 oz., while the Wisconsin-made Gruyère right next to it is just $4.87 for 7 oz. That’s $3.10 less for an EXTRA ounce!

The days of carefree grazing boards might be behind us (for now…fingers crossed!), but you don’t need imported French cheeses or Italian prosciutto to put together an impressive board. The U.S. produces excellent artisanal cheeses! While we navigate this timeline with import taxes kicking us while we’re down, try a sharp, aged Wisconsin white cheddar, a log of creamy Vermont goat cheese, or an Oregon blue cheese instead. Pair your American-made cheeses with domestic cured salami, crisp sliced apples, local honey, and candied Georgia pecans. It looks gorgeous, takes no time at all, supports local dairies, and dodges hefty markups.

2. Pour West Coast Bubbles or Switch to a Festive Mocktail

If your holiday tradition calls for a celebratory toast, you might need to leave the French Champagne and Italian Prosecco behind. Luckily, California, Oregon, and Washington state produce exceptional sparkling wines at a fraction of the cost of imported bottles, giving you festive bubbles without paying international shipping and tariff penalties! If alcohol isn’t in your budget, period, swap it out and save yourself a bunch of money by making a festive mocktail instead!

3. The Real Math: Why We Retired Our Holiday Pot Roast

At Budget Bytes, we don’t typically build holiday recipes around $70 rib roasts, but a cozy beef chuck roast used to be one of our favorite reasonably-priced celebratory centerpieces. Historically, our absolute max to keep a recipe accessible on the site is staying under $3.50 per serving.

  • 2026 Beef Pot Roast (8 Servings): That exact same 2.61 lb cut of chuck roast at the same store jumped to $25.02—a 44% increase on the beef alone. Factoring in slight cost increases on the rest of the ingredients, the meal totaled $33.95, or $4.24 per serving.

A 44% jump pushes an everyday pot roast right out of budget territory. Save our pot roast recipe for later, but we’re pivoting for the holidays this year.

Why This Strategy Works

This menu works because it centers on staple proteins and produce, featuring domestically raised turkey alongside hearty crops like potatoes, carrots, onions, garlic, and cranberries. The U.S. produces massive quantities of these staples locally, so their prices aren’t tied to shipping containers, port disputes, or trade friction. The rising cost of gas and diesel does affect domestic products (and our plans to travel for the holidays!), but we’re doing the best we can.

By buying raw or frozen, domestically grown produce and relying on basic American dairy and pantry seasonings, you avoid the packaging tariffs hitting canned goods and the trade surcharges driving up imported meats and luxury items. You don’t have to sacrifice flavor, tradition, or hospitality to survive this season’s grocery landscape; you just have to shop with a plan.

To our friends outside the U.S.A.: While this breakdown is tailored to U.S. supply chains, the exact same principle applies where you are. Leaning into your domestically grown root crops, greenhouse produce, and domestic staples is your best defense against punishing import markups and currency exchange rates.
