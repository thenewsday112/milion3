If you’re looking for a filling, comforting dinner that hits all the right marks without leaving you with a sink full of dishes, this Creamy Chicken and Tortellini Skillet is about to become your new weeknight obsession! Tender, seasoned chicken, plump cheese tortellini, sweet onions, and aromatic garlic come together in a velvety Parmesan cream sauce. Packed with lean protein and a healthy dose of spinach, it’s a complete, satisfying meal that goes from skillet to table in under 30 minutes. My husband even asked me to make an extra batch so he could bring some to his buddies at work!

From the Freezer to Creamy Comfort Food

The real beauty of this creamy chicken tortellini is its one-skillet convenience and how it delivers maximum flavor with hardly any effort (minimal cleanup? Count me in!). I season the chicken before coating it in a little flour, which helps thicken the broth and pan juices into a creamy sauce without making a separate roux. Even the frozen tortellini goes straight into the skillet without precooking. I add the cream when the broth is a little cooler to keep the sauce smooth, and I leave it slightly loose because the tortellini keeps soaking it up as it sits (which makes it even better for leftovers!).

Frozen spinach is another budget-friendly shortcut I love here. It goes straight from the freezer into the skillet with no chopping, making this a restaurant-worthy dinner without the expensive ingredients or extra prep! Prefer a meat-free alternative? Just leave the chicken out of this dish and swap the chicken broth, or try our spinach tortellini skillet next.

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Butter and olive oil are better together here! I use both because the olive oil helps coat the skillet for sautéing, while the butter adds a rich flavor that carries through into the creamy sauce. Keep the heat at medium, though, as butter can still burn when mixed with oil.
  2. Don’t cut the chicken too small! Aim for evenly sized, generous bite-sized pieces (check out my step-by-step photos below for reference). Chicken shrinks as it cooks, and tiny pieces can dry out quickly. Giving the chicken some room to make contact with the hot skillet also helps develop a little golden color and extra flavor. Just make sure the chicken reaches 165°F internally before serving.
  3. Keep the tortellini FROZEN until you’re ready to use it. No thawing or boiling beforehand! This recipe is designed for tortellini straight from the freezer, so thawing it first can throw off the cooking time and leave you with mushy pasta. Different brands may cook a little faster or slower, so check that the tortellini is tender all the way through before serving.
  4. A gentle simmer is your friend. Once the heavy cream and Parmesan go in, I keep the heat at medium-low. You only want small and gentle bubbles. Too much heat can reduce the sauce too quickly or cause the cheese to turn grainy instead of melting smoothly. Stir everything gently, too, so those little cheese-filled tortellini stay intact!
  5. Customize your skillet! I think adding peas or substituting them instead of spinach would be delicious. Asparagus or broccoli florets would also be delicious choices for substitutes or additions.

how to make Creamy Chicken and Tortellini Skillet step-by-step photos

Gather and prep your fresh ingredients. Keep the frozen tortellini in the freezer until you’re ready to add it.

Prep the chicken: Dice 1 lb. chicken breast into even, bite-sized pieces, keeping them slightly larger than you might think since they’ll shrink as they cook.

Prep the aromatics: Now dice 1 small yellow onion and mince 3 cloves of garlic.

Season and coat the chicken: Add ½ tsp salt, ½ tsp black pepper, ½ tsp garlic powder, and ½ tsp Italian seasoning to the diced chicken. Toss until every piece is evenly seasoned.

Then sprinkle over ¼ cup all-purpose flour.

Toss again until the chicken is coated on all sides with the flour.

Sauté the onion: Heat 1 Tbsp olive oil and 2 Tbsp salted butter in a large sauté pan over medium heat.

Once the butter has melted and begins to gently bubble, add the diced onion. Cook for about 4 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onion looks glossy, softened, and slightly translucent.

Sear the chicken: Push the onions to the edges of the pan, then add the flour-coated chicken, spreading the pieces out so they contact the hot surface. Cook for about 5 minutes, until the undersides are lightly golden, then flip and cook for another 5 minutes.

Avoid moving the chicken around too much while it sears. Let it sit against the hot pan to develop color and flavor.

Add the broth and garlic: Pour 2 cups chicken broth into the skillet along with the 3 cloves of minced garlic, stirring to combine everything with the chicken and onions. Gently scrape the bottom of the pan to release any flavorful browned bits. Reduce the heat to medium-low and let everything simmer gently for about 4 minutes. The broth should start to look slightly thickened as the flour coating incorporates into the liquid.

Add the frozen tortellini: Pour the entire 19 oz. package of frozen cheese tortellini directly into the simmering chicken and broth mixture. There’s no need to thaw or precook it. Gently stir to distribute the tortellini throughout the skillet without breaking the delicate pasta.

Make it creamy: Pour in ¾ cup room-temperature heavy cream and stir gently to combine. Adding the cream after the frozen tortellini helps because the pasta has slightly cooled the broth, reducing the chance of the sauce curdling. Keep the heat at medium-low so the sauce simmers gently rather than boiling vigorously.

Add the cheese and spinach: Stir in ½ cup shredded Parmesan cheese and 1 cup frozen spinach. Continue simmering gently over medium-low heat for about 2 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the cheese has melted into the sauce and the spinach is heated through. The sauce should look smooth and creamy, with the spinach evenly distributed. Check that the tortellini is tender all the way through, simmering for longer if needed. Make sure the chicken has reached an internal temperature of 165°F.

Check the sauce and serve: The sauce should remain slightly loose but still lightly coat the back of a spoon. Don’t worry if it looks a little thinner than a typical Alfredo sauce! The tortellini will continue absorbing the liquid as it sits, thickening the sauce naturally.

Taste and adjust the salt and pepper to your liking. Serve and enjoy!

Storage & Reheating

Let any leftovers cool, then transfer them to an airtight container and refrigerate within two hours of cooking. They’ll keep for up to 3-4 days. The tortellini will soak up the creamy sauce as it sits (which is why I especially like the sauce to be looser when I know I’ll have leftovers!), so expect it to thicken in the fridge.

Reheating

Warm leftovers in a skillet over medium-low heat. You may need to add a splash of chicken broth or milk to loosen the sauce if the pasta has absorbed a lot. Stir gently until heated through to 165°F. You can also microwave individual portions in short intervals, stirring between each one for even heating.

Freezing

I wouldn’t recommend freezing this one. The cheese-filled tortellini can lose its tender texture, and the cream sauce may separate once thawed. You’ll get much better results keeping any leftovers in the fridge and enjoying them over the next few days.

This easy Ham and Bean Soup is loaded with tender canned white beans, colorful veggies, and savory chunks of browned ham, all in a thick, creamy broth (made without any actual cream or dairy). It’s a great way to use up leftover holiday ham, but I certainly don’t wait for a holiday to make it! I use just 8 basic ingredients plus a few seasonings to make this hearty, easy, budget-friendly soup, and it’s ready in less than an hour!

How I Build Big Flavor in Less Time

Traditional ham and bean soups often start with dried beans and a ham bone that simmer together for hours. And there’s definitely a time and place for that! But I wanted an easy version that didn’t require soaking beans or spending all afternoon waiting for dinner. So I use canned cannellini beans and fully cooked ham to get a head start. They’re already cooked, which means I can spend my time building flavor instead.

One thing I do differently is brown the ham before adding anything else. This gives the edges a little color and leaves behind tasty browned bits on the bottom of the pot (called fond). When I add the onion, carrots, celery, and garlic, their natural moisture loosens those bits and brings that extra flavor into the soup. Sautéing the vegetables also softens them and brings out a little natural sweetness. For the broth, I borrowed a trick from my white bean soup and purée one can of beans with its liquid. This thickens the soup and adds a lovely creaminess without flour, cornstarch, or heavy cream. I leave the other two cans whole for a chunky texture, and after the initial flavor-building stages, everything comes together with just 15 minutes of simmering!

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Give the ham a chance to brown. I cook the diced ham in oil over medium heat for 3-5 minutes, until the edges are lightly browned and golden-brown bits form on the bottom of the pot. This builds the salty ham flavor you’d normally get from simmering a ham bone for hours, just in much less time! If those bits start turning very dark, lower the heat so they don’t burn.
  2. Blend one can of beans, liquid and all! Purée ONE can of cannellini beans with its canning liquid, then drain the other two. The smooth beans thicken the broth, and the whole beans give the soup its chunky texture. No blender? You can thoroughly mash that first can with a potato masher or fork instead. It won’t be as smooth, but this is a chunky soup anyway!
  3. Let the soup boil and thicken. The 15-minute boil allows some of the broth to evaporate while the puréed beans thicken the soup. I give the pot a good stir every few minutes, making sure to scrape along the bottom, to keep the beans from sticking. If it gets thicker than you’d like, stir in a little more broth or water until you reach your preferred consistency.
  4. Taste before adding salt. Cooked ham is pretty salty, especially when it’s cured, and chicken broth adds even more seasoning. That’s why I wait until the ham goes back into the pot before tasting and adjusting. I didn’t need any extra salt in my batch, but I finished each bowl with a little extra black pepper.

What Kind of Ham Can I Use?

For this quick ham and bean soup, I recommend fully cooked ham (or leftover cooked ham from another meal) rather than a ham hock. We’re using canned beans and other ingredients that cook quickly, so there’s no need to spend hours simmering a ham bone to flavor the broth! Thicker pieces work best because they hold their shape and develop nicely browned edges when sautéed.

Boneless vs. Bone-In Ham Steaks

Both of these ham steaks are fully cooked and work well in this recipe:

  • Boneless ham steak: My easiest choice! There’s no bone to work around, and the thick slices are easy to cut into bite-sized pieces. You can buy ham steaks in smaller packs (shown above) or use a package of fully cooked, boneless, sliced ham (as I did in the recipe below).
  • Bone-in ham steak: This works just as well once you’ve removed the bone and any tough rind. Just remember that you need 1 lb. of usable ham, so you’ll need to buy a little extra to account for the weight of the bone.

What About Other Types of Ham?

I saw a LOT of ham options at the store when shopping for this recipe, and you probably will too, especially around the holidays. The fully cooked, boneless spiral-cut ham and smoked shank portion ham shown above can both work, BUT I’d save those bigger hams for a holiday centerpiece (like our baked ham) and use the leftovers for this soup.

I wouldn’t splurge on a big ham for this recipe unless you plan to use it for several meals. Ham slices are cheaper, easier, and all you need for this quickie soup.

Ham hocks are a different story! They’re better suited to traditional bean soups that simmer for hours. The long simmer gives the connective tissue time to soften and the smoky flavor time to develop. The pork hocks pictured above are only partially cooked, so they aren’t suitable for this shortcut recipe as written. If you’d prefer to make soup using a ham hock and dried beans, check out our navy bean soup recipe instead. It uses a smoked ham hock and takes over two hours of cooking, plus soaking time for the beans.

Ham and Bean Soup

This incredibly easy and deliciously chunky Ham and Bean Soup features a medley of colorful vegetables, browned ham, and plenty of hearty white beans. Instructions

  • Gather and prepare all ingredients. Dice the onion, peel and slice the carrots, slice the celery, and mince the garlic.
  • Dice the ham into bite-sized chunks. Add the ham and cooking oil to a large soup pot. Sauté the ham for 3-5 minutes over medium heat, or until it achieves a decent amount of browning. Remove the browned ham to a clean bowl.
  • Add the onion, carrots, celery, and garlic to the pot in place of the ham. Sauté the vegetables for about 5 minutes over medium heat, or until the onions have softened. Allow the moisture released from the vegetables to help dissolve the browned bits of ham from the bottom of the soup pot as you stir.
  • While the vegetables are sautéing, add one of the three cans of beans to a blender, with the liquid from the can, and purée until smooth. Drain the remaining two cans of beans.
  • Add all three cans of beans (one puréed and two drained) to the soup pot with the vegetables. Also add ¼ tsp dried thyme, ¼ tsp pepper, and stir to combine.
  • Next, stir in 2 cups chicken broth. Then turn the heat up to medium-high and allow the soup to come to a boil.
  • Once it reaches a boil, turn the heat down to medium and allow the soup to continue to boil for 15 minutes, stirring occasionally. As the soup boils, it will reduce and thicken. If the soup becomes too thick, add more chicken broth or water to achieve your desired soup consistency.
  • After the soup has boiled for 15 minutes and has thickened, stir the cooked ham back into the soup. Give the soup a taste and adjust the salt or pepper to your liking. I did not add any additional salt, but I did top each bowl with a little fresh pepper. Serve hot!

How to Make Ham and Bean Soup Step-by-Step Photos

Gather all of your ingredients: This is the type of ham I used (honey cured boneless ham). You can use any cooked ham, but a thicker slice works better than thin sandwich slices. You can also use cooked holiday ham. You’ll need 1 lb. of cooked ham.

Prep the veggies: Dice 1 yellow onion, peel and slice 3 carrots, slice 3 celery ribs, and mince 2 cloves of garlic. I recommend keeping the vegetables fairly uniform in size so they soften evenly during the short cooking time.

Brown the ham: Start by dicing 1 lb. of fully cooked ham into bite-sized pieces.

Add the ham and 1 Tbsp cooking oil to a large soup pot or Dutch oven. Sauté over medium heat for 3-5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the edges turn golden brown. You should also see some browned bits developing on the bottom of the pot. Keep an eye on the heat so they don’t burn. Remove the ham to a clean bowl and leave those browned bits behind.

Sauté the vegetables: Add the prepared onion, carrots, celery, and garlic to the same pot. Sauté over medium heat for about 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onions look translucent and the vegetables begin to soften. They’ll release some moisture as they cook, so use your spoon to gently scrape up the browned bits left by the ham.

Purée one can of beans: As the vegetables cook, add one 15.5 oz. can of cannellini beans to a blender, including all the liquid from the can. Blend until smooth and creamy. This mixture will help thicken the soup without adding cream!

Drain the other two cans: Now drain the remaining two cans of beans, but don’t purée them. We want those beans to stay whole for texture.

This is what the veggies should look like after sautéing for about 5 minutes. The bottom of the pot should also look noticeably cleaner as those bits dissolve into the vegetables.

Add the beans and seasonings: Add the puréed beans and both cans of drained beans to the pot with the softened vegetables. Sprinkle in ¼ tsp dried thyme and ¼ tsp freshly cracked black pepper. Stir gently until the creamy bean mixture coats the vegetables and whole beans. Try not to mash the whole beans as you stir.

Pour in the broth: Add 2 cups chicken broth and stir until everything is combined. The soup will look fairly watery at this point, but that’s normal! The puréed beans will give the broth more body as the liquid reduces during cooking.

Increase the heat to medium-high and bring the soup to a boil. Reduce the heat to medium and let it continue boiling for 15 minutes, stirring every few minutes to prevent the beans from sticking to the bottom of the pot. The broth should gradually reduce and become thicker and creamier. The vegetables should also be tender when pierced with a fork. If the soup gets too thick, add a splash of chicken broth or water until it reaches your preferred consistency.

Add the ham and serve: Once the soup has thickened, stir the browned ham back into the pot to warm through.

Give everything a taste before adding any salt, as the ham and chicken broth already contribute quite a bit! I didn’t need any extra salt in my batch, but I did add a little freshly cracked black pepper to each bowl. Serve hot and enjoy!

Storage & Reheating

This ham and bean soup makes great leftovers. Let it cool quickly, then transfer it to airtight containers and refrigerate within 2 hours. It’ll keep in the refrigerator for 3-4 days. The soup will naturally thicken as it sits because the beans will keep absorbing moisture.

Reheating

I like to reheat this soup on the stovetop over medium heat, stirring occasionally until it comes to a boil and is heated all the way through (165°F). Add a splash of chicken broth or water as it warms to loosen the consistency. You can also reheat individual servings in the microwave, stirring partway through so they heat evenly.

Freezing

This soup freezes really well, too! Once cooled, divide it into individual portions in freezer-safe containers or bags. I prefer to divide my soup into single servings before freezing, so they can be reheated as needed and in the amount needed. Quart-sized freezer bags are great for freezing soup, as are the small blue-top Ziploc food storage containers. Freeze for up to 3 months for the best quality.

Epic Brown Butter Rice Krispie Treats

Rice Krispie Treats like no other, these are nutty, milky, not too sweet and a little salty. Studded with crunchy pumpkin seeds, they are highly addictive! Less sweet and higher protein than regular ones! Course Dessert Cuisine American Prep Time 15 minutes minutes Cook Time 10 minutes minutes Total Time 20 minutes minutes Servings 32 pieces

Ingredients

  • 6 cups Rice Krispie cereal see note 1
  • ½ cup toasted pumpkin seeds or sub other kinds of nuts or seeds
  • ¼ cup hemp hearts optional, feel free to sub other small seeds like chia seeds or sesame seeds, see note 2
  • 1 scoop unflavoured whey protein powder optional, see note 3
  • ¾ teaspoon table salt or another fine grain salt
  • ½ cup unsalted butter
  • 9.5 oz marshmallows mini ones dissolve more quickly but regular size ones are fine
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract

Instructions

  • Prepare a 9x13 pan lined with parchment paper, or whatever containers you want.
  • Combine the rice krispie cereal, pumpkin seeds, hemp hearts, protein powder, salt and mix well. 6 cups Rice Krispie cereal, ½ cup toasted pumpkin seeds, ¼ cup hemp hearts, 1 scoop unflavoured whey protein powder, ¾ teaspoon table salt
  • In a pot or a large wok, ideally nonstick, add the butter and melt over medium heat. Once melted and the bubbling starts to subside, stir frequently until the milk solids at the bottom of the pan is dark brown. Do not walk away, it burns fast! ½ cup unsalted butter
    • I highly recommend you watch the video tutorial for this process if you've never browned butter before.
  • Once the butter has browned, immediately add the marshmallows and quickly stir to mix and then turn the heat down to LOW. Add the vanilla and keep stirring until the marshmallows are mostly melted but still a little bit lumpy. 9.5 oz marshmallows, 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • Remove the pan from the heat, add the cereal mixture and quickly toss with two spatula until the rice krispies are evenly coated in the marshmallow. (If using an electric stove, move the pan off the element during mixing).
    • If for some reason the mixture has cooled and firmed up before mixing is complete, you can put it back on LOW heat to soften it and make the mixing easier.
  • Transfer into the prepared pan and smooth out the rice krispies without compressing it in order to keep it light and airy. It will not fill a 9x13 pan completely, so you'll have space around the edges to use your hand/spatula to press the edges to straighten them (press through the parchment, don't touch the treats with your bare hands!) Has Substitution Culture Gone Too Far?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

This excerpt was originally published in Pre Shift, our newsletter for the hospitality industry. Subscribe for more first-person accounts, advice, and interviews.

When Chanita Chayaluk decided to open her restaurant and wine bar Taan, she hoped “to showcase Thai food in Chicago in a different light,” she says. “The first [wave of] immigration of Thai people in Chicago was in the 1970s, and a lot of [Thai] food here still feels like it’s from that time.” In September, she opened with a menu including miang, a Northern Thai dish made with fermented tea leaves, and a whole, deep-fried fish in a fish-sauce caramel. 

Another quirk of operating in Chicago? “We’re still pretty ‘Midwest nice,’” says Chayaluk. “We try to overaccommodate and appease the masses.” In the early days of service, guests made frequent requests for substitutions or accommodations due to preferences and dietary restrictions. “It would slow down service and speed, and that was not just affecting us, but the guest experience in many ways,” she says. With a small kitchen and staff, it was hard to keep up. “It just felt like we were Chipotle.” When a guest asked for green curry mussels “but without the mussels,” it was a turning point. 

Chayaluk implemented a limited substitution policy at Taan, stating on Instagram that ingredients like fish sauce and shrimp paste are “the fabric that defines our cuisine,” and therefore, could not be removed from any dishes that include it. 

A decade or so ago, substitution culture was generally frowned upon and derided. Recently, though, some restaurants report it bubbling back up—and in full force. There’s a swirl of factors that got us here: Lunch slop bowl spots primed customers to expect a detailed level of customization with their orders. Unreasonable Hospitality pushed an intensified “customer is always right” mentality. And influencers entering the food-review space have made criticism of anything—including a restaurant choosing not to honor certain dietary preferences—ripe for an immediate, viral takedown. Couple all of that with the fact that it is hard to keep a restaurant afloat these days, and you’ve got several operators giving in, whether to specific requests, or to altering their menus altogether (see also: the great de-spicification of menus.)

At the same time, restaurants like Taan and New York’s Ha’s Snack Bar—which proudly announces in its Instagram bio that everything contains fish sauce—are standing their ground.

For Lom Wong in Phoenix, Arizona, substitution and modification requests have ramped up after receiving national attention. Chef Yotaka “Sunny” Martin was recognized by the James Beard Awards last year, and the restaurant was named a Michelin Bib Gourmand in August. “[After the award,] we had more guests than we were used to picking things apart. They were asking us to modify dishes or not understanding our service style and why we weren’t coursing things in a European fashion,” says chef and co-owner Alex Martin. “[There is a] European distinction of what an award-winning restaurant looks like, so people equated that recognition with a white-tablecloth restaurant. We were just like, ‘Well, that’s not who we’ve ever been, and it’s never what our restaurant will ever be.’”

Like Taan, Lom Wong took to social media to set expectations, including that the restaurant would not “make substitutions simply to make a dish more familiar,” and that certain dishes might differ from Western expectations of them; beef might have gristle, fishes will be whole, and both choices are fully intentional. “Sunny is our guiding light of cultural context,” says Martin. “[She advises, considering if] changing something about a dish compromises the integrity of the dish.” For example, the mango salad can be ordered with the shrimp on the side, but the restaurant won’t remove beef or shrimp paste from dishes that necessitate them. 

Chayaluk takes a similar approach at Taan; there is always a vegan, gluten-free option (currently a fried rice) that she describes as a “catchall” for those who can’t have other items on the menu. “We make sure that that dish is also really, really good and substantial; it’s not just an appetizer,” she says. “It’s not an afterthought.” 

Staff at both restaurants are encouraged to uphold the boundaries, which both Chayaluk and Martin say are as much in place for protecting their staff’s peace as they are for ensuring quality control for guests. While there has been some backlash, the expectation-setting at the start of the meal (and before, thanks to the social media posts) has for the most part been taken well. “The way we explain [our policy] to guests is kindly saying we respect your preferences, but they’re your preferences,” says Martin, “and steering them toward dishes on the menu that might already align with those.”

The ‘Tavern-Style’ Pizza Takeover

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
The thin-crust pizzas popular throughout the Midwest have taken off as the “tavern-style” pizza trend | Brad Bobrow/La Merna

In the suburbs of Chicago where they both grew up, it was just pizza, according to Brittany and Brennan Grosvenor, the couple behind Proof in Portland, Oregon. Ordering came down to the question of “thin-crust, deep-dish, or double-dough,” Brittany recalls of her family’s Friday night tradition. The Grosvernors sell the former at Proof, though the language of “thin crust” has given way to the more nationally familiar phrasing of “tavern-style,” the pizza trend that has swept from coast to coast in recent years. 

Outsiders might associate Chicago with its famous, tourist-beckoning deep-dish pizza, but Brittany says that’s just not reflective of reality, citing deep dish as a much less frequent treat than the Midwestern, thin-crust style that’s gaining popularity. “The pizza you get every week, or if you go to a kid’s birthday party, it’s always tavern-style,” she says.

“Tavern-style” pizza — which is prevalent  across the Midwest, with Chicago, Milwaukee, Minnesota, and St. Louis touting similar pies — is known for its thin, cracker-like crust, cut into a lattice instead of triangular slices. Depending on the region and the pizza shop where one’s nostalgia lies, the sauce might lean sweet or spicy. Lightness and crispness, though, is the style’s unique sell. Writing for Esquire in 2023, Omar Mamoon called the variant, which was then still on the rise, “a foil” to famously thick Detroit-style pizza, which had its big moment in the late 2010s. 

The beauty of the tavern-style pie is its shareability. A pizza yields more slices when cut into a grid than into triangles, explains Bradley Shorten, who’s behind both Kim’s Uncle Pizza in his hometown Chicago suburb of Westmont, Illinois, and the recently opened La Merna in Washington, D.C. Pizza cut into little squares makes for great drinking food, especially since it fits on a cocktail napkin. What makes a good pie, according to Shorten, is that every square has a little of every ingredient; the toppings go to the edge. And while the four, small corner slices might not be the “best” ones, Shorten says, they’re the ones “everybody goes for,” especially if they’re from Chicago.

Over the past few years, as “taverns” have become growing fixations for a cadre of restaurateurs, the “party cut” pizza and tavern-style pizza spots have also become ubiquitous, with openings like Keepers in New York city; One Trick Pony in Forth Worth, Texas; Tillie’s Tavern Pizza in Wheat Ridge, Colorado; and Zarella and Dimmi Dimmi Corner Italian in Chicago, where the style has always been abundant. For some Chicagoans, these openings across the country are an opportunity to correct the record about their city’s pizza culture, and for other Midwesterners to claim their stake in the region’s pizza, too.

When Mike Fadem opened the second location of his popular NYC pizzeria Ops — known for its wood-fired Neapolitan-esque pies with leopard-spotted crusts and pliant dough — he wanted to offer something new. Fadem is from St. Louis, where the city’s namesake pizza is super-thin. Despite his love for it, “it’s maybe not the best version of tavern pizza,” Fadem admits, noting the iteration’s many critics, some of whom take issue with its use of the gooey processed cheese called provel. The trajectory of tavern-style pizza has been like a game of telephone, he explains: St. Louis pizza is derived from thin-crust Chicago pizza, which itself came from Neapolitan pizza. Divergence has accompanied each jump.

One of Fadem’s employees at Ops, meanwhile, is from New Jersey, which claims its own thin-crust, tavern- or bar-style pizza tradition. (The bar pies of the Boston area also have their own devoted following.) Accordingly, “I had this interest in ‘can we make an Ops version of this?’” Fadem says. With Ops known for sourdough, Fadem also wanted to highlight the rich flavor of whole grains. And he wanted it to be really crispy, a quality he says draws in New Yorkers. Fadem’s take is on the menu as the regionally agnostic “tavernetta.” Though St. Louis and New Jersey served as initial inspiration, the best pizza from his research was actually in Milwaukee. The Tavernetta hasn’t displaced the other pies at the restaurant; it’s ordered in addition to them, with most groups getting at least one.

In June, Los Angeles cocktail spot Bar Next Door celebrated its third anniversary by adding food to its menu. Previously, the bar had allowed customers to bring in pies from Prince Street Pizza next door since Prince Street co-owner Lawrence Longo is a partner in it. “But, you know, after having a slice, sometimes you get a little full and you’re like, I want to go to bed,” says owner and bar director Brynn Smith. “That’s the whole goal for us: for people to stay a little longer, drink more. We thought the tavern-style pizza would be perfect.” The fact that the pizzas — they’re technically listed on the menu as “bar pies,” to better go with the establishment’s name — are made exclusively for the bar by Prince Street just adds another reason for people to go out, Smith says.

The idea of the tavern as a social and convivial space is inseparable from the tavern-style pizza. Shorten’s goal with La Merna — which he runs with his wife Meg Schmitt, who grew up in St. Louis — was the feel of a Chicago dive bar or a Wisconsin supper club: a place with wood-paneled walls and checkered floors, where people could slide into a red leather booth for pitchers of beer and pizza and hang out for a while. As my colleague Francky Knapp wrote last year, the rising appeal of the “tavern” vibe across restaurants nationwide is rooted in the sense of providing somewhere homey and lived-in. At taverns, diners are beckoned to sprawl out and stay awhile; to free themselves of pretense and to tap into something welcoming in its familiarity. This, for so many people, is exemplified by the archetype of hometown pizza shop, which is forever tethered in our cultural imaginations to the trappings of a previous, simpler era.

It’s for this reason that, in Oregon, the Grosvenors have started serving their take on Bosco sticks, the stuffed breadsticks of Midwestern cafeterias, and Italian beef with giardiniera. While they could find certain Midwestern hits around Portland, it sometimes frustrated them that they couldn’t find everything in one place. According to Brennan Grosvenor, “We’re just trying to embody the feeling: If you went into a tavern in Chicago, what could you order there?”

Knowing how to roast vegetables well is a power move. It’s a single technique that can be deployed across an impressive range of vegetables turning them into platters of food that is simple, unfussy, and just plain good.

How To Roast Vegetables – The Basics

You can use this basic technique for a wide range of vegetables – potatoes, beets, broccoli, cauliflower, string beans, cabbage, artichoke hearts, asparagus, etc. Seriously, don’t overthink it. Once you get your roasting technique nailed down, you can fancify things with added spices, under-dressings, and the like. But to start, here’s what to know:

  • High heat is your friend. Somewhere in the range of 400°F – it will give you tender interiors and nice, golden exteriors. Go too hot and you’ll burn the edges before the insides have time to cook. Some recipes might go a bit higher, others a bit lower, but if I’m just doing a basic vegetable roast, I typically set the dial right around 400°F.
  • Don’t crowd the pan. Your vegetables are going to put off a good amount of moisture as they roast, and they like some space to crisp instead of steam.
  • Cut vegetables to a similar size. This way they will cook in the same amount of time.
  • Group vegetables by cooking time. Like with like  on the pan. You want to arrange your ingredients in zones based on estimated cooking time. For example, put asparagus which doesn’t take long to roast, on a small pan (or at the edge of a larger pan). Place ingredients that take longer to cook on another pan or in another zone, for example-potatoes or winter squash, That way you can remove them as they finish cooking.
  • Don’t skimp on the oil. You don’t have to go wild, but make sure everything gets a thin, even coat of olive oil before roasting.

Use this play-by-play: Heat oven to about 400°F. Toss each type of vegetable in a large bowl with a splash of olive oil and a good pinch of salt I often add a couple scoops of nutritional yeast in at this point but that’s entirely optional. Roast, tossing a couple times after things start browning, until vegetables are tender and have a good amount of golden color.

Just Some Things I Tried and Have Opinions About

I don’t know that anyone cares about this, but I spend too much time thinking about these things.

  • This recipe works best with Greek yogurt rather than regular yogurt! It gets more evenly brown and has a nice rise like you see in the pictures, whereas regular yogurt is wetter and makes the batter more dense. (It does still work, if you’re in a pinch.)
  • You want rolled old fashioned oats for this one – don’t use the Costco sprouted oats, and do not use quick oats. They both absorb liquid in a different way. I just buy the Quaker old fashioned rolled oats!
  • I did try this with grating the apples, but I prefer just throwing them into the blender at the end of blending. 1) It’s easier, and 2) I felt like the muffins got a better rise with the apples being more incorporated.
  • Our previous muffin recipes similar to this have used maple syrup, but it gets really expensive to use that much real maple syrup, so I switched to brown sugar for this one!
  • The streusel is optional – they’re still pretty and delicious without!
  • The batter will bake differently the longer it sits! I find he sweet spot is about 10 minutes. You may find that your second batch gets a bit more puffy than your first.

Apple Streusel Meal Prep Muffins

Description

These apple streusel meal prep muffins are super wholesome, loaded with streusel, and ideal for prepping ahead! My kids love them!

Ingredients

Units USM

For the Muffins:

  • 1 cup whole milk Greek yogurt
  • 3 eggs
  • 3 1/2 cups old-fashioned oats (I don’t recommend the Costco sprouted oats or quick oats)
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar, lightly packed
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons baking soda
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 1/2 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 2 cups roughly chopped Honeycrisp apples (I did not peel mine)

For the Streusel:

  • 4 tablespoons butter, melted
  • 1/3 cup brown sugar
  • 1/2 cup + 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Prep: Preheat the oven to 350 degrees. Spray a muffin tin with avocado oil.
  2. Blend: Add ingredients to the blender in order, but leave out the apples for now. Blend in short bursts until it starts to move (you may need to help it along initially). Once it gets going, blend for 15-20 seconds, until mostly smooth. There should still be some flecks of texture, similar to a bran muffin batter.
  3. Apples: Add the apple chunks; blend again for 15-20 seconds until they are worked into the batter in small flecks.
  4. Streusel: Mix the streusel ingredients in a small bowl until it resembles small pebbles – I start with a fork, and then mix with my hands. (The batter can rest for 10 minutes during this time.)
  5. Assemble: Pour batter in the muffin tin – filling each cup 2/3 of the way full, but not all the way to the top. Add a spoonful of streusel on each one. (I typically get 16-17 muffins from this recipe, so I bake in two batches.)
  6. Bake: Bake for 16-18 minutes, until the tops spring back when gently pressed. Cool for 10-15 minutes so they are easier to remove from the pan. I love eating these warm with a shmear of butter!
  7. Storing: I put them in a ziplock bag and keep them in the freezer or fridge. To reheat, I pop them in the microwave for 30 seconds (from fridge) or 60 seconds (from frozen).

Pastina Soup (Italian Penicillin)

5 Stars 4 Stars 3 Stars 2 Stars 1 Star

4.9 from 11 reviews

  • Author: Lindsay
  • Total Time: 45 minutes
  • Yield: 6–8 servings 1x
Print Pin

Description

This soup has become my go-to recipe when my family is sick. The broth is amazing – simple, light, flavorful, and nourishing! It’s absolute comfort food. 

Ingredients

Units USM

For the Broth:

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 large carrots, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 2 stalks of celery, cut into large chunks
  • half of 1 yellow onion, peeled and cut into large chunks
  • 4 cloves garlic, peeled and smashed
  • 10 cups chicken or vegetable broth (I use 10 cups water plus 2 tablespoons Better Than Bouillon chicken base)
  • 1 teaspoon salt

For Serving: 

  • 1 1/2 cups pastina (I use Stelline from the Good and Gather brand at Target)
  • freshly grated Parmesan cheese for topping
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Sauté Veggies: Heat oil in a large Dutch oven over medium-high heat. Add the chunky carrots, celery, onion, and garlic to the pot. Sauté for 5 minutes. 
  2. Simmer: Add broth and salt. Simmer for 30 minutes, until the vegetables are very tender.
  3. Remove Veggies: Use a slotted spoon to transfer the vegetable chunks to a bowl. Set aside to cool for a few minutes (you don’t want to add scorching hot veggies to your plastic blender). 
  4. Blend: When the veggies have cooled a bit, blend them with 1-2 cups of broth until very smooth. Add the blended mixture back to the pot; stir to incorporate.
  5. Pastina: Cook pasta according to package directions. Drain and toss with olive oil and a pinch of salt. 
  6. Serve: Put a scoop of pastina in a shallow bowl. Ladle the steaming-hot broth over the top, then top each bowl with freshly grated Parmesan.

Yat gaw mein, also known as Baltimore yak or dirty yak, is Baltimore carryout comfort food at its finest. A Chinese American remix of Beijing’s zha jiang mian, the dish dates back to the late 19th century, when Chinese immigrants started settling in the city. Setting up shop in traditionally Black and working-class neighborhoods, Chinese cooks adapted their recipes to locally available ingredients, swapping fermented pastes for a simpler, soy-based gravy. Over time, something new emerged: savory wheat noodles, stir-fried with shrimp, beef, and vegetables and punctuated with a hard-boiled egg. Today you can find the beloved dish at counter spots around town like Jimmy’s Carryout, Danny’s, and Pimlico Chinese Carry Out.

In this at-home version from Steve Chu, the chef behind Baltimore’s Ekiben, smoky wok-charred noodles are tossed with umami-packed soy sauce and oyster sauce, rich fat, and sweet caramelized onions. What makes the dish “dirty” is the flavor that develops when soy sauce, sugar, and aromatics scorch against the hot metal of the wok, creating deep savory notes balanced by subtle sweetness. Be sure to keep the wok on high heat and stir frequently, since this allows the wok hei—which translates to “the breath of a wok” and refers to that elusive smokiness—to work its magic. The finishing squeeze of ketchup might sound unusual, but it delivers the acidic punch that makes the dish complete.

Makes: 2–4 Time: 25 minutes

Ingredients

  • Kosher salt
  • 1 lb. fresh wheat noodles, such as lo mein or udon
  • ¼ cup duck fat, chicken fat, vegetable oil, or other cooking fat
  • 1½ cups bean sprouts
  • 1 medium onion, thinly sliced
  • 1 medium scallion, thinly sliced
  • ¼ cup dark soy sauce, plus more if needed
  • 2 Tbsp. light soy sauce
  • 2 Tbsp. oyster sauce
  • 1 Tbsp. MSG or chicken bouillon powder
  • 1 Tbsp. sugar
  • 1–2 tsp. Tianjin chile flakes or crushed red chile flakes
  • Steamed or poached shrimp or medium-rare seared beef slices, for topping
  • 1 hard-boiled egg, peeled, for topping
  • Ketchup and hot sauce, for serving

Instructions

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil. Add the noodles and cook for 1 minute less than the package instructions so they remain slightly undercooked. Drain, then rinse briefly under cold water. Set aside to cool. 
  2. Heat a wok over high until lightly smoking. Add the duck fat, followed by the bean sprouts, onion, and scallion. Stir-fry until the mixture begins to brown around the edges, about 1 minute. Add the noodles, both soy sauces, the oyster sauce, MSG, sugar, and chile flakes. Stir-fry, tossing continuously so the sauce coats the noodles evenly and begins to brown against the wok, 1–4 minutes. (If a darker color is desired, add a little more dark soy sauce.)
  3. Add 1½ cups of water to the wok, scraping up the browned bits, and simmer until the noodles absorb some of the liquid, about 1 minute. (If you prefer a saucier dish, add a little more water.) Add the shrimp and hard-boiled egg, tossing gently so the egg warms through and takes on some of the dark soy color without breaking apart. 
  4. Serve immediately, with ketchup and hot sauce on the side.

If you’ve ever been starstruck by a perfect Cinderella pumpkin or a stately stalk of Brussels sprouts, you likely understand the appeal of a book focused on a single fruit or vegetable. While books like this aren’t always long, they typically go deep, with the fervor of an author who is fully obsessed.

One charming example, The Core of an Onion: Peeling the Rarest Common Food by Mark Kurlansky, melds authority with a dose of irreverence. First published in 2023, it’s a slim 200 pages, but the writer (who’s also written nonfiction books dedicated to oysters, salt, and cod) packs a lot in. “Onions have a way of taking on unexpected significance,” he posits. The first section covers the allium’s history and cultural impact. (Did you know that ancient Egyptians tied them to mummies’ feet as a preservative?) Kurlansky then offers loosely structured recipes for breads, sauces, and more, all centering the humble bulb.

Elevating a perhaps overlooked ingredient is a core tenet of single-­subject cookbookery. In The Compleat Squash: A Passionate Grower’s Guide to Pumpkins, Squash, and Gourds, a canonical 2004 ode to members of the Cucurbitaceae ­family, master ­gardener Amy Goldman Fowler’s passion is abundantly evident. “Sometimes I don’t know where pumpkins begin and I end,” she writes, before segueing into a series of squash and gourd portraits, each showing an individual specimen more striking than the last. Fowler shares recipes too, in addition to breed spotlights, resources for seed ­buying, and advice on growing your own.

Some such tomes aim to introduce the reader to the joys of less ubiquitous ingredients. Lane Selman and Margarett Waterbury’s Radicchio: A Celebration of the Beloved Italian Vegetable tells the “human and natural history” of the bitter chicory, along with instructions for how to grow it and cook with it. Selman is the founder of the Culinary Breeding Network, an initiative that rallies scientists, farmers, and chefs to geek out over new produce varieties. The book gets weird in the best way possible, with profiles of Italian growers and recipes including gnocchi with radicchio pesto, radicchio with prosciutto ­gratin, and chocolate–radicchio cake (why not?).

Radicchio reads almost like a community cookbook, and that’s intentional. Selman’s inspiration to pursue the topic was the sheer lack of writing on this oddball vegetable: “All of these people that we’ve met on our way down the rabbit hole needed to be included,” she says. “They’re the ones who have given us all this information. Sometimes it takes a village.”

Other single-subject cookbooks showcase pantry ­staples that are rarely in the spotlight. Anna Hezel’s Little Book of Olives: A Guide to Cooking, Eating, and Drinking for the Brine-Inclined presents recipes including cantaloupe and crushed-olive panzanella, Castelvetrano and pistachio pesto, and a whole chapter of olive desserts, plus shopping tips and a breakdown of varieties. The book’s retro aesthetic lends ­gravitas to an ingredient often relegated to garnish status.

Finally, some books exist more to dazzle than to inform, as is the case with pop artist Ed Ruscha and River Café chef Ruthie Rogers’s 2025 release Squeeze Me: Lemon Recipes & Art. In the pair’s high-gloss love letter to the lemon, pop art–coded photos with cheeky captions and a suite of minimalist recipes—such as lemon–­mascarpone tart and limoncello—make this ode to the simple citrus equally at home on a coffee table or the kitchen shelf.

While styles and forms vary, these books serve a ­common goal: to illuminate the sometimes surprising pleasures of a particular fruit or vegetable. That they manage to do so in such delightful fashion is proof that, once in a while, ­working with less really is more.

Miso walnut chocolate chip cookies

There are two things I need to tell you about these cookies.

One is that they’re crazy-good. And the other is that they’re huge. The recipe makes just five cookies but each one is so packed with flavor, that you’ll be glad you can have one all to yourself. Your other four friends or guests will be delighted as well. (But they’re quite generous, so it’s okay to share…)

What makes these cookies extra special is their slightly savory, umami note, due to walnuts glazed with white miso. Miso is fermented soybean paste that can be purchased in Asian markets, but has become so popular, you can often find it in grocery stores. In these cookies, it adds a little je ne sais quoi, since it adds a subtle, yet complex note of flavors. Umami is often described as a flavor that’s craveable, and it certainly makes a compelling case for that description in these cookies.

Miso Walnut Chocolate Chip Cookies

Adapted from SIFT by Nicola Lamb When you form the five mounds of dough, avoid compacting them too tight. You want to form them into loose balls rather than rolling them in your hands into smooth rounds. They're best when you chop the chocolate from bars of good-quality dark chocolate, and I leave the pieces fairly large – about the size of almonds, but you can use store-bought chocolate chunks if you wish. I baked my batch of cookies for 15 to 16 minutes, although the original recipe says 18 minutes. All ovens are different, but they're done when they're light brown across the top. The cookies shown in the post were baked on a cloudy day, so may look a bit dark. You want to err on the side of underbaking rather than overbaking. Another tip is that you can sprinkle extra flaky salt on top of each cookie when they come out of the oven. These cookies are best enjoyed the day they’re made but are delicious the next day as well. Ingredients

Miso walnuts

  • 3 tablespoons mirin rice wine
  • 3 tablespoons granulated sugar
  • 3 tablespoons (50g) white miso
  • 1 cup (100g) walnut halves toasted

Cookie dough

  • 6 tablespoons (90g) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 6 tablespoons (85g) light brown sugar
  • 6 tablespoons (85g) granulated sugar

Instructions

  • To make the miso walnuts, in a medium saucepan or skillet over moderate heat, warm the mirin, the granulated sugar, and the miso, stirring to incorporate and smooth out the ingredients. Description

Pan-fried chicken in a luscious Cajun cream sauce, served with mashed potatoes and green beans – loving this easy, cozy weeknight dinner!

Ingredients

Units USM

Chicken:

  • 1 tablespoon avocado oil
  • 1 lb. chicken breast
  • 1 1/2 teaspoon Cajun seasoning (I used McCormick)

Sauce:

  • 1 tablespoon butter
  • 1 shallot, minced or thinly sliced
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 1 teaspoon Cajun seasoning (I used McCormick)
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 3/4 cup heavy cream
  • 1 cup chicken broth (I use water + 1 tsp Better Than Bouillon)

For serving: 

  • Mashed Potatoes
  • Green beans or salad
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Prep the Chicken: Cut the chicken into thinner pieces horizontally, or pound them out a bit so they’re thinner.
  2. Cook the Chicken: Season the chicken filets with Cajun seasoning. Heat the oil in a skillet over medium-high heat (I use a nonstick for this). Sauté the chicken on each side for 3-4 minutes, until the internal temperature reaches 165 and the outside is golden; remove from the pan and set aside on a plate.
  3. Sauté Shallots: Wipe off any overly browned bits from the pan. Turn the heat to medium-low so the butter and shallots don’t burn. Add butter and shallots; saute until softened and fragrant.
  4. Make The Roux: Add tomato paste and Cajun seasoning; saute for 3-4 minutes until the tomato paste has developed a rich color. Add flour and mix; cook for 2-3 minutes to remove any floury taste.
  5. Finish Sauce: Add broth gradually; whisk to incorporate after each addition. Once it’s smooth, add heavy cream and whisk again to incorporate. Let it simmer for 5-10 minutes to thicken slightly. Add chicken back to the pan so it is partially submerged with sauce.
  6. Serve: Serve pieces of chicken with a spoonful of the sauce over the top. I love this SO MUCH with mashed potatoes and broccoli – see notes!

Notes

To air fry the chicken: Air fry at 375 for 10-12 minutes, or until at least 165 degrees internal temperature.

Roasted Potatoes and Broccoli (Oven): Toss 3-4 cups halved baby potatoes with avocado oil and salt on a sheet pan. Roast the potatoes at 425 for 15 minutes. Scoot the potatoes over, add 3-4 cups broccoli and ¼ cup water to the same sheet pan, and roast for another 15 minutes.

Roasted Potatoes and Broccoli (Air Fryer): Toss 2 cups halved baby potatoes with avocado oil and salt. Air fry at 375 for 12 minutes. Add 2 cups frozen or fresh broccoli; air fry at 400 for another 10-12 minutes.

Marmite Roast Potatoes may not be for everyone, but if you’ve come this far, they may be the roasties you’ve been looking for!  In fact this recipe could help you to make the perfect roast potato, full stop.

What are Marmite Roast Potatoes?

This isn’t like Marmite on toast; each of our Marmite Roast Potatoes isn’t smeared with a massive dollop of Marmite.

Marmite Roast Potatoes are fantastic roasties whose crispy exteriors have a hint of the savoury tang that makes Marmite so unique.

Where do Marmite Roast Potatoes come from?

What is Marmite?

The star of Marmite Roast Potatoes isn’t actually Marmite. It plays only a small part in the story of the fantastic roastie! However, some may not know exactly what we’re talking about, and it can be a little confusing, so here’s a brief description of the black stuff.

Marmite is a thick, dark brown/black spread made from yeast extract. It was the happy accident of a scientist in Germany who found that a byproduct of brewing beer tasted terrific. How he found this out is not precisely known, but it must have been an odd day…

Soon after, the Marmite Food Company was established in Burton on Trent, England, and Marmite has been made there ever since.

We’ve used it before in our Marmite Cheese Scones recipe, as it goes so well with cheese.

Sonja would like it pointed out here that New Zealand has its own Marmite, which is similar but different from the British version.

The flavour is similar to that of its British cousin, although the UK version includes more sugar and caramel. We could debate the merits of each, but you can use either in this Marmite Roast Potatoes recipe. NZ Marmite is less runny and a bit stronger, so you’ll need to account for that.

And the UK one is miles better…

What are Roast Potatoes?

We tend to name things very simply here in the UK; roast potatoes, unsurprisingly, are potatoes that are roasted.

The way they are roasted, though, is always up for debate. Typically, they are placed in the oven while the meat of a roast dinner is cooking.

But potato preparation involves all sorts of questions: Do you parboil them? Do you peel them? What fat do you coat them in? Do you pre-heat the oven tray?

All these questions pop up in a conversation about roast potatoes; every family has its preferred method. We even know some people who don’t roast them at all, preferring to deep fry them.

Ultimately, like stovies, a full Scottish breakfast and mince and tatties, recipes vary from family to family. Here, we’ll show you how we do it and how best to ensure that the marmite flavour sings!

What Potatoes Are Best for Marmite Roast Potatoes?

Who knew there were so many types of potatoes? Not us until we started investigating for this post…

It turns out that different types of potatoes are best for different things. For example, potatoes with higher starch, sometimes referred to as more “floury,” are best for roasting. This is because they tend to have a low moisture content, so they fluff up in the middle and crisp on the outside.

In the UK, this would generally be Maris Piper or King Edwards potatoes, among others. In the USA, it’s often Russet potatoes, and in Canada, Yukon Gold is popular.

What Fat is Best for Marmite Roast Potatoes?

Roasting potatoes requires some fat to coat them and help them crisp up on the outside. As we mentioned, everyone tends to use whatever suits them best!

Duck fat is popular in the UK but obviously doesn’t suit vegetarians. Butter and oil are also popular, and we actually like to use a combination of both.

Should you Parboil the Potatoes?

To parboil means to partially cook. Basically, you boil the potatoes for 5-10 minutes before you put them in the oven.

There are a couple of reasons you might want to do this. First, it reduces the cooking time in the oven, which is useful when you’re cooking lots of potatoes at once, as you often are with roast potatoes. Second, it helps them crisp up, which, as we mentioned, is often considered to be the best thing for roast potatoes!

Alternatively, you can toss the potatoes in the fat and cook straight in the oven.

Things you’ll need to make Marmite Roast Potatoes

  • Large baking tray with a lip on the side to hold in the melted fat
  • Large pot to boil the potatoes in

Ingredients for Marmite Roast Potatoes

  • 1.5kg Maris Piper Potatoes (or your choice!)
  • 50g salted butter
  • 6 tbsp olive oil
  • 1-2 heaped tsp Marmite

How to make Marmite Roast Potatoes – Step-by-step method

Peel your potatoes and slice them into a uniform size. We like them to be around the size of half a satsuma.

Preheat the oven to 180C/356F.

Bring them to the boil on the stove for 5-10 minutes. We tend to do the longer time.

Take them off the boil and drain. Allow them to air dry a bit and shake them in the pot so some of the edges fluff up.

Put the butter and oil on a baking tray and put it in the oven so they heat up.

Remove and add the Marmite, stirring it into the oil/butter mix. I tend to use two teaspoons, one to measure out a heap of Marmite and the other to scrape it off, then stir them both through. We like to go heavy on the Marmite, but it’s up to you!

Carefully place the potatoes on the tray and turn them so they’re coated in the oil/butter/Marmite mix.

Roast in the oven for 30-45 minutes, depending on the size of the potatoes. Turn at least once.

For extra crispiness, turn up to 200C/400F for the final 5 minutes.

Serve and enjoy!

Tips and Tricks for the Perfect Roast Potato

Things we think make roast potatoes even better:

  • Cut them to equal size
  • Parboil
  • Leave them to steam so they dry out a bit more
  • Heat the tray and melt the fat first so the potatoes sizzle when you put them in it
  • We prefer them with an onion or beef-based gravy
