The ‘Tavern-Style’ Pizza Takeover

📅 Oct 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →
The thin-crust pizzas popular throughout the Midwest have taken off as the “tavern-style” pizza trend | Brad Bobrow/La Merna

In the suburbs of Chicago where they both grew up, it was just pizza, according to Brittany and Brennan Grosvenor, the couple behind Proof in Portland, Oregon. Ordering came down to the question of “thin-crust, deep-dish, or double-dough,” Brittany recalls of her family’s Friday night tradition. The Grosvernors sell the former at Proof, though the language of “thin crust” has given way to the more nationally familiar phrasing of “tavern-style,” the pizza trend that has swept from coast to coast in recent years. 

Outsiders might associate Chicago with its famous, tourist-beckoning deep-dish pizza, but Brittany says that’s just not reflective of reality, citing deep dish as a much less frequent treat than the Midwestern, thin-crust style that’s gaining popularity. “The pizza you get every week, or if you go to a kid’s birthday party, it’s always tavern-style,” she says.

“Tavern-style” pizza — which is prevalent  across the Midwest, with Chicago, Milwaukee, Minnesota, and St. Louis touting similar pies — is known for its thin, cracker-like crust, cut into a lattice instead of triangular slices. Depending on the region and the pizza shop where one’s nostalgia lies, the sauce might lean sweet or spicy. Lightness and crispness, though, is the style’s unique sell. Writing for Esquire in 2023, Omar Mamoon called the variant, which was then still on the rise, “a foil” to famously thick Detroit-style pizza, which had its big moment in the late 2010s. 

The beauty of the tavern-style pie is its shareability. A pizza yields more slices when cut into a grid than into triangles, explains Bradley Shorten, who’s behind both Kim’s Uncle Pizza in his hometown Chicago suburb of Westmont, Illinois, and the recently opened La Merna in Washington, D.C. Pizza cut into little squares makes for great drinking food, especially since it fits on a cocktail napkin. What makes a good pie, according to Shorten, is that every square has a little of every ingredient; the toppings go to the edge. And while the four, small corner slices might not be the “best” ones, Shorten says, they’re the ones “everybody goes for,” especially if they’re from Chicago.

Over the past few years, as “taverns” have become growing fixations for a cadre of restaurateurs, the “party cut” pizza and tavern-style pizza spots have also become ubiquitous, with openings like Keepers in New York city; One Trick Pony in Forth Worth, Texas; Tillie’s Tavern Pizza in Wheat Ridge, Colorado; and Zarella and Dimmi Dimmi Corner Italian in Chicago, where the style has always been abundant. For some Chicagoans, these openings across the country are an opportunity to correct the record about their city’s pizza culture, and for other Midwesterners to claim their stake in the region’s pizza, too.

When Mike Fadem opened the second location of his popular NYC pizzeria Ops — known for its wood-fired Neapolitan-esque pies with leopard-spotted crusts and pliant dough — he wanted to offer something new. Fadem is from St. Louis, where the city’s namesake pizza is super-thin. Despite his love for it, “it’s maybe not the best version of tavern pizza,” Fadem admits, noting the iteration’s many critics, some of whom take issue with its use of the gooey processed cheese called provel. The trajectory of tavern-style pizza has been like a game of telephone, he explains: St. Louis pizza is derived from thin-crust Chicago pizza, which itself came from Neapolitan pizza. Divergence has accompanied each jump.

One of Fadem’s employees at Ops, meanwhile, is from New Jersey, which claims its own thin-crust, tavern- or bar-style pizza tradition. (The bar pies of the Boston area also have their own devoted following.) Accordingly, “I had this interest in ‘can we make an Ops version of this?’” Fadem says. With Ops known for sourdough, Fadem also wanted to highlight the rich flavor of whole grains. And he wanted it to be really crispy, a quality he says draws in New Yorkers. Fadem’s take is on the menu as the regionally agnostic “tavernetta.” Though St. Louis and New Jersey served as initial inspiration, the best pizza from his research was actually in Milwaukee. The Tavernetta hasn’t displaced the other pies at the restaurant; it’s ordered in addition to them, with most groups getting at least one.

In June, Los Angeles cocktail spot Bar Next Door celebrated its third anniversary by adding food to its menu. Previously, the bar had allowed customers to bring in pies from Prince Street Pizza next door since Prince Street co-owner Lawrence Longo is a partner in it. “But, you know, after having a slice, sometimes you get a little full and you’re like, I want to go to bed,” says owner and bar director Brynn Smith. “That’s the whole goal for us: for people to stay a little longer, drink more. We thought the tavern-style pizza would be perfect.” The fact that the pizzas — they’re technically listed on the menu as “bar pies,” to better go with the establishment’s name — are made exclusively for the bar by Prince Street just adds another reason for people to go out, Smith says.

The idea of the tavern as a social and convivial space is inseparable from the tavern-style pizza. Shorten’s goal with La Merna — which he runs with his wife Meg Schmitt, who grew up in St. Louis — was the feel of a Chicago dive bar or a Wisconsin supper club: a place with wood-paneled walls and checkered floors, where people could slide into a red leather booth for pitchers of beer and pizza and hang out for a while. As my colleague Francky Knapp wrote last year, the rising appeal of the “tavern” vibe across restaurants nationwide is rooted in the sense of providing somewhere homey and lived-in. At taverns, diners are beckoned to sprawl out and stay awhile; to free themselves of pretense and to tap into something welcoming in its familiarity. This, for so many people, is exemplified by the archetype of hometown pizza shop, which is forever tethered in our cultural imaginations to the trappings of a previous, simpler era.

It’s for this reason that, in Oregon, the Grosvenors have started serving their take on Bosco sticks, the stuffed breadsticks of Midwestern cafeterias, and Italian beef with giardiniera. While they could find certain Midwestern hits around Portland, it sometimes frustrated them that they couldn’t find everything in one place. According to Brennan Grosvenor, “We’re just trying to embody the feeling: If you went into a tavern in Chicago, what could you order there?”

I’ve been wanting to make Rasta Pasta for a while because I’m a BIG fan of bold, spicy flavors. This Jamaican-inspired dish brings all that flavor to the table with tender jerk-seasoned chicken, sweet bell peppers, and penne coated in a creamy Parmesan sauce. The peppers and creamy sauce help balance the warmth of the jerk seasoning, and everything cooks in one skillet for less cleanup! It’s colorful, hearty, genuinely delicious, and ready in 45 minutes. Better yet, at just $2.80 per serving, it’s a fun way to switch up pasta night without spending a fortune!

A Spiced Chicken and Peppers Pasta

If you’ve never heard of Rasta pasta, here’s a little background! It’s believed to have originated in Negril, Jamaica, in 1985, when chef Lorraine Washington served homemade fettuccine with tomato sauce and ackee (Jamaica’s national fruit) to a group of construction workers. The red, gold (yellow), and green colors associated with the Rastafari movement inspired one of the workers to name it Rasta pasta. And you’ll spot those same colors in the three bell peppers I use in my recipe, too! There are plenty of versions of this dish today, but the one I’m most familiar with is this creamy, jerk-seasoned pasta. My recipe is a simple, budget-friendly take on this dish, rather than a recreation of the original.

For my version, I’ve kept things easy on both the cook AND the grocery budget! I sear the jerk-seasoned chicken first, then cook the colorful peppers in the same skillet so none of those delicious browned bits go to waste. I also skip boiling the penne separately and simmer it right in the chicken broth, where it absorbs all that savory seasoning and releases starch to help thicken the sauce. Half-and-half keeps things creamy without the extra cost of heavy cream, and I stir in Parmesan off the heat for a smooth finish. It’s a similar approach to our creamy Cajun chicken pasta, but with the bold flavor of jerk seasoning and plenty of colorful peppers.

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Cut the chicken into thin cutlets. I slice each chicken breast horizontally in half before seasoning. This gives me four thinner pieces that cook more evenly and quickly, without the outside drying out before the center is done. Aim for 3-4 minutes per side, but always check the thickest part reaches 165°F with a meat thermometer. And here’s a little budget win: I only need 1 lb. of chicken for four hearty servings, thanks to all those peppers and pasta!
  2. Adjust the jerk seasoning to your spice tolerance. Jerk blends can vary a LOT in heat and saltiness. For a milder pasta, I’d choose a mild jerk blend rather than leaving out most of the seasoning. That way, you still get those lovely aromatic flavors from spices like allspice and thyme. The broth and Parmesan add salt, too, so give the finished sauce a taste before adding any more.
  3. Keep an eye on the pasta and the liquid. Unlike some of our other one-pot pasta recipes, I simmer this one uncovered. This lets some of the broth evaporate as the penne releases starch, creating a flavorful, slightly thickened base for our creamy sauce. Keep the broth at a steady simmer and stir often. The penne should still have a little firmness in the center, with most of the broth absorbed. It’ll finish cooking once you add the half-and-half. If the pan looks dry before the pasta is ready, add a splash of hot broth or water. Still looking too soupy? Give it another minute or two to reduce before adding the dairy. Different pans and stovetops can make a difference here!
  4. Be gentle with the creamy sauce. Half-and-half is my budget-friendly alternative to heavy cream, but its lower fat content makes it more likely to separate if you boil it too hard. Once you add it, keep the heat low and let the sauce thicken around the pasta. Then turn off the heat before stirring in the Parmesan to help prevent clumping. I also save the fresh lime for serving rather than adding it directly to the hot dairy sauce. Everyone can add a squeeze to their own bowl, and there’s less risk of the acidity making the sauce grainy.

Meal Prep it!

I’m all for getting an extra lunch out of dinner, and this Rasta pasta is PERFECT for meal prepping! Whenever I’m serving veggies on the side, I’ll usually make a little extra and pack them alongside the pasta (like the broccoli and green beans shown here). I like using divided meal prep containers to keep the veggies from sitting in the creamy sauce. Then, once the pasta is cool, I’ll just pop the lid on and store everything in the fridge for up to 4 days. The pasta does soak up some of the sauce as it sits, so I add a little splash of water or broth when reheating in the microwave to bring back its saucy consistency.

If you know you’ll be reheating the pasta throughout the week, cook it just until tender rather than letting it get overly soft in the pot.

Rasta Pasta

This easy Rasta Pasta recipe has jerk-seasoned chicken, colorful peppers, and a creamy Parmesan sauce. All made in one pot in just 45 minutes! Instructions

  • Gather all ingredients.
  • Dice onion, slice bell peppers, and mince garlic.
  • Slice each chicken breast horizontally in half to create four thin cutlets. Season both sides with about 1 tablespoon of the jerk seasoning.
  • Heat the oil in a large Dutch oven or deep skillet over medium-high heat. Add the chicken and cook for 3-4 minutes per side, or until browned and cooked through with the temperature reading at least 165°F. Transfer the chicken to a plate to rest.
  • Add the onion and bell peppers to the same pot. Cook for 4-5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the vegetables begin to soften and the browned bits from the chicken have loosened from the bottom of the pot.
  • Add the garlic and remaining ½ tablespoon jerk seasoning. Cook for 1 minute, stirring constantly.
  • Add the penne and chicken broth. Stir to combine and bring to a simmer. Cook uncovered for 8-10 minutes, stirring frequently (see notes) so the pasta doesn't stick, until the pasta is nearly tender and most of the broth has been absorbed.
  • Stir in the half-and-half and continue cooking for 3-4 minutes, stirring frequently, until the pasta is tender and the sauce is creamy.
  • Remove the pot from the heat and stir in the Parmesan. If the sauce is thicker than you’d like, add a splash of water or broth.
  • Slice the chicken into strips, cutting against the grain to keep the chicken juicy. (see notes)
  • Add sliced chicken onto pasta and finish with sliced green onion and a squeeze of fresh lime if desired. Enjoy.

how to make Rasta Pasta Step-by-Step Photos

Gather your ingredients: Before getting started, gather and measure everything you’ll need for this pasta. I recommend having your jerk seasoning, chicken broth, half-and-half, and Parmesan ready to go so you can focus on cooking once the skillet gets hot. You’ll also need a large, deep skillet or Dutch oven with enough room to stir the pasta as it cooks.

Prep the vegetables: Dice 1 yellow onion, slice 1 red, 1 green, and 1 yellow bell pepper into strips, and mince 3 cloves of garlic. Try to keep the pepper slices roughly the same thickness so they soften evenly in the skillet.

Prepare and season the chicken: Carefully slice 2 large boneless, skinless chicken breasts (about 1 lb. total) horizontally through the middle to make 4 thinner cutlets. Sprinkle about 1 Tbsp of jerk seasoning over both sides, making sure each cutlet is evenly coated.

Cook the chicken: Heat 1 Tbsp cooking oil in a large Dutch oven or deep skillet over medium-high heat. Once the oil is hot and shimmering, add the seasoned chicken. You should hear a nice sizzle! Cook for 3-4 minutes per side, until the outside is nicely browned and the thickest part reaches an internal temperature of 165°F. Transfer the chicken to a plate and let it rest while you prepare the pasta. Don’t wash the skillet! Those browned bits left behind will add flavor to the sauce.

Soften the vegetables: Add the diced onion and sliced bell peppers to the same skillet. Cook for 4-5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onion begins to turn translucent and the peppers start to soften but still have a little bite. As you stir, gently scrape up the flavorful browned bits left behind by the chicken.

Add the garlic and seasoning: Stir in the 3 minced garlic cloves and the remaining ½ Tbsp jerk seasoning. Cook for 1 minute, stirring constantly, until the garlic smells fragrant and the seasoning is evenly distributed. Keep everything moving so the garlic and spices don’t burn.

Cook the pasta in the broth: Add 8 oz. penne pasta and 2 cups chicken broth to the skillet. Stir well, making sure the pasta is coated in the seasoned broth, then bring everything to a simmer. Cook uncovered for 8-10 minutes, stirring frequently and scraping along the bottom to prevent sticking. The pasta should be nearly tender, with most of the broth absorbed and a little starchy liquid remaining. Don’t worry if the pasta isn’t completely cooked yet…it’ll finish cooking in the next step.

Make it creamy: Pour in 1 cup half-and-half and stir to combine. Continue cooking for 3-4 minutes, stirring frequently, until the penne is tender and the sauce has thickened. Keep it at a gentle simmer, not a vigorous boil. The sauce should look creamy and lightly coat the pasta.

Finish with Parmesan: Remove the skillet from the heat and stir in ½ cup (45g) finely grated Parmesan cheese. Keep stirring until the cheese melts into the warm, starchy sauce and everything looks smooth and creamy. If the sauce seems a little too thick, stir in a splash of water or chicken broth until it reaches your preferred consistency.

Slice the chicken: Once the chicken has rested, slice each cutlet into thin strips, cutting across the direction of the muscle fibers (against the grain). This shortens the fibers, making the chicken more tender and easier to chew.

Garnish and serve: Arrange the sliced jerk-seasoned chicken over the creamy pasta. Finish with 2 sliced green onions and a squeeze of fresh lime from 1 lime cut into wedges, if desired. (However, I highly recommend the lime! Its fresh acidity adds a lovely finishing touch to the warmly spiced sauce). Serve warm and enjoy!

How to Serve Rasta Pasta

This recipe makes four generous 2-cup servings, so it’s a pretty hearty meal all on its own! I like to serve it in bowls with the sliced jerk chicken on top, a sprinkle of green onions, and a squeeze of fresh lime to brighten up that creamy sauce. If I’m adding a side, I’ll usually go for something fresh and crunchy. A simple side salad or our cucumber mango salad is a delicious match, with sweet mango and tangy dressing that pair beautifully with the warm jerk spices. It also makes four servings and takes just 15 minutes! Steamed broccoli is another easy option, or try our sautéed green beans, which also serve four and are ready in 15 minutes.

And if you really want to lean into the Jamaican-Italian fusion of this recipe, why not add some homemade garlic bread? Those buttery, crispy edges are perfect for scooping up any leftover creamy sauce. It’s ready in 25 minutes, so you can get it in the oven while the pasta cooks!

Storage & Reheating

Let any leftovers cool, then transfer it to an airtight container and refrigerate for up to 3-4 days. I recommend storing any extra green onions and lime wedges separately so you can add them fresh when serving.

Reheating

The pasta will absorb some of the sauce as it sits, so I like adding a splash of water, chicken broth, or milk when reheating. You have two options:

  • Stovetop: Add the pasta to a skillet with a splash of liquid and warm over medium-low heat, stirring occasionally until heated through.
  • Microwave: Place a portion in a microwave-safe bowl with a little water or broth. Cover loosely and heat in 30-second intervals, stirring between each, until heated through.

Whichever method you use, reheat the chicken and pasta to 165°F throughout. Keep the heat gentle to help prevent the creamy sauce from separating.

Freezing

I don’t recommend freezing this Rasta pasta. The creamy sauce can separate once thawed, and the penne can become overly soft when reheated. It’s definitely best enjoyed fresh or refrigerated for a few days.

One of the first lessons I learned, right after I moved to Paris, was to never, ever be on time for a dinner party. When i got my first invitation to a dinner party, my hosts told me, “Come at 8pm…which in Paris, means to come at 8:20 or 8:30pm.”

Subsequently, when I have guests for dinner, I expect them to be at least 20 minutes late, although there’s much debate on how late you’re actually supposed to be. But if you’re on time, or early, your host likely won’t not be ready and you’ll probably catch them by surprise.

It’s a tricky balance when you’ve inviting people for dinner, since you want make sure when everyone’s at the table, dinner will be ready and perfectly cooked. If your friends show up very late, and you’ve made something like pork roast, which can take a while to bake, but will dry out if overcooked, you’re sunk.

So one technique I rely on is braising. Braising is cooking something long and slow, which softens cuts of meat and poultry, and infuses whatever you’re cooking with flavor. In the case of a tagine, a North African dish that benefits from braising, your cooking the meat in a broth of onions, aromatics, and spices, which all come together after a stint in the over to make a perfect one-pot meal, that’s can be kept warm in the oven until everyone is at the table.

In Paris, with so many Arabic and North African butchers around, it’s easy to find cuts of meat that lend themselves to slow-braising, and tagines are quite popular in France. When I wrote my book, My Paris Kitchen, someone wrote to me, wondering why I had non-French recipes in my book. Just like people in other countries, such as Americans who make Mexican and Asian foods at home, or Dutch people who eat rijsttafel, an Indonesian meal composed of many side dishes, French people regularly eat a variety of foods from other cultures, especially in Paris, which is a melting pot, where you can enjoy couscous in Belleville, dosas in the Indian quarter, and Vietnamese noodles in the thirteenth arrondissement. But really, you can get all those foods in just about every corner of the city.

Instructions

  • Massage the pieces of lamb shoulder with the salt.
  • Heat a few tablespoons of oil in a Dutch oven over medium-high heat, and sear the lamb pieces very well, turning them only after they’re nicely browned (this step adds flavor to the tagine.)   • Preheat the oven to 350ºF (175ºC). Once the lamb is browned, remove the pieces from the pot and set them on a plate. Recipe Success Tips

How to Make Italian Wedding Soup Step-by-Step Photos

Gather all your meatball ingredients: You’ll need ¼ cup plain breadcrumbs, ¼ cup grated Parmesan, ½ tsp Italian seasoning, ⅛ tsp salt, ⅛ tsp black pepper, 1 lb. ground Italian sausage (hot, mild, or sweet), and 1 large egg.

Gather all your soup ingredients: You’ll need 1 yellow onion, 2 cloves garlic, 3 carrots, 3 stalks celery, 2 Tbsp olive oil, 1 tsp Italian seasoning, 6 cups chicken broth, ½ cup small pasta, and 4 cups fresh spinach.

Prep the vegetables: Finely dice 1 yellow onion, 3 carrots, and 3 stalks celery, and mince 2 cloves garlic. I like to keep the carrots and celery fairly small, so they soften quickly, and you get a little of everything in each spoonful.

Combine the dry meatball ingredients: In a bowl, stir together ¼ cup plain breadcrumbs, ¼ cup grated Parmesan, ½ tsp Italian seasoning, ⅛ tsp salt, and ⅛ tsp black pepper. Mixing these together first helps distribute the seasonings evenly throughout the meat mix.

Add the sausage and egg: Add 1 lb. ground Italian sausage and 1 large egg to a large bowl with the breadcrumb mixture. You can use mild, sweet, or hot Italian sausage, depending on how much spice you like! I like using mild personally.

Mix the meatballs: Use a fork to start mixing the ingredients, then finish combining them with your hands if needed. The mixture should look evenly combined and feel slightly sticky. Don’t go overboard with the mixing, though. Working the meat too much can leave you with dense meatballs.

Shape the meatballs: Measure about 1 Tbsp of the mixture to form ~20 meatballs. Place them on a baking sheet as you go. I find a cookie scoop makes this so much easier because the meatballs all come out roughly the same size, which helps them cook evenly. Set them aside for now.

Sauté the vegetables: Add the diced onion, carrots, celery, and minced garlic to a large soup pot with 2 Tbsp olive oil and 1 tsp Italian seasoning. Cook over medium heat for about 4 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onion begins to look translucent, the garlic smells fragrant, and the vegetables start to soften. Don’t worry if the carrots are still a little firm! They’ll continue cooking in the broth.

Add the broth: Pour 6 cups chicken broth into the pot and give everything a good stir. Place a lid on top and bring the soup up to a boil. I like to give the bottom of the pot a little scrape as I stir to make sure all those flavorful bits from the vegetables make it into the broth.

(Optional) Brown the meatballs: If you prefer a little extra color and savory flavor, you can brown the meatballs in a skillet with 1 Tbsp olive oil over medium heat before adding them to the soup. Look for a nice golden color on the outside, but don’t worry about cooking them all the way through just yet. They’ll finish cooking in the broth. This step is completely optional.

Add the meatballs to the soup: My favorite (and easiest!) method is to skip the browning and add the raw meatballs directly to the boiling broth. Let them cook for about 10 minutes, adjusting the heat if the soup starts boiling too vigorously. The meatballs should become firmer and opaque on the outside as they cook. To check they’re done, insert a meat thermometer into the center of the largest meatball. It should read 160°F for pork sausage (165°F for poultry sausage). If they’re not quite there yet, give them another minute or two. Don’t rely on the outside color alone!

Cook the pasta: Once the meatballs are cooked through, add ½ cup small pasta to the boiling soup. I’m using pearl couscous here, but acini di pepe, orzo, ditalini, or pastina also work. Continue cooking for about 6 minutes, or until the pasta has plumped up and is tender when you bite into it. Different pasta shapes may need a little more or less time, so tasting a piece is your best bet.

Add the spinach: Stir 4 cups fresh spinach into the soup and let it wilt for about a minute. It might look like a mountain of spinach at first, but trust me, it’ll shrink down in no time. The leaves should turn a deeper green and soften into the broth.

Taste and serve: Give the soup a final taste and adjust the salt or other seasonings as needed. You should have tender mini meatballs, soft vegetables, plump little pasta shapes, and wilted spinach in a delicious, savory broth. Serve hot and enjoy every cozy and hearty spoonful!

Storing & Reheating

One thing to keep in mind with this soup is the pasta keeps absorbing liquid as it sits. So your leftovers will likely look thicker the next day. They’ll still taste delicious, but I recommend cooking the pasta separately if you’re making this soup specifically for meal prep or freezing.

Fridge

Store leftovers in airtight containers in the refrigerator for up to 3-4 days. Divide large batches into smaller, shallow containers so they cool quickly, and refrigerate within two hours of cooking. For the best texture, store the cooked pasta separately from the broth, vegetables, and meatballs. If you’ve already mixed everything together, that’s absolutely fine! Just expect the pasta to soften and soak up some of the broth as it sits.

Reheating

Reheat the soup in a saucepan over medium-low heat, stirring occasionally, until it’s steaming hot throughout and the meatballs reach 165°F. I like to add a splash of extra chicken broth or water if the pasta has absorbed some of the liquid. Start with a small amount, stir, and add more as needed.

You can also microwave individual portions. Heat in short intervals, stirring between each one so the meatballs and broth warm evenly. If you stored the pasta separately, add it toward the end of reheating so it doesn’t become too soft.

Freezing

Italian wedding soup is a great freezer meal! Here’s how I recommend freezing it for the best results:

  • Freeze without the pasta: Freeze the cooked soup with the meatballs, vegetables, and broth, then prepare fresh pasta when you’re ready to eat. The broth-based soup freezes well for up to 3 months. Divide the cooled soup into freezer-safe containers or bags and leave a little room for expansion. I find smaller portions especially handy for an easy lunch.
  • Thaw and reheat: Thaw overnight in the refrigerator or reheat directly from frozen, stirring as it thaws and warms through. Add freshly cooked pasta before serving, and a handful of fresh spinach if you’d like to brighten it up.
  • Already added the pasta? You can still freeze the finished soup! The pasta will just be softer after thawing and reheating, and you may need to add a little extra broth to loosen everything up.

The Apple Cake I Grew Up Baking

Growing up, my mom, dad, sister, and I would go apple picking in Massachusetts every fall. After wandering through the orchards, we’d bake this cake together, filling the house with the warm, rich scent of cinnamon and nutmeg. We’d even pack a few slices to enjoy on the road during our annual October trip to Salem!

Besides the triple hit of apple flavor from fresh diced apples, applesauce, and apple juice, the moisture in this cake is a solid 10/10! Recipe Success Tips

  1. Measure the apples after chopping. I’ve been making this cake for YEARS now, and one thing I don’t leave to chance is how much apple goes into the batter! I use a mix of Granny Smith and Honeycrisp (or Fuji) apples because I like having both tart and sweet flavors in the cake. But apples vary in size, so the important thing is to have 4 cups of peeled, diced apples. Keep the pieces fairly uniform, about 1-2 cm, so they soften evenly as the cake bakes. With this much fruit, you don’t want oversized chunks or extra apples throwing off the texture.
  2. Use the spice mix you’ve already got. I make my own apple spice mix with cinnamon, nutmeg, allspice, and ginger because I like how the flavors balance each other. But there’s no need to buy four separate spices if you already have apple pie spice or pumpkin pie spice in your pantry! You can try using 2 tablespoons of premixed spice in place of my homemade blend. Just know that the flavor may be slightly different, especially if your mix contains cloves.
  3. Mix the batter gently once the flour goes in. Add the dry ingredients a little at a time, mixing on low until they’re fully incorporated. Scrape down the sides and bottom of the bowl to catch any hidden flour, then fold in the diced apples by hand. Overmixing at this stage can make the cake tougher instead of soft and tender.
  4. Don’t let the apples fool your toothpick test! This cake is packed with fresh apple pieces, so it’s easy to accidentally poke a piece of fruit when checking for doneness. Start checking around the 45-minute mark, inserting a toothpick into the cake near the center. If you hit an apple (you might see some soft apple pulp on the toothpick rather than wet cake batter), try another spot. Look for a set center and a toothpick that comes out clean. Give it a few more minutes if underbaked and check again.
  5. Let the cake cool completely before finishing. I know this warm apple cake smells irresistible…but letting it cool helps the crumb set, making those 15 slices easier to cut and serve. Wait until it’s completely cool before adding the powdered sugar, too. Otherwise, the sugar will absorb moisture from the warm cake and disappear into the surface.

A Few Tricks for Better Brown Butter

Browning butter is easier than you might think! It’s one of my favorite tricks for adding a little extra flavor to baked goods, like my cherry blondies and this apple spice cake. But knowing when to take it off the heat makes all the difference! Here’s how I do it:

  • Use a light-colored saucepan. I recommend stainless steel or another light-colored pan so you can actually see the butter changing color. Dark pans make it much harder to spot when the brown butter is ready.
  • Watch for three stages. First, the butter melts and starts bubbling. Next, a layer of white foam forms as the water cooks off. Finally, the foam begins to subside, and the milk solids at the bottom turn golden brown. That’s when I start paying extra attention!
  • Keep the butter moving. Give the pan a gentle swirl every so often, especially as you prepare the apples. This helps the milk solids brown more evenly and prevents them from sitting too long against the hot bottom of the pan.
  • Trust your nose AND your eyes. After about 5-10 minutes over medium heat, the butter should smell toasted and nutty, with golden-brown flecks throughout. Remove it from the heat as soon as it reaches this stage and swirl occasionally as it cools. Don’t strain out those little brown flecks, either. They’re where all the brown butter flavor is!

Granny Smith apples are tart and firm, which helps balance the sweetness of the cake and keeps the pieces from turning mushy. Honeycrisp and Fuji add a sweeter apple flavor and also hold their shape well during baking. Pink Lady or Braeburn can replace Granny Smith, and Gala or Golden Delicious can replace Honeycrisp or Fuji, although the flavor and texture may vary slightly. My preferred combination is still Granny Smith with Honeycrisp or Fuji! Why this cake works: Pure butter cakes can dry out faster, while pure oil cakes lack that rich, buttery flavor. I use a combination of browned butter and neutral oil for the best of both worlds: rich flavor and long-lasting moisture. Then there’s my triple moisture boost! Make-ahead apple prep tip: This apple spice cake stores really well once baked, but if you want to get ahead on the prep, peeling and chopping four apples is one of the more time-consuming parts. My favorite method for preventing apples from browning is the honey water soak (best for taste!)
  • Ratio: Dissolve 2 tablespoons of honey in 1 cup of room-temperature water (increase the amounts as needed).

This can help keep your prepared apples from browning for several days. I always save the honey apple water to make tea!

how to make Apple Spice Cake step-by-step photos

Gather your apple spice mix ingredients: You’ll need 4 tsp ground cinnamon, 1 tsp ground nutmeg, ½ tsp ground allspice, and 1 tsp ground ginger.

Gather your cake ingredients and preheat the oven to 350°F: You’ll need 2½ cups all-purpose flour, 1 tsp baking powder, 1 tsp baking soda, 1 tsp fine salt, 2 Tbsp apple spice mix (as below), 1 stick unsalted butter, ½ cup neutral oil (canola or vegetable), ½ cup granulated sugar, 1 cup packed light brown sugar, ½ cup unsweetened applesauce, ¼ cup apple juice or cider, 3 large eggs (room temperature), 2 tsp vanilla extract, 2 Granny Smith apples, 2 Honeycrisp or Fuji apples, and 1 Tbsp powdered sugar.

Start browning the butter: Add 1 stick of unsalted butter to a saucepan over medium heat and allow it to melt. Keep an eye on it as it begins to bubble and foam. You’ll continue browning the butter as you prepare the apples, so don’t wander too far from the stove! (If the butter finishes browning before you’ve finished prepping the apples, take it off the heat straight away so it doesn’t burn.)

Peel and core the apples: Peel and remove the cores from 2 Granny Smith apples and 2 Honeycrisp or Fuji apples.

Dice the apples: Cut the peeled apples into small, fairly uniform pieces, about 1-2 cm in size. You should have approximately 4 cups of diced apples altogether. While you’re chopping, keep checking the butter and swirl the pan occasionally to keep the milk solids from burning.

Check on the butter: This is what your butter will look like at the halfway point, a bit foamy on top and the butter solids will start to turn golden yellow. Keep a close eye on it as the color develops because it can go from golden to burnt surprisingly quickly.

Combine the dry ingredients: In a large mixing bowl, whisk together 2½ cups all-purpose flour, 1 tsp baking powder, 1 tsp baking soda, 1 tsp fine salt, and the ingredients for the apple spice mix (4 tsp ground cinnamon, 1 tsp ground nutmeg, ½ tsp ground allspice, and 1 tsp ground ginger). Make sure the spices are evenly distributed throughout the flour so you don’t end up with concentrated pockets of cinnamon or other spices in the finished cake.

Finish browning the butter: After approximately 5-10 minutes over medium heat, the butter should be golden brown, with browned milk solids at the bottom and the foam on top beginning to subside. Remove from the heat and let cool for a few minutes, swirling occasionally to release heat and prevent the residual heat from burning the solids.

Mix the wet ingredients: In a separate large bowl, combine 3 large eggs, ½ cup neutral oil, ½ cup unsweetened applesauce, ¼ cup apple juice or cider, and 2 tsp vanilla extract. Add 1 cup packed light brown sugar and ½ cup granulated sugar, followed by the cooled browned butter. Mix until combined.

Combine the wet and dry mixtures: Gradually add the flour mixture to the wet ingredients, working in small additions. Use a hand mixer on low speed to blend everything together. Give the sides and bottom of the bowl a quick scrape to catch any flour that hasn’t been incorporated, but avoid mixing longer than necessary.

Fold in the apples: Add all 4 cups of peeled, diced apples to the batter. Gently fold them in with a spatula until they’re evenly distributed. I switch to a spatula for this step so the apple pieces stay intact and I don’t overwork the batter.

Prepare your baking pan: Lightly grease a 9×13-inch baking pan with butter or nonstick cooking spray, making sure to coat the corners and sides. This makes it easier to get the apple spice cake out once baked.

Transfer the batter to the pan: Pour the spiced apple cake batter into the prepared 9×13-inch pan. Use a spatula to spread it into an even layer.

Bake the cake: Place the pan in the preheated 350°F oven and bake for approximately 45 minutes, or until a toothpick inserted near the center comes out clean. Be careful not to mistake moisture from a piece of apple for uncooked batter! If you see wet cake batter, continue baking for a few more minutes before checking again. The cake will be deeply browned and set in the center once done.

Cool, dust, and serve! Allow the cake to cool completely, then dust the top evenly with 1 Tbsp powdered sugar. Waiting until it’s cool helps keep the powdered sugar from disappearing into the surface.

Slice into 15 pieces and enjoy!

How to Serve Apple Spice Cake

I mean, it’s cake after all, so you really can’t go wrong serving it however you like! I think it’s delicious as-is, with a simple dusting of powdered sugar and a hot cup of coffee for an afternoon treat. But if I’m serving it for dessert, I’d warm up individual slices and add a scoop of vanilla ice cream. A drizzle of our easy caramel sauce would be delicious, too! And since this recipe makes 15 generous slices, it’s great for fall gatherings, Thanksgiving celebrations, or potlucks. You can even serve it straight from the pan without any extra fuss.

Storing Leftovers

Store your apple spice cake covered tightly or in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to 4 days. You can store it whole or cut it into individual slices for easy snacking! Let refrigerated slices come to room temperature before serving to soften the crumb. Or if you want a warm slice to serve with ice cream, microwave it for about 10-15 seconds before serving.

Freezing

Wrap individual slices (once completely cooled) tightly in plastic wrap, then place them in a freezer-safe bag or container. Freeze for up to 3 months for the best quality. Thaw in the refrigerator, then bring to room temperature or gently warm before serving. Save the powdered sugar for after thawing!
