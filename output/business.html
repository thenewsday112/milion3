Publisher’s Platform: Radical transparency? What I am asking the FDA to do Is not radical at all

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has rightly made “radical transparency” the motto of his Department of Health and Human Services. HHS even has a page by that name. I think it is a good motto, and I want to help Secretary Kennedy and Acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas deliver on it in food safety, with two citizen petitions that ask for nothing the federal government has not already done, has not already said it believes in, and, as of Oct. 26, has not been told by the Department of Justice that it has the authority to do. These petitions are meant as a hand up.

Before getting to the petitions, it is worth looking at where the phrase came from and how it found its way into food safety, because HHS and FDA put it there themselves.

Where “radical transparency” came from

The phrase did not start in Washington. It is a management idea popularized by Bridgewater Associates founder Ray Dalio, who built his hedge fund around what he called radical truth and radical transparency and pitched it in a widely watched 2017 TED talk. Dalio’s own summary of the business case is worth keeping in mind for what follows: if you are handling things well, radical transparency will make that clear, and if you are handling things badly, it will make that clear too.

Kennedy made it his own at his confirmation hearings. Before the Senate Finance Committee on Jan. 29, 2025, he promised “radical transparency” within the department. Answering Senator Grassley, he said that if Congress wanted information, the doors are open. And he added a line every outbreak investigator should tape to the wall: “We should tell Americans when we don’t know.”

Three weeks later, in his first address to HHS staff on Feb. 18, 2025, he gave the idea its philosophical core: “Only through radical transparency can we provide Americans with genuine informed consent, which is the bedrock and the foundation stone of democracy.”

At the start, the phrase was mostly about Congress, conflicts of interest and vaccines. It did not stay there.

How it moved into food, then into outbreaks and recalls

On March 10, 2025, HHS directed FDA to explore closing the self-affirmed GRAS pathway, and the press release said HHS and FDA “are committed to promoting radical transparency to make sure all Americans know what is in their food.” That is the first time I can find the phrase attached to food.

Marty Makary, then FDA’s incoming commissioner, brought it with him. In his confirmation testimony he described a generational opportunity to usher in radical transparency. Then, on July 9, 2025, FDA sent a letter to food industry leaders about recall communications that said this: “At the FDA, we believe that radical transparency in food safety and nutrition actions is key to reducing foodborne illness and chronic disease.” Read that again. Foodborne illness. Recalls. Radical transparency. FDA’s words, not mine.

The next day, FDA published more than 200 drug rejection letters under the headline “FDA Embraces Radical Transparency”, and in September it announced it would release them in real time, calling radical transparency “one of the guiding principles of this administration.” Those letters are redacted for confidential commercial information, but, in FDA’s words, they “will contain company names.” Hold that thought.

On Sept. 24, 2025, FDA’s food program launched Executive Incident Summary abstracts for closed outbreak investigations. The constituent update opened: “The FDA is reinforcing our commitment to radical transparency to ensure Americans know what is in their food and that it is safe to consume.” Both the recall letter and the abstracts came out of FDA’s food program, led at the time by Deputy Commissioner for Food Kyle Diamantas. By then the phrase had become the administration’s brand more broadly; that same month the Senate HELP Committee held a hearing titled “Restoring Trust Through Radical Transparency.”

The people have changed since then; the commitments have not. Makary resigned in May 2026, and Diamantas, the same official whose food program wrote radical transparency into the recall letter and the outbreak abstracts, has been acting commissioner ever since. In August, President Trump nominated Dr. Heidi Overton to be the next commissioner. The commitments the agency put in writing are still there for its leaders to build on, and that continuity is an asset.

For my part, the place to start is food safety, where HHS and FDA have already laid a strong foundation in their own words.

The unfinished part

The outbreak abstracts were a real step forward, and FDA deserves credit for them. The one piece still missing is the piece the public most needs: who and where. Food Safety News reported that FDA has withheld company names in its mango outbreak summary, and Food Safety Magazine described a summary of a fatal Listeria outbreak in which FDA redacted all key details, down to the states where the patients lived. When Stop Foodborne Illness petitioned FDA in February to stop treating company names as confidential, invoking radical transparency, FDA answered that the names are protected by the Trade Secrets Act. That was an honest statement of the legal question the agency believed it faced, and, as I explain below, that question now has an answer.

The cost of that gap shows up in real outbreaks. In September FDA closed a romaine E. coli investigation and named no grower, no processor and no brand. In October, in a 50-person E. coli O157:H7 outbreak linked to chicken wraps, the public learned the brand from a lawsuit and a newspaper, not from FDA or FSIS – although FSIS later confirmed the source to a reporter (I represent plaintiffs in that outbreak). However, when FDA declared that outbreak over on Oct. 28, its entry still did not name the brand, the maker or the states, and it explained that more detailed product information would not have given consumers an action they could take to prevent illness. I understand the reasoning, but it assumes the only reason to name a food is so people can throw it out. As I explain below, that is not the only consumer who needs to know.

Not who might have been the source, but who was

I want to be clear about one thing, because it is the heart of both petitions. I am not asking FDA to name every firm that might have been the source of an outbreak, or to publish working hypotheses while an investigation is still sorting out the facts. Everyone remembers the 2008 tomato warning that turned out to be peppers. I am asking FDA to name the firm its own investigation found actually was the source.

The 2024 romaine outbreak is the clearest example I know. That fall, an E. coli O157:H7 outbreak linked to romaine sickened 89 people in 15 states, hospitalized 36, caused seven cases of hemolytic uremic syndrome and killed one person. FDA closed the investigation with the word romaine and nothing more. When the traceback summary was first released, the processor, the grower and the ranch were blacked out. When it was later released without redactions, only after firms in the supply chain signed authorizations consenting to the release of their own information, the same document showed that the traceback had converged on one processor and one grower. There was no uncertainty to protect. The government had the answer the whole time, and the public learned it through records requests more than a year later, after the season, the product and any chance to act had passed.

That is the line the petitions draw: disclosure of what FDA has confirmed, through the same traceback and laboratory evidence it relies on to close an investigation, and not of what it merely suspects.

The two petitions

That is why I am filing two citizen petitions together.

The first, on outbreak firm disclosure, asks FDA to amend its regulations to say that the name and location of a firm is not confidential commercial information when epidemiologic evidence implicates a food in an outbreak and traceback or laboratory evidence, including whole genome sequencing, identifies the firm as the source of the contaminated food. That is the same evidence FDA already uses to close an investigation, and it is not a suspect list. It asks FDA to disclose implicated growers, processors and distributors whether or not there is a recall, and to finish the transparency initiative it started by publishing those abstracts without redacting firm names.

The second, on recall consignee lists, asks FDA to publish the list of retail stores that received a recalled food in every Class I recall (the kind that can cause serious illness or death) and to classify every food recall, and publish the classification, within a set number of days.

That is it. Neither petition asks FDA to reveal a recipe, a process, a customer price or anything else that is a genuine trade secret. They ask for who and where.

Why none of this is radical

Start with USDA. For meat and poultry, FSIS has published retail consignee lists in Class I recalls under a final rule adopted in July 2008, during the Bush administration. A consumer who bought recalled ground beef can find out whether their store got it. A consumer who bought recalled cheese, sprouts or bagged salad usually cannot. Eighteen years on, the beef and poultry industries are still standing.

Congress noticed the gap. In the Food Safety Modernization Act of 2011, it told FDA, in its new mandatory recall provision, to consider USDA’s consignee policy. FDA did and wrote a guidance document saying it would publish consignee lists in some cases.

FDA itself has named farms, processors and brands in the same circumstances it faces today, at least eight times since 2006. States do it routinely.

And then there are those drug rejection letters. FDA has already decided, under the banner of radical transparency, that a drug company’s name is not the confidential part of a letter rejecting its application. Applying that same principle to food would bring the agency’s two halves into line: if a pharmaceutical company’s name can be published with a failed application, the name of a farm that whole genome sequencing has tied to children on dialysis can be too.

None of this is radical. It is a proven practice waiting to be adopted.

Good for consumers

Kennedy said radical transparency is how Americans get “genuine informed consent.” You cannot consent to anything about a food when the government knows who made it and will not say. A named firm and a published store list let a family check their refrigerator, let a doctor connect a patient’s illness to a source, and let a health department find cases it would otherwise miss. Over time, names build a track record, so consumers can tell which companies keep turning up in outbreak after outbreak.

Gross deception of the consumer, and who the consumer is

Section 705(b) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act lets FDA share information about food in situations involving, in the Commissioner’s opinion, imminent danger to health or gross deception of the consumer. Most of the conversation about outbreak disclosure has focused on the first of those, imminent danger. The second deserves just as much attention, and I think it is the more natural fit for what these petitions ask.

Think about who the consumer is. It is the mom and dad in the grocery aisle trying to buy what is safe for their kids. It is also the buyer for Walmart or Taco Bell, or for a school district or a hospital, deciding which supplier to keep and which to drop. Every one of them makes that decision on the information available. When FDA has confirmed which company’s food made people sick and that fact stays in the file, each of those decisions is made on a record the government knows is materially incomplete. The parents cannot steer around a brand, and the buyer cannot weigh a supplier’s record, that FDA has already identified.

Deception does not have to be a false statement; an omission can mislead just as surely. A commodity-only advisory that leaves shoppers blaming every grower of a crop, a firm publicly denying it was the source while the agency’s traceback converged on it, or an outbreak table that still lists a food as “suspect” after a federal agency has confirmed it to the press each leaves consumers with a false picture that FDA holds the record to correct. And unlike imminent danger, deception does not end when people stop getting sick. The parent and the buyer are still making choices next season.

The limiting principle is FDA’s own knowledge. This ground reaches what the agency has confirmed, not its working hypotheses, which is exactly the line the petitions draw. I wrote about this in more detail when FDA closed the chicken wrap outbreak, and I have revised both petitions to rest on it.

Good for business

This is the part the industry should hear most clearly. When the government names a commodity and stops, consumers avoid the whole commodity. During this summer’s Cyclospora outbreak, Numerator data reported by Grocery Dive showed that 6.5 million households stopped buying salad mixes and kits in a single month. That cost fell on every grower and processor in the category, including the ones who did everything right. In 2008, tomato growers lost a season to a Salmonella outbreak that turned out to be peppers. Anonymity does not prevent harm; it spreads one firm’s harm across an entire industry.

A name is the only thing that separates the firm that caused an outbreak from the competitors who did not. It lets a buyer at a national chain weigh a supplier’s history the way a parent weighs a brand, lets insurers price risk and gives every firm a reason to find its problem before next season. Ray Dalio would recognize the logic: if you are running a safe operation, transparency makes that clear.

Good for HHS and FDA

The agencies promised to rebuild public trust. Few things would build trust faster than the public hearing the name of an implicated firm from FDA first, rather than from a court filing or a reporter. A clear rule also gets FDA out of the business of making ad hoc, case-by-case judgments that look inconsistent from the outside, and it makes the food program consistent with what FDA is already doing for drugs. It lets the agency finish an initiative it launched itself. And it is a concrete, announceable win: no new statute, no new appropriation, a rule FDA can write on its own.

And now DOJ says FDA has the authority

The sticking point for FDA has been legal, and it has been a fair one: the agency has said the Trade Secrets Act ties its hands. On Oct. 26, 2026, the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel answered that argument in an opinion addressed to HHS.

OLC agreed that FDA’s current regulations generally do not authorize disclosure of supply chain information, so the Trade Secrets Act largely forbids it, and that the existing route through 21 CFR 20.91 is exceedingly narrow in practice. But it concluded that section 705(b) of the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, together with FDA’s rulemaking authority in section 701(a), permits the agency to amend its regulations to authorize disclosure of the names and locations of firms in a supply chain in situations involving imminent danger to health or gross deception of the consumer. Disclosure under such a regulation, OLC said, would be authorized by law and would not violate the Trade Secrets Act.

OLC also drew a line: a section 705(b) regulation authorizing disclosure after an outbreak has already ended would likely be inconsistent with the statute. That is imminent-danger logic, since an outbreak’s danger ends. But OLC treated the two statutory conditions as independent, allowing a regulation that permits disclosure when either one is met, and deception of the consumer does not end with the last illness. OLC did not decide whether its timing limit reaches the deception ground, and I have said so in the petition. The petitions also rest on a separate rule that a firm’s name and location simply is not confidential commercial information in the first place, so FDA has more than one sound path.

In other words, the obstacle has never been the statute. It has been FDA’s own regulations, and the Department’s lawyers have now told HHS that FDA can change them. The petitions ask it to.

Secretary Kennedy, Acting Commissioner Diamantas, Dr. Overton

HHS and FDA have already done the hard part: they made the commitment, and they put it in writing for food, in the GRAS announcement, in the recall letter to industry and in the launch of the outbreak abstracts. Mr. Diamantas, those last two came from the food program you ran. These petitions offer a way to complete that work and put your name on the result. You are also a lawyer, the first to lead the agency, and you will recognize that the OLC opinion clears the legal path the agency has long said it needed. Dr. Overton, if you are confirmed, this would be a meaningful early win to carry forward. And none of it requires anything radical. It is ordinary transparency, the kind USDA has practiced since 2008, that FDA has used at least eight times, that Congress asked you to consider in 2011, that you already apply to drug companies, and that the Justice Department says you have the authority to adopt.

Secretary Kennedy said we should tell Americans when we don’t know. These petitions ask for the other half of that promise, telling them when we do. I hope HHS and FDA will take it, and I am glad to help however I can.

‘The Gilded Age’ Is Back! Here’s What to Eat While You Watch the Show

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I can be persuaded to watch almost anything that promises extravagant set designs and beautiful costumes. If there’s history and a little mischievous social maneuvering involved, even better. My coworkers recently recommended The Gilded Age, and within two weeks, I’d watched all three seasons just in time for season four to drop.

There’s plenty to admire: Bertha Russell’s determination, Peggy Scott’s writing career and rooms furnished with a reassuring disregard for minimalism. I love the history, too, from the railroad fortunes and New York bankers to Lewis Latimer’s work in electric lighting and the woman who helped bring the Brooklyn Bridge to completion. Every episode gave me another reason to keep watching.

My husband is often still at the restaurant in the evenings, so once the kids are asleep, I have a few hours to settle in with a show. While I watched The Gilded Age, I usually poured a glass of wine (some of my favorites are under $25 at Walmart) and enjoyed the fire as Christine Baranski made an art of Agnes’s disapproval. But with the new season arriving on Nov. 1, I’m borrowing a few ideas from the show to make a watch party spread.

Set the Scene

I have a decidedly romantic streak when it comes to my surroundings, so the show’s interiors have my full attention. I’m particularly taken with the glassware and china that can make even a small supper feel indulgent. With a few soft lamps and the fire going, I’m looking forward to borrowing a little of that grandeur for my own evening.

  • A favorite plate and a special glass: Bannister may have reservations about Bertha’s colored glasses, but as a collector of vintage glassware, I find it rather inspiring. Thrift stores are wonderful places to look for floral china, small silver-plated trays and individual glasses to mix and match for a pretty tablescape.
  • A few flowers on the table: A handful of stems from the grocery store in a little pitcher is plenty. They’re equally charming when arranged in tiny bud vases or in one center arrangement. Finding flowers that mirror the colors in your china is a great way to make everything look put together.
  • Something soft beneath the dishes: Because so many watch parties happen in the living room rather than the kitchen, cover your coffee table with a tablecloth. It’ll look pretty, and it’ll catch any crumbs before they end up in the carpet. Add even more Gilded Age elegance with cloth napkins under silverware rather than paper napkins.
  • A little note for company: A handwritten card listing dishes like “Ada’s Clam Chowder” would be a nice addition if you have company for your watch party. Cream card stock and a nice pen are enough to give the food a thoughtful connection to the show.
  • Music before the show: A little opera while crafting vintage cocktails would be perfect for setting the scene, though I’d leave the battle over opera boxes to Mrs. Astor and Bertha.

A Glass for Agnes—or Ada

I’m more than happy to borrow the show’s habit of pouring wine into crystal glasses. Mine usually contains Champagne, but Ada’s season-three temperance campaign gives us a reason to put some thought into an alcohol-free option too.

Agnes’s feelings about wine appear to be settled, but there’s room for both sisters.

  • Champagne or sparkling wine: I’d choose a bottle I enjoy and pour it into a favorite glass. There’s simply no need to improve on a good Champagne.
  • A Champagne cocktail: Brandy and bitters give a Champagne cocktail a little extra ceremony, which is something perfectly befitting of a Gilded Age watch party.
  • An alcohol-free spritz: Sparkling water with cranberry juice and an orange twist would be my contribution to Ada’s cause, but any mocktail would make an equally good toast to the evening. I’d use the same glassware and leave the pledge unsigned.
  • Tea: Some evenings call for a pot of black tea and a little milk. I like bringing the pot into the living room on a tray.

Small Bites for High Society

The Russells may require a meticulously planned succession of courses, but I’d be content with a small selection of savory bites. Oysters, warm crab dip or little sandwiches would make a perfectly elegant dinner by the fire.

  • Oysters: Oysters make frequent appearances at the show’s lavish dinners, and a few with lemon would be enough to make the evening feel special. Our Oysters Rockefeller are baked with butter, spinach and Romano. And with John D. Rockefeller reportedly arriving this season, his namesake oysters certainly deserve a place at the table.
  • Crab: Marian’s visit to the Scotts is difficult to forget, though I’d rather take inspiration from their crab luncheon than her assumptions. A lovely crab meat au gratin would bring some of that indulgence home, served warm with toasted bread or crackers.
  • Asparagus: Few vegetables receive such careful instruction on how to eat them. After Bannister explains the difference between English and American asparagus etiquette, the Russell kitchen decided to avoid the matter altogether. I’d serve our roasted asparagus anyway, with a squeeze of lemon, and trust everyone to choose their own utensils.
  • Tea sandwiches: Mamie Fish’s doll-themed tea party is more commitment than I’m looking for, but I can appreciate the sandwiches. Cucumber and cream cheese, butter with thinly sliced radish or chicken salad would all be good on soft bread.

Dinner Across Fifth Avenue

For something more substantial, you can look to the show’s luncheons as much as its elaborate dinner parties. A bowl of soup or a sandwich can feel quite grand when the ingredients are good and someone else is responsible for the evening’s drama.

  • Clam chowder: A bowl of New England clam chowder is enough to win over Reverend Forte though it does little to impress Agnes. I’m with Ada on this one. Our New England clam chowder has tender potatoes, clams and a creamy broth that sounds perfect for a November season premiere.
  • Lobster rolls: The lobster left behind after Bertha’s disastrous first reception deserves a more appreciative audience. Our lobster rolls put that idea into buttery toasted buns, making them much more convenient for my own seating arrangements.
  • Steak: George’s Arizona business trip includes a steak order in a saloon, a considerable change of scenery from the Russell dining room. A steak seared in a hot skillet and finished with butter and fresh herbs would be a fitting way to bring a little of the show’s indulgence home.
  • Salmon: The exquisite salmon platters at Bertha’s dinners are hard to overlook, even among all the other beautiful dishes. For an easy at-home version, borrow the fish and simplify the presentation: a grilled fillet with lemon and garlic would be perfect for any cozy viewing party.

Dessert in the Drawing Room

A pot of tea and something sweet would be enough for me on an evening when dinner is already behind us. I like having dessert ready before I sit down, whether that means a trifle waiting in the fridge or a few pastries brought home in a paper bag.

  • Trifle: Bannister’s assessment of trifle as a nursery food seems unnecessarily limiting. Layers of cake, cream and fruit have plenty to offer the rest of us. Our strawberry trifle would be a good place to start. A splash of sherry over the cake would move it a little further from the nursery.
  • French pastries: Aurora Fane’s gathering put me in the mood for a plate of French pastries. Buy a few macarons, perhaps something chocolate, and let the bakery determine how ambitious the evening becomes. Arrange the pastries on a pretty plate, and voila!
  • Apple pie: For all the elaborate food in the show, apple pie is an appealing return to something familiar. Armstrong’s visit to her mother casts it in an unhappy light, but I’m prepared to overlook that for a warm slice with a buttery crust.
  • Ice cream: Ice cream has Agnes’s approval, which is no small recommendation. Strawberry would be a fitting choice from the period. A scoop in a small glass bowl with a crisp cookie would be all the dessert I’d need.

Before the first kitchen timer goes off, international trade duties have already driven up the cost of your holiday dinner. Between a historic shortage in American cattle herds raising beef prices sky-high, tariffs on tinplate steel inflating the price of canned goods, and foreign trade duties hitting European cheeses, wines, and sweets, hosting a holiday meal in 2026 can feel like walking a tightrope. I’ve been seeing these price hikes firsthand while shopping, and it’s definitely made me rethink a few of my usual grocery picks.

And while we can’t control international trade policies, we can outsmart them at the grocery store!

Your Guide to a Budget-Friendly Holiday Feast

Hosting family shouldn’t mean taking on a mountain of stress or spending your whole paycheck on dinner. With a few smart, budget-savvy pivots, I’m here to help you put together a celebratory feast that tastes incredible, looks festive, and sidesteps the latest markups on the shelves. Alongside my favorite budget-saving swaps and shopping tips, we’ve also put together a complete holiday menu that feeds eight people for around $45!

TL;DR: The 4 Smartest Swaps for a Tariff-Proof Holiday Table

Instead of buying the exact same grocery list you’ve used over the holidays for years, we found making these four simple adjustments will help keep your receipt total down:

  1. Ditch the canned goods: Tariffs on tinplate steel and imported aluminum have turned these former budget heroes into surprise budget busters. Buying canned pumpkin puree, canned green beans, canned yams, and canned cranberry sauce means you’re paying a hefty markup just for the packaging. Stick to fresh, bagged, or frozen U.S. produce instead. It’s cheaper, avoids the packaging surcharge, and often tastes noticeably better.
  2. Rethink the centerpiece protein: Avoid expensive beef roasts and foreign seafood this holiday season, both of which are carrying markups right now. Focus on domestic whole turkeys, bone-in turkey breasts, pork roasts, or glazed holiday hams. These proteins rely on local agricultural supply chains and are largely insulated from foreign trade taxes. We found whole turkey for as little as $0.85 per pound at Walmart, making it a fantastic budget-friendly centerpiece!
  3. Keep your dairy and drinks domestic: European cheeses like French Brie and Italian Parmigiano-Reggiano, along with wine and imported European butters, are carrying heavy import taxes. The U.S. produces both dairy and wine, so if you’re avoiding tariffs, they make a great swap. Switch to Wisconsin aged cheddar, Vermont goat cheese, domestic sweet cream butter, and West Coast wines.
  4. Focus on seasonal fruit, grain, and spice desserts: High tariffs have sent the price of specialty chocolates soaring, but we’re not giving up dessert! Lean into fruit-forward desserts like a pear galette or an apple crumble. You could also try an egg-rich chess pie, or any number of bread-based desserts. Other great options include sweet potato pie or pecan pie using pantry grains and domestic sweeteners.

“Kicking the Can” at Thanksgiving

A stress-free Thanksgiving dinner often relies on convenience canned products like jellied cranberry sauce, condensed cream-of-mushroom soup bases, and pumpkin puree. But in 2026, paying a premium for steel packaging is the quickest way to run up your total at the register.

Aside from straight facts about how tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs are impacting our grocery carts this holiday season, let’s talk about yield, which applies to pre (and post–let’s hope!) tariff times, too. For example, if you want to give 8 people an honest half-cup portion of carrots, you cannot rely on the canned carrot’s label touting that 3.5 servings are ready to be enjoyed inside. You’d need at least 4 cans, yielding roughly 5 to 6 cups total; at $0.96 per can, that’s $3.84, and over a third of what you bought was salt water and packaging.

On the flip side, a 4 lb. bag of raw carrots yields a solid 14 to 16 generous side servings. Even after peeling and trimming the tips (about a 10% prep loss), you are left with roughly 57 to 58 oz of pure edible veg. At $4.77 for the 4 lb bag, you feed twice as many guests for roughly 40% less out of pocket than buying the 6 to 7 cans needed to match that yield, paying around 8.3 cents per edible ounce compared to over 13 cents for canned solids, while skipping the canned brine (and tinplate steel and aluminum tariffs) completely!

Here is how to ditch the cans and build a fresher table for less money with just a little bit of extra elbow grease:

Make a 15-Minute Fresh Cranberry Sauce with 3 Ingredients

A can of jellied cranberry sauce seems cheap until you compare its price per ounce to a simple bag of fresh, American-grown cranberries. Simmering whole cranberries on the stove with a little sugar, a splash of water, and fresh orange zest takes less than twenty minutes. It thickens naturally as it cools, costs less, and delivers a vibrant flavor that makes the canned jelly completely obsolete. Our easy three-ingredient cranberry sauce recipe (shown above) has become my go-to.

Only need a small batch of cranberry sauce? Put the rest of those fresh cranberries to good use and make my sugared cranberries. They’re a delicious, festive, budget-friendly treat that doubles as a gorgeous garnish for your turkey platter and holiday desserts.

Swap Out the Green Bean Casserole

Traditional green bean casserole often relies on canned french-cut green beans and canned cream soups…a double hit of packaging markups! Instead, grab two pounds of fresh green beans from the produce section or a couple bags of frozen from the freezer section. Sauté them in a skillet with a drizzle of olive oil or domestic butter, garlic, and a pinch of other ingredients you probably already have on hand. It’s crisp, colorful, and feels like something you’d order at a nice restaurant.

Elevate the Bread Basket

Instead of spending $8 to $12 on a small wedge of imported cheese to snack on before dinner, make the bread basket your showstopper instead. Bake a quick batch of warm homemade dinner rolls or a skillet of golden cornbread, and serve it alongside whipped domestic sweet cream butter drizzled with local honey and sea salt. They cost pennies per serving, actually fill people up, and feel deeply comforting. Short on time? Don’t feel bad about grabbing a bag of ready-made dinner rolls at the grocery store like we did this time. At only $1.52 for 12 dinner rolls, I have no regrets…just extra time to spend with my family!

Prep Ahead to Keep Your Sanity

Prepped carrots, celery, and potatoes in deli containers with cold water.

Admittedly, scratch-cooking fresh foods takes more prep than opening a row of cans with a can opener, but that doesn’t mean you need to be glued to the stove all day. Make your cranberry sauce two days in advance. Peel and chop your carrots, celery, and potatoes the day before and keep them refrigerated in water. Then on the holiday morning, you’re just assembling, roasting, and mashing, not stressing!

Christmas & Holiday Entertaining: Luxury Without the Luxury Tax

Christmas dinner and New Year’s Eve often lean toward luxury imports like Champagne, ornate cheese boards, and expensive cuts of beef and roasts. You can still create an indulgent spread without absorbing tariffs:

Publisher’s Platform: Twenty-two years of asking whether we should ban petting zoos: After Brookshire Farms, Bengtson’s and the State Fair of Texas, it is time I answered my own question

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I have been asking the same question in print since 2004. In an op-ed called “E. coli and the Fair,” I asked, “So what do we do? Banish state and county fairs? Eliminate Petting Zoos?” and answered myself in three words: “Of course not.” In 2012 the headline was “Ban Petting Zoos?” In 2015, after a toddler died following a small-town Maine fair, it was “As I have said before – is it time to ban petting zoos?” In 2019, after a two-year-old died following the San Diego County Fair, it was “Seriously, time to ban petting zoos?”

A question asked four times over twenty-two years is not really a question anymore. It is a way of not answering. This week three health departments in three states opened investigations into E. coli infections tied to animal attractions at fall events. So here is my answer.

This week, in three states

At Bengtson’s Pumpkin Farm in Homer Glen, Illinois, five people with confirmed Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC), ages 4 to 38, four of them hospitalized. The petting zoo closed on October 23; the rest of the farm stayed open, and more than 200 visitors have filled out the county’s symptom survey. At Brookshire Farms in San Luis Obispo, California, five confirmed cases and an exposure window of October 16 through 26; the farm closed itself down. And in Dallas, the county is investigating up to seven possible cases in children, some treated in intensive care, and whether they trace to the petting zoo at the State Fair of Texas. The county is not yet calling it an outbreak.

I laid out what we know about each investigation yesterday, and this morning I posted twenty-five years of outbreaks just like them and the prevention playbook that everyone already has. This post is about something else: what I have been telling fairs, legislators and parents for two decades, what came of it, and what I think we should do now.

Read in a row, those posts tell a story I am not especially proud of. I have been right about the risk for twenty-two years and mostly wrong about how much signs and guidelines would fix it.

What two decades taught me

Signs alone have never been enough – but the right sign helps. Every post on that list asked for signs. The State Fair of Texas’s old Children’s Barnyard relied on hand-sanitizing gel and signs before the fair scrapped it in the mid-2000s. A sign that says “Please wash your hands” is wallpaper. The Compendium found that awareness of the risk actually protected visitors at the 2004 North Carolina State Fair. The lesson is that a sign has to tell parents what is at stake, in plain words and in the languages families at that fair speak: animals in this area can carry E. coli, it can cause kidney failure in young children, and soap and water – not sanitizer – is how you protect them.

Guidelines are not requirements. The CDC issued recommendations in 2001. State public health veterinarians have published the Compendium since 2005. As I wrote this morning, there is still no federal law on disease risk where animals and the public mix, and most states rely on voluntary guidance. Twenty-five years of outbreaks is the result of voluntary.

Laws work only as well as their weakest line. North Carolina did the most after its 2004 outbreak – Aedin’s Law, a permit and inspection for every animal exhibition at a sanctioned fair, and detailed rules that put soap-and-water stations within ten feet of exhibit exits and kept sanitizer dispensers out of the transition areas. It still had outbreaks in 2011 and 2012. Washington’s rule lets operators substitute an “alternative hand sanitizing method” approved by the local health officer. That is the loophole the Arizona State Fair walked through in 2025, and the Big Fresno Fair walked through in 2005.

The market is already banning petting zoos – one sick child at a time. Texas scrapped its fair petting zoo when insurers raised rates. By 2015, North Carolina Health News was reporting that petting zoos were becoming a thing of the past at the N.C. State Fair. After last year, the Arizona State Fair dropped its petting zoo for 2026 and the Maricopa County Fair dropped direct animal contact. Each of those decisions came after children were hurt. A ban by lawsuit and insurance premium is the most expensive, least predictable ban there is.

I was wrong about testing. In 2004 and again in 2015 I suggested testing animals before they arrive. The veterinarians have persuaded me that a negative test means little: these animals shed intermittently, and in San Diego in 2019 every animal and environmental sample came back negative after a child had died. Prevention has to assume every goat, calf and lamb is positive.

So – should we ban petting zoos?

Not all of them. I said in 2015 that petting zoos were not going to be banned and perhaps should not be, and I still believe a child who learns where food comes from by standing next to a cow is better off for it. But “not all of them” is not “none of them.” Here are the bans I now support:

1.     No direct contact for children under five with the highest-risk animals. Preweaned calves, lambs and goat kids, and animals that have recently given birth, are exactly what draws toddlers and exactly what sheds the most. The 2023 Compendium already says they are not appropriate where high-risk groups are expected. Children under five can see them over a double barrier. They should not be hand-feeding them.

2.    No animal contact anywhere that cannot provide soap and running water at the exit. No sanitizer-only petting zoos. If a pumpkin patch, a festival or a traveling operator cannot plumb a sink, the exhibit is look-don’t-touch.

3.    No permit, no petting zoo. Every operator, every location, every season – including traveling operators who work several fairs a year. The same operator name turned up at the Big Fresno Fair in 2005 and the Arizona State Fair in 2025.

The Petting Zoo Preservation Act – the short version

In 2015 I promised a Model Act. Here it is in outline, built from Pennsylvania’s law, North Carolina’s Aedin’s Law and its rules, Washington’s rule, the Arizona Public Health Association’s proposal after last year’s outbreak, and the 2023 Compendium:

4.    A state permit and pre-opening inspection for every animal-contact exhibit open to the public, renewed each season and each location.

5.    Soap, running water and paper towels within a few steps of every exit, at a height children can reach, sized for peak crowds. Sanitizer is never a substitute.

6.    A staff member at every exit whose job is to make sure hands get washed.

7.     Plain-language warning signs at every entrance and exit, in the languages local families speak, naming E. coli, kidney failure and the risk to children under five – and the same information sent home before school field trips.

8.    No food, drink, bottles, pacifiers, sippy cups or toys in animal areas; stroller parking outside; no feed sold in ice cream cones; food sold and eaten only in animal-free areas.

9.    The under-five and recently-birthed-animal rules above.

10. Veterinary health certificates, and records of where every animal came from and where it has been, available to investigators on request.

11.  Proof of liability insurance as a condition of the permit.

12.  Mandatory reporting and public notice: operators report illnesses they learn of, and when a health department links cases to a venue, it names the venue, publishes case counts, ages, hospitalizations and HUS on a schedule, and alerts local physicians. It is the same principle behind my FDA petition on outbreak firm disclosure – families cannot protect themselves from a source no one will name.

13.  Civil penalties and permit suspension for violations.

None of that is radical. Most of it has been on the books in one state or another for twenty years. It just has not been on the books in all of them, and it has rarely been enforced.

To the people who can do something about it

To legislators in Texas, Illinois and California: you have three investigations open this week. Pass the Act. To Arizona: bring back the bill that died. To fair boards and pumpkin patch owners: you do not need to wait for a law. Plumb the sinks, staff the exits, keep the bottle-fed lambs away from the toddlers, and tell parents the truth on the sign at the gate. To insurers: ask for those things before you write the policy, not after the claim.

And to parents: wash with soap and water as soon as you leave the animal area, even if your child did not touch anything. Keep food, bottles and pacifiers out. If your child develops bloody diarrhea within about ten days of a farm or fair visit, see a doctor, tell them about the visit, and watch for decreased urination, paleness and lethargy – signs of hemolytic uremic syndrome. More on staying safe around farm animals is at Fair-Safety.com.

In 2004 I wrote that ignoring these risks “makes good business for lawyers like me. But I’ll gladly give up that business if it means not having to see four-year-olds hooked up to kidney dialysis machines.” I meant it then. I mean it now. I would very much like this to be the last October I write this post.

Frequently asked questions

Has Bill Marler called for a ban on petting zoos? He has asked the question since 2004. His position now is not a blanket ban, but bans on direct contact for children under five with the highest-risk animals, on animal contact at any venue without soap and running water at the exit, and on operating without a state permit.

Is hand sanitizer enough after visiting a petting zoo? No. Soap and running water is preferred. Sanitizer does not work well on visibly dirty hands, and sanitizer-only stations were in place at the 2005 Big Fresno Fair and 2025 Arizona State Fair outbreaks.

Which states regulate petting zoos? Pennsylvania, North Carolina and Washington are among the states with rules specific to animal exhibits. Most states rely on voluntary guidance such as the Compendium, and there is no federal law on disease risk at animal exhibits.

Are the Bengtson’s, Brookshire Farms and State Fair of Texas cases connected? Nothing released so far suggests they are. Whole genome sequencing will show whether any of the isolates match.

📅 Oct 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Andrew Reiner

Mexico City is a colossal, beautifully overwhelming metropolis and an absolute paradise for those who live to eat. Across thousands of restaurants, markets, street stalls, bakeries and fondas, food from every corner of the country converges. So deciding where (and what) to eat can feel daunting.

I’ve spent over a decade writing about Mexico’s food scene, along with popping up on TV shows to talk about it occasionally. My favorite way to navigate it all is still right here on the ground with my food tours. Sidestepping the obvious spots and encouraging travelers to go deeper into the food culture here constantly reminds me of the fascinating nature of how we chilangos eat.

Andrew Reiner

To me, that means having the flexibility to move between completely different culinary worlds. We can spend lunchtime standing on a crowded sidewalk, leaning over a taco drenched in salsa and lime juice, then dress up that same evening for a multicourse dinner at a dimly lit restaurant. We’re fiercely loyal to the neighborhood places that have fed us for years and know our names, but we remain insatiably curious about the next opening or dish worth crossing town for. 

That same flexibility shapes the city’s food. CDMX knows exactly how to pull tradition into modernity without losing its spirit. These 12 spots offer a taste of the city’s versatility: dishes and bites deeply rooted in history but shaped by the wonderfully chaotic, ever-evolving way we eat today. 

Scrambled Eggs at Fonda Margarita

Adolfo Prieto 1364 B, Tlacoquemecatl del Valle, Benito Juárez
+52 55 5559 6358

Andrew Reiner

I remember being dragged out of bed early on a weekend as a teenager to have breakfast here. Although I was still drowsy, the smell of the wood-fired stoves, the café de olla (traditional “pot coffee”) bubbling away, and sounds of the older musicians playing classic tunes on their guitars were enough to bring me around. I was fully awake by the time a plate of refried beans with scrambled eggs and a stack of warm tortillas made it to the table. It’s the kind of humble combination you’d find in almost any Mexican home. At Fonda Margarita, these eggs, which are cooked over a wood fire and enriched with lard, take on a different life. Getting here early is a requirement, as they open around 6:30 a.m. and by 10 a.m., all the food is gone.

Chilaquiles at Chilpa

C. Chilpancingo 35, Colonia Condesa,
+52 55 5264 4976

Andrew Reiner

Few breakfast menus are complete without chilaquiles. What started as a way to use up day-old tortillas is now one of the country’s quintessential breakfasts: triangles of tortillas, deep-fried until crisp, then bathed in salsa. At Chilpa in leafy Condesa, several salsas are available, from a red, tomato-based version to the bright, tangy tomatillo number, all with different spice levels. Pile on your choice of cecina, shredded chicken, or fried eggs. Crema, grated cheese, and chopped onion are standard, making it easy to order a different version every visit. 

Chocolate Concha at Panadería Gala

Tonalá 143, Roma Norte

Andrew Reiner

If you asked my Mexican grandma what breakfast looks like, she’d say coffee and a pan dulce—more often than not that would mean a concha. The quintessential Mexican pastry makes an appearance in virtually every bakery in town, from the most traditional versions made with a combination of lard and shortening to the tender butter-forward renditions found in most modern bakeries. At Panadería Gala, a speakeasy-style bakery, the extra-bouncy conchas get an extra layer of indulgence when topped with a white or dark chocolate ganache quenelle.

Margarita at Despacho Margarita

Córdoba 115, Roma Norte

Andrew Reiner

The Margarita may be known worldwide as the quintessential Mexican cocktail. Legend has it that the iconic drink was first conceived in Ensenada, Baja California, and its ties to coast and borderlands are still strong. But chilangos tend to save it for specific occasions and places such as a beach vacation, a favorite cantina, or a particular restaurant where we know they’ll make a killer one. At Despacho Margarita, the classic shaken version gets a new life, alongside spicy and frozen variations. In the slushy machine there are usually two flavors: a classic tequila and lime version and a seasonal mezcal-based one that changes monthly, with unique flavors such as nopal and shiso or raspberry and cayenne pepper. 

Banderas at Cantina Covadonga

+52 55 5533 2701

Cantinas have long been places to gather over good food and several rounds of drinks. In these laid-back establishments, both are given equal importance. At Covadonga, a cantina without background music, generations mix at the tables, from the young and cool to sixty-somethings who come to play dominoes (available on request). 

Banderas have been a popular order here from the beginning: three shot glasses lined up, one with tequila, another with lime juice, and a third with sangrita, a red-hued blend of tomato and fruit juices. Together they mimic the three colors of the Mexican flag (hence the name). They’re not meant to be knocked back but rather sipped slowly, alternating between glasses.

Gorditas, quesadillas, and sopes are all on the menu, but the tlacoyo may be the most emblematic of central Mexico and Mexico City. 

Football-shaped and stuffed with your choice of fava bean purée, requesón (a soft cheese similar to ricotta), or refried beans, tlacoyos are typically topped with cactus salad for a tangy contrast, grated queso fresco, and red or green salsa, depending on your preference. They’re the kind of morning or afternoon snack you might grab during errands or before wandering into the adjacent market.

Fresh Oysters at Taller de Ostiones by FISM

You can’t talk about Mexico City without talking about its obsession with seafood. One of the first “recipes” on this blog was No-Recipe Cherry Jam, posted in 2005, which wasn’t quite a recipe. Nevertheless, a lot of people successfully used those guidelines to make cherry jam over the last fifteen years. The basis for it was how I’ve been making jam forever, using 3 parts sugar to 4 parts fruit puree. The standard ratio is to use equal parts sugar to fruit puree but I like to use less.

I often make cherry jam with sweet cherries, which are what one usually finds at the market. But if you can find sour cherries, those are wonderful in the mix, but when fresh, they have a short shelf life so should be used, pronto.

So feel free to mix things up, cherry-wise!

Unlike other recipes, making jam can require a bit of using your instincts, rather than relying on set times and temperatures. Checking the jam texture on a chilled plate is the best way to tell you when it’s done. (As an example, jam usually sets between 218-220ºF,103-104ºC, but this one set a few degrees higher.) Because cherries are low in pectin, this one often “sets” a few degrees higher, although don’t expect cherry jam to be as stiff as other jams that you might be used to, or buy. It’ll be a bit runny, which is just how it should be.

One tool I do recommend is a cherry pitter. Not everyone has one, and they do fall into the “single use” category, but come cherry season, mine gets a good workout. I wrote more about why you should get a cherry pitter here.

The biggest question I get about jam recipes is people asking about reducing the amount of sugar in them. Sugar in jam does a couple of things. One, it helps the mixture jell, along with any natural pectin in the fruit. And two, it preserves it. If you use less, the jam will be runnier and won’t last as long.

Europeans tend to like their jams runnier than their American counterparts, so in the morning, you can have a European experience when you spoon some of this jam on your morning toast, as we do.

Unlike other jams that wrinkle when you do the wrinkle test (putting a spoonful of jam on a frozen plate, returning the plate to the freezer and when the jam wrinkles with you nudge it, it’s done), this one will be more like thickened syrup. It’s done when it’s thick and sticky and holds its shape for a few moments after you drag a spoon through it (below.) The cherries will be shiny and glossy, like beautiful ruby-colored jewels.

Cherry Jam

If you only have 1 pound (450g) of cherries, feel free to cut the recipe in half. The liqueur is optional but heightens the flavors of the jam. Use only enough so it complements the fruit – you don't want to overwhelm it. As mentioned, this jam will be a bit on the runny side when "set." Sour cherries will produce a softer-set jam than sweet cherries. Course Breakfast Cuisine American Keyword cherries, cherry, jam

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds (900g) sweet or sour cherries or a mix
  • 2 1/2 cups (500g) sugar
  • freshly squeezed juice of one medium lemon plus additional juice if desired
  • 1-2 drops of almond extract or 1-2 teaspoons of kirsch or another liqueur, such as amaretto or vodka

Instructions

  • Remove the stems and pit the cherries. Put them in a large non-reactive pot. If using only sweet cherries, add 2-3 tablespoons of water to the pot. (Sour cherries will exude enough liquid on their own so no need to add additional water.) Put a small plate or saucer in the freezer.
  • Cover the pot and cook over medium heat, stirring occasionally, until the cherries are wilted and cooked through, about 10 minutes.
  • Remove the lid and stir in the sugar and lemon juice. Increase the heat to medium-high and cook, stirring frequently until the mixture thickens. It's hard to say how long it will take, but when the mixture resembles the sixth picture in the post, the one in the pot with the bubbles, turn off the heat and test the jam by putting a small spoonful on the frozen dish. Return the dish to the freezer for a few minutes until the jam on it is cool. Run a spoon through it and if the liquid is very thick and leaves a clear patch, as shown in the photo in the post, it's done. If not, return the plate to the freezer and continue to cook the jam, testing it at various intervals, until it's done. (If you want to use a thermometer to check the jam, it should be ready somewhere between 220º and 225ºF, 104º-107ºC.)
  • When ready, turn off the heat and stir in the almond extract or liqueur (or both, if you wish). If desired, you could add an extra squeeze of lemon juice to balance the sweetness. Ladle into clean jars.

Strawberry Jam

Many traditional recipes use an equal amount of sugar to fruit, by weight, but I find it’s okay to decrease it to these proportions. Sugar is both a thickener and a preservation agent. If you reduce it too much more, your jam may not thicken and won’t keep for a long time. (Which is okay if you want thinner jam, you keep it in the refrigerator, and eat it relatively quickly.) This recipe can be scaled up or down, depending on how many strawberries you have. But if you want to make a lot more, best to make it in a couple of batches. To prepare the jars, pour boiling water into them, as well as into the lids. Let them sit 5-10 minutes, then carefully pour out the hot water and set them upside down on a cooling rack until ready to fill. Since I keep my jams in the refrigerator, I don’t put them up them in a traditional sense. I usually just overturn the jars and let them cool, which provides a reasonable seal, then refrigerate them. I find my jams keep well this way for up to one year. Check the National Center for Home Food Preservation website for instructions on how to can fruit jams. Note: Last season strawberries tend to have less natural pectin than ones that arrive earlier. So your jam may be less jelled. One strategy is the finely grate a green apple into the strawberries before cooking them, which adds pectin. (There are guidelines here for using liquid and powdered pectin.) I don’t mind the slightly runnier jam and it’s terrific on yogurt or served with fresh goat cheese for dessert.

Instructions

  • 2. Toss them in a large pot, such as a Dutch oven casserole made of a nonreactive material, with the sugar. Normally apricots give up a lot of juice when baked, but these late-season ones didn't. Fruit varies in moisture but the nice thing about the layer of frangipane is that it keeps the tart dough crisp on the bottom, in case your fruit is juicy. Almond flour is sometimes called almond meal. You can use blanched or unblanched, which has a darker color. If you're avoiding almonds, feel free to swap in hazelnut or pistachio flour. You can certainly use other fruits in this tart, such as sliced nectarines, peaches (peeled or unpeeled), plums, or figs. You'll need about 1 1/2 pounds (700g) of fruit. More important than precise numbers to follow, it's best to try to push the fruit as close together as possible, or if using sliced fruit, to overlap it as much as possible, because it'll cook down during baking. You can also make this without the frangipane but you may want to sprinkle the bottom of the tart dough with some crumbled cookies (such as speculoos or Graham crackers) or moon dust if the fruit is particularly juicy. You can also swap out raspberries or blueberries for the cherries, using them to fill in the spaces between the fruit. I add the cherries at the beginning, but if using raspberries or blueberries, you can add them during the last 15 minutes or so of baking. Ingredients

For the galette dough

  • 1 1/4 cups (175g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 7 tablespoons (100g, 3 1/2 ounces) unsalted butter cubed and chilled
  • 3 1/2 to 4 tablespoons ice water

For the frangipane

  • 3/4 cup (95g) almond flour
  • 1/4 cup (50g) sugar
  • 2 tablespoons (20g) all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons (30g, 1 ounce) unsalted butter at room temperature
  • 1/4 to 1/2 teaspoon pure almond extract
  • 1 large egg, at room temperature

For the fruit galette

  • 1 1/2 pounds (700g) fresh apricots, halved and pitted, or quartered if large (see headnote for other fruits that can be used)
  • 18-20 cherries, sweet or tart, pitted
  • 1 tablespoon (15g, 1/2 ounce) unsalted butter melted
  • 2 tablespoons coarse sugar (or granulated sugar)
  • strained apricot jam, for glazing the tart

Instructions

To make the dough

  • To make the dough, mix together the flour, sugar, and salt in a medium bowl, or in the bowl of a stand mixer.
  • Add the cubed butter and mix, using a pastry blender (or the paddle attachment if using a stand mixer), until the butter is in small, but still visible pieces, about the size of corn kernels.
  • Add the 3 1/2 tablespoons of ice water and mix until the dough starts to come together. If it feels dry, add an additional 1/2 tablespoon of ice water. Form the dough into a disk with your hands, wrap it in plastic wrap and refrigerate for at least 30 minutes.

To make the frangipane

  • In a small bowl, mix together the almond flour, sugar, and all-purpose flour. Add the butter and almond extract, and mix in with a spatula until smooth. Mix in the egg.

To make the tart

  • Preheat the oven to 375ºF (190ºC). Line a baking sheet with parchment paper.
  • On a lightly floured surface, roll the dough to a 14-inch (35cm) circle, turning it over and dusting lightly with flour, to keep it from sticking to the counter.
  • Brush off any excess flour and transfer the dough to the prepared baking sheet. (At this point, you may want to chill the dough for a few minutes, as the frangipane may be a bit tricky to spread if the dough feels warm.)
  • Smear the frangipane on the tart dough, leaving a 2-inch (5cm) rim around the edges. (If the frangipane is quite thick, you can dollop it in 5-6 places over the tart dough, which'll make it easier to spread.) Place the apricots on the frangipane and put cherries in the spaces between the apricots. Fold the edges of the dough over the fruit to make a crust. After pleating and folding the rim, make sure there are no "gullies" in the pleats for fruit juices to run out of. Pinch together any cracks.
  • Brush the rim of the crust generously with melted butter then brush the remaining butter over the fruit. Sprinkle the rim with about 1 tablespoon of coarse or granulated sugar. Then sprinkle another 1 tablespoon over the fruit. (If using fruits like nectarines and peaches, you can reduce the sugar to a few teaspoons.)
  • Bake the tart until the crust is well-browned and the fruit is cooked through, about 40-50 minutes. Remove from the oven and let the tart cool for 5 to 10 minutes, then brush the fruit with apricot jam.

Notes

Serving: Serve with a compatible ice cream, such as vanilla, crème fraîche or noyau ice cream. (The last two are in my books, The Perfect Scoop, and My Paris Kitchen, respectively), or another favorite flavor. A dollop of crème fraîche, sabayon, or softly whipped cream are other options. (Although often I like it just as it is.) Storage: The dough can be made up to two days ahead and refrigerated, or it can be frozen for up to two months. The tart is best enjoyed the same day it’s made, but is okay the second day.

Scotland is well known for its high road and its low road. Now you have to add the Scottish Rocky Road too!

Scottish Rocky Road is the coming together of two greats. The joy and simplicity of Rocky Road, a classic in the eyes of many and the taste and nostalgia-inducing wonder of two Scottish sweet treats.

Ok, so we might be overdoing it a bit, but seriously, Scottish Rocky Road is ridiculous. All the tastes and textures you’d hope for as a fan of Scottish food in such a basic recipe.

You’re going to love it.

What is Rocky Road?

As mentioned above, Scottish Rocky Road is the coming together of classics. An Aussie one and a Scottish one.

“Aussie?” we hear you cry. Yes, Australian. Rocky Road has a fascinating history and it certainly didn’t begin life where we had assumed it had.

Rocky Road is believed to have originated in Australia back in 1853. Interestingly, it wasn’t the creation of a skilled chef or a talented home baker but rather the initiative of business owners trying to salvage sweets that had been damaged during transit from Europe. They blended these confections with local nuts and chocolate.

The name “Rocky Road” comes from the rough paths that these entrepreneurial treats had to travel to reach the gold fields where they were sold. We love this story, and although some American companies claim to have invented Rocky Road, we prefer to believe this fascinating tale from the Southern Hemisphere!!

We make it Scottish by adding two of Tunnock’s best sweet treats. For those in the know, you’ll already be excited, but for those not sure who or what Tunnocks is, let me explain.

What is Tunnock’s?

Scottish Rocky Road is made “Scottish” with the edition of two things: Tunnock’s Tea Cakes and Tunnock’s Caramel Wafers. Both are uber-popular treats here in Scotland. Tunnock is the name of the company that, since 1890, has been making sugary snacks. They started life in Uddingston, Glasgow.

The Tunnock’s Carmel Wafer began life in 1952. It is a dry wafer covered in caramel and coated in milk chocolate. It’s chewy, sweet, and ridiculously moreish!

The Tunnock’s Teacake arrived a wee bit later in 1956, and is iconic in both shame, taste and even the silver and red foil it’s wrapped in! A Teacake is a biscuit topped with a marshmallow ball covered in a thin layer of milk chocolate.

They melt in your mouth with a flavour like nothing else. Children throughout Scotland have had chocolatey faces and sticky hands because of the famous treats, so they couldn’t be missed out of our Scottish Rocky Road.

What is Scottish Rocky Road?

Scottish Rocky Road is the simple coming together of the Australian traybake, one of the world’s most famous traybakes and two of Tunnock’s most popular Scottish confectionaries that are known and celebrated throughout Scotland and, in many cases, the world.

The two things go perfectly together, and the only thing you will dislike is how much of this you will end up eating!

Things you’ll need to make Scottish Rocky Road

  • 8inch square baking tray
  • Baking paper
  • Microwave-proof bowl if melting chocolate in the microwave, or a heat-proof bowl and pan for a double boiler on the stovetop
  • Metal spoon for stirring the chocolate – Don’t use a wooden spoon as you don’t want the chocolate to seize, and wooden spoons can carry water!
  • Large sharp knife cut cutting the Tunnock’s sweets

Ingredients for Scottish Rocky Road

  • 400g Milk Chocolate
  • 100g Butter
  • 200g Tunnocks Teacakes
  • 200g Tunnocks Caramel Wafers
  • 50g white or dark chocolate for topping (optional)
  • 2 tbsp Golden Syrup (optional)
  • Raisins or other dried fruit (optional)

Why all the optional ingredients?

Given the amount of chocolate, caramel, and marshmallow in this Rocky Road, it can be very sweet!

Golden Syrup is good for breaking up the chocolate a bit and making it softer to eat, but does add to the sweetness so you can opt to not include it if you would prefer.

We like white chocolate but using dark chocolate, or no topping at all can always cut through the sweetness, as can adding raisins to the mix as well.

We suggest you make the recipe as is the first time around, and then adjust to your own tastes for the following times you make it (because we’re sure you will!).

Rocky Road is great because you can add anything you like to it and make it your own!

How to Make Scottish Rocky Road – Step by Step Method

Chop the Tunnocks Teacakes into quarters, lay them on a baking tray covered in baking paper and put them in the freezer. The more time, the better, so you may want to do this in advance.

Slice the Tunnocks Caramel Wafers into thin pieces.

Melt the chocolate, butter and golden syrup if using – this can be done in 30-second blasts in the microwave on medium heat, stirring in between with a metal spoon or in a double boiler on the stovetop (a bowl over a pot of simmering water).

Allow the melted chocolate mix to cool briefly.

Put the frozen Teacakes and sliced Caramel Wafers in a bowl along with raisins, if using them.

Pour over the melted chocolate mix and stir briefly and gently to coat the biscuits, but not too much or the marshmallow will all melt.

Tip into a lined 8-inch square baking tray and even out so there are no air gaps, but be careful not to melt the marshmallow.

Top with slices of Tunnocks Caramel Wafers.

Place in the refrigerator for a minimum of 2 hours to set.

Decorate with melted white or dark chocolate, slice and enjoy.

How do you stop the chocolate from seizing?

A common mistake when making Rocky Road is melting the chocolate too quickly, and it seizes. This is when it goes thick and grainy.

To avoid this, make sure you go “low and slow” with the heat. Do short bursts in the microwave on a reduced temperature, or a very low temperature on the stovetop if using a double boiler.

Stir regularly to make sure the chocolate is melting and you’re not overdoing the heat.

Introducing water can also cause the chocolate to seize, so you want to avoid steam in the stovetop or condensation in the microwave, another reason to keep temperatures low.

This is also why you should use a metal spoon to stir rather than wooden, as they can retain water.

If you do find the chocolate seizes, then you can generally save it by introducing a small amount of fat. Add tiny bits of butter or even olive oil and stir through gently so it melts and the chocolate becomes smooth again.

The DIY beef tartare hand rolls—freshly ground bottom round, fluffy rice, and smoky pickled egg cream, plus freeze-dried capers and nori for rolling—are a must, as is the tuna crisp, featuring house-cured slices of tuna loin and belly atop a fried wonton crisp slathered with horseradish. The chefs deliver these dishes to the tables themselves to an eclectic soundtrack featuring everything from Meat Loaf to old-school hip-hop. The drink list is heavy on natural wines, though my go-to is the Toki Highball cocktail or a can of sake. In homage to Moran’s birthplace, there’s also Guinness by the pint, and Irish gin and tonics compete with Orion beer and sake cups.

Kisser

747 Douglas Avenue

Camille Tambunting

The diminutive Kisser, modeled after a Japanese kissaten, a neighborhood spot serving comfort food where locals can eat, drink, and hang out, has been a smash hit since opening in 2023. After spending years in some of the best kitchens in L.A. and Nashville, co-owner couple Brian Lea and Leina Horii decided to focus on their own vision. Inspired in part by Horii’s family sushi restaurant in California, the menu blends traditional Japanese cuisine and their fine-dining techniques with dishes like onigiri stuffed with snow crab and avocado, a perfectly crispy chicken katsu sandwich on fluffy housemade milk bread, and bowls of udon in delicate broth. It’s one of the best places in town to enjoy fresh fish, particularly the chirashi bowl brimming with pristine sashimi and glistening salmon roe. Make sure to end your meal with a green melon cream soda or a crackly miso crème brûlée.

Folk

823 Meridian Street
(615) 610-2595

Courtesy Folk

Folk is a neighborhood gem that Nashville needs now more than ever as hotels and chains threaten to take hold of the dining scene. The restaurant is perched on a quiet corner in East Nashville’s McFerrin Park and welcomes diners with wood-fired sourdough-crust pizzas—my favorite is adorned with littleneck clams, parsley, bonito, and lemon—and rustic Italian-leaning dishes like Marcella beans with spicy pepper relish, bouncy focaccia with whipped ricotta, and more. From the high-ceilinged dining room, adorned with paper lanterns and exposed-brick walls, you can glimpse the bustling kitchen and blazing pizza oven. Natural wines and classic cocktails are the move at the bar, where the afternoon light streams in during the daily apéro hour. The crowd of shockingly healthy houseplants are as well-nourished as the patrons.

Redheaded Stranger

305 Arrington Street
(615) 544-8226

Courtesy Redheaded Stranger

Not far from Folk stands a Tex-Mex taco shop with a sense of humor where you can gobble down one chef’s highly agreeable take on Taco Bell’s Crunchwrap. To make it, chef Bryan Lee Weaver (also of Butcher & Bee) fills his fluffy handmade tortillas with Texas red chili, pork green chili, or both when you order it “Xmas-style.” If tacos are more your speed, you’ll find toppings like brisket, tater tots, and freshly roasted Hatch chiles. To me, the sleeper hit is the vegetarian taco with charred poblano, whipped feta, and crispy rice, thanks to its variety of textures and smoky heat. The queso dip comes with more of those warm, velvety tortillas (never chips!), and the frozen margaritas are tart and free of sickly-sweet sour mix. The interior offers retro-diner vibes, with a Formica bar and red leather booths, but there’s nothing like grabbing a patio seat in the summertime to let those margs work their Texas magic.

Henrietta Red

1200 4th Avenue North
(615) 490-8042

Emily Dorio ( Courtesy Henrietta Red)

Lucky for Nashvillians, former Per Se chef and Nashville native Julia Sullivan came home to open her first solo restaurant in Henrietta Red. This airy, tiled Germantown spot boasts an oyster bar glistening with mollusks, including Maine’s salty Mookie Blues and Alabama’s buttery Murder Points. Despite Nashville’s landlocked status (the Cumberland River doesn’t count), Henrietta Red deftly combines coastal ingredients with Southern flavors in dishes like oysters roasted with ‘nduja butter and whole trout with dandelion greens. Wood-fired seafood is the focus here, but the fresh pastas and vegetables—particularly the beet salad with pistachio and mint—stand out too. Brunch brings its own reason to visit, with dishes like shrimp toast with beets and tahini, and smoked fish cakes with lemon-fennel aïoli.

Prince’s Hot Chicken

5814 Nolensville Pike
(615) 810-9388

Despite the ever-widening swath of restaurants serving Prince’s signature dish around the globe, you have to visit the original purveyor to get real-deal Nashville hot chicken. As the story goes, the ultra-spicy sandwich was created as punishment by a jilted lover of Thornton Prince; but, instead of catching on fire, Prince loved the spice so much that he’d go on to perfect the recipe that launched his business. Today, Prince’s remains the best in class, serving up fried chicken spiced with cayenne pepper oil atop a slice of white bread with a pickle (a foil to all that cayenne). If you’re sticking closer to downtown, swing by Prince’s outpost in the Assembly Food hall to get your hot chicken fix. 

Wendell Smith’s

407 53rd Avenue North
(615) 383-7114

Courtesy Wendell Smith’s

Wood-paneled walls line the dining room of this classic meat ’n’ three restaurant that’s been serving up Southern soul since 1952. Every day, the fourth-generation owners welcome a regular crowd hungry for roast beef, baked ham, and pit barbecue, plus daily specials like fried catfish, chicken and dumplings, and throwback fixins like candied yams, turnip greens, baked apples, fried corn, and creamed potatoes. Wendell Smith’s is a slice of Nashville that has hung on to its corner lot despite unchecked development all around it, still sharing space with the adjacent liquor store of the same name that’s been operating just as long. 

Loveless Cafe

8400 Highway 100
(615) 646-9700 

Courtesy Loveless Cafe

What began 70 years ago as a humble motel and café at the start of the Natchez Trace Parkway has been thriving from the moment Lon and Annie Love began serving biscuits and fried chicken to travelers out of their home kitchen. After their continuing success, the couple turned their home into a restaurant and opened 14 rooms as a motel to accompany their new dining destination. The motel went out of operation years ago, and its rooms are now home to retail space, with smoked hams, jams, and biscuit mix on offer. Though there is now expanded outdoor seating and an outdoor bar serving bloody Marys when the wait is long, there’s nothing better than grabbing a seat in the tiny dining room that still feels like home.  

Sperry’s Restaurant

5109 Harding Pike
(615) 353-0809

Courtesy Sperry’s Restaurant

An institution since 1974, this legendary West Nashville steakhouse is a windowless dining room featuring backlit stained glass, carpeting, a stone fireplace with a roaring fire (most nights), and sporty portraits of the hounds and horses that once lived on the grounds of the abutting Belle Meade Mansion. Martinis and Manhattans pair perfectly with Sperry’s steaks and seafood, from a filet Oscar smothered in crabmeat and béarnaise to king crab legs served with drawn butter. Nashville’s very first salad bar remains gloriously intact here, and diners can pile their plate with neon-green goddess and bacon crumbles (though the plated iceberg wedge salad topped with bacon and black olives is my choice). Bananas Foster, another highlight, is served tableside in a plume of booze-soaked flames and cinnamon-fueled sparks. The doors open at four for happy hour, when sausage-stuffed mushrooms and discounted cocktails draw a steady stream of locals each day.
