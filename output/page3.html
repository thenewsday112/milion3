Copycat Taco Bell Caramel Apple Empanadas

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 1-1/2 cups finely chopped peeled tart apples
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/8 teaspoon ground allspice
  • 1/3 cup thick caramel apple dip
  • 2 sheets refrigerated pie crust
  • 1 large egg yolk
  • 2 tablespoons water
  • 1/4 cup butter, melted
  • 1 cup cinnamon sugar
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. In a small saucepan, combine apples, sugar, cinnamon and allspice. Cook until apples are tender, 4-5 minutes. Drain; discard liquid. Transfer to a small bowl. Add caramel; stir to coat. Scoop by tablespoonfuls onto waxed paper. Freeze until firm, about 1 hour.
  2. Unroll pie crust; cut into 14 circles using a 4-in. circle cookie cutter (rerolling dough scraps as needed).
  3. In a small bowl, whisk together egg yolk and water. Brush edges of crusts with egg wash; place apple filling in the center. Fold crusts over filling and seal edges well with a fork. Place 2 in. apart on parchment-lined baking sheets. Freeze until firm, about 30 minutes.
  4. Brush remaining egg wash over tops. Cut 2 small slits on the top of each empanada.
  5. Bake at 425° for 12-14 minutes or until golden brown. Remove from oven and brush with melted butter. Carefully toss in cinnamon sugar. Serve warm.

Nutrition Facts

1 piece: 234 calories, 13g fat (6g saturated fat), 29mg cholesterol, 159mg sodium, 28g carbohydrate (14g sugars, 0 fiber), 2g protein.

Cherry Peach Pie with Salted Pecan Streusel

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 1-1/3 cups all-purpose flour
  • 1-1/2 teaspoons sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 teaspoon pumpkin pie spice
  • 4 tablespoons shortening, cubed
  • 4 tablespoons cold butter, cubed
  • 3 to 4 tablespoons water
  • TOPPING:
  • 1/2 cup all-purpose flour
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1/4 cup packed brown sugar
  • 1/4 cup crushed gingersnap cookies
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup old-fashioned oats
  • 1/2 cup chopped pecans
  • 1/4 cup butter, melted
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • FILLING:
  • 2 cups sliced peeled peaches
  • 1 cup frozen pitted dark sweet cherries, thawed
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • 1/3 cup sugar
  • 1/3 cup packed brown sugar
  • 4 tablespoons potato starch
  • Pinch salt
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. In a large bowl, mix flour, sugar, salt and pie spice; cut in shortening and butter until crumbly. Gradually add ice water, tossing with a fork until dough holds together when pressed. Shape into a disk; wrap and refrigerate 1 hour or overnight.
  2. Preheat oven to 400°. On a lightly floured surface, roll dough to a 1/8-in.-thick circle; transfer to a 9-in. pie plate. Trim crust to 1/2 in. beyond rim of plate; flute edge. Refrigerate while preparing filling.
  3. For the topping, in a medium bowl, whisk together flour, sugars, crushed cookies and salt. Stir in oats and pecans. Add the butter and vanilla until small clumps form. Set aside.
  4. In a large bowl, toss peaches and cherries with lemon juice and vanilla. In a separate bowl, whisk together sugars, potato starch and salt. Add to fruit, toss until well coated.
  5. Transfer filling to prepared crust. Sprinkle with reserved topping; pressing down to adhere as needed. Bake for 20 minutes. Reduce oven setting to 350°, bake until crust and topping are golden brown and fruit is bubbly, 20-25 minutes longer. Cool completely on a wire rack.

Nutrition Facts

1 piece: 503 calories, 23g fat (9g saturated fat), 31mg cholesterol, 563mg sodium, 70g carbohydrate (38g sugars, 2g fiber), 5g protein.

French Onion Stuffing

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 16 ounces sourdough bread, cut into 1-in. cubes
  • 2 tablespoons unsalted butter
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 pounds medium onions, thinly sliced
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon pepper
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/4 cup dry sherry
  • 2 tablespoons minced fresh thyme sprigs, plus more for garnish
  • 2 large eggs, room temperature
  • 2 cups reduced-sodium beef broth
  • 6 ounces Gruyere cheese, grated
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. Preheat oven to 350°. Spread bread cubes in a single layer on two baking sheets. Bake 10 minutes; flip bread cubes and rotate baking sheets. Bake until very dry and golden brown, 5-8 minutes. Set aside to cool.
  2. Meanwhile, in a Dutch oven, heat butter and olive oil over medium heat. Add onions; cook until softened, 10-15 minutes, stirring frequently. Stir in salt and pepper. Reduce heat to medium-low; cook until deep golden brown, 40-50 minutes, stirring occasionally. Add garlic; cook until fragrant, 30-60 seconds. Add dry sherry; use a wooden spoon to scrape up browned bits. Stir in thyme. Remove from heat.
  3. In a large bowl, whisk eggs until smooth. Stir in cooked onions and bread cubes. Stir in beef broth until evenly moistened.
  4. Spoon stuffing into a greased 13x9-in. or 3 quart baking dish. Cover with foil; bake 30 minutes. Uncover; bake until crispy and browned, 15-20 minutes.
  5. Remove from the oven; increase oven temperature to 425°. Sprinkle cheese on top. Bake until melted and bubbly, 3-5 minutes. Garnish with thyme to serve.

Nutrition Facts

1 serving: 394 calories, 16g fat (7g saturated fat), 79mg cholesterol, 915mg sodium, 46g carbohydrate (10g sugars, 4g fiber), 16g protein.

Steal My Food Itinerary: 3 Days on Prince Edward Island

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →
Raspberry Point oysters at Landmark Oyster House on Prince Edward Island. | Kate Heddings

Recently I spent a few days exploring Prince Edward Island, a place that embraces its local foodways with gusto. There’s a complex history here, with roots in Indigenous Mi’kmaq, Acadian, Irish, Scottish, and other cultures. And if one thing unites these histories, it’s the island’s abundance of seafood and produce, which have helped earn it the nickname Canada’s Food Island. How can you not want to visit Canada’s Food Island?

On a stretch of land only about 140 miles long, you can eat oysters pulled out of nearby waters, potatoes grown on local farms, dig into a juicy burger made with PEI beef, and lick ice cream made with milk from the cows you pass along those famous red-dirt roads. That’s a lot of good eating packed into a place you can drive across in an afternoon.

I had to structure my visit, therefore, around where to dine, but also include a bucket-list visit to Green Gables Heritage Place. (I am a complete freak for L.M. Montgomery’s novel Anne of Green Gables, set on PEI around the turn of the last century.) I also had to schedule time at the PEI International Shellfish Festival, which promised endless trays of freshly shucked oysters and clams, hot buttered lobster rolls, steaming bowls of mussels, and plenty of entertainment, from shucking competitions and cooking demos to live music and dancing.

My husband and I rented a car and stationed ourselves in Charlottetown, the island’s main hub. Prince Edward Island is situated in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, just a hop away from Nova Scotia. You’re never far from the ocean here, and on a sunny day, the air seems to sparkle. The island is known for its red-sand cliffs and beaches, rolling landscapes, fishing harbors, and lighthouses. If you’ve read Anne of Green Gables, you’ll know just how pretty PEI is.

We landed late Thursday afternoon, ready to hit the ground running. With just shy of three days, I had carefully mapped out the places I wanted to visit, using Charlottetown as my base. 

Day 1

Drinks and snacks: Slaymaker & Nichols

Charlottetown isn’t big, and most things here are walkable within 20 minutes or so. First stop in PEI: Slaymaker & Nichols, a gastropub and inn with the feel of an old-fashioned supper club. It takes its name from the 19th-century circus that once performed on the site. They take cocktail making seriously here. The summery feel of the day prompted me to try the watermelon mule, with hibiscus-infused vodka and chunks of pickled watermelon. I liked it, especially alongside the creamy lobster dip with tomatoes and harissa, and the buttery shrimp pil pil, with lots of tasty little spears of asparagus.

More drinks: Reddins

I was saving myself for my first lobster roll, which we grabbed up the street in an unremarkable spot. It was pretty mediocre, so I won’t go on about it. But after eating, we stopped into Reddins, a speakeasy that channels PEI’s Prohibition history in a dark, intimate third-floor bar with vintage furniture, good jazz, and whimsical cocktails. Our excellent bartender, AB, told us the space had once been a pharmacy where folks came during Prohibition to get their “medicine” — drugstores could legally dispense alcohol by prescription. We tried a few of the signature cocktails, and the one that stood out was the Pink Canary, with vodka, rose, lychee, citrus, and rosé bubbles, served in a small bathtub. Yup, you heard that right. It was sweet and awfully fun to sip. 

Tip: You need a secret password to get in when you buzz from downstairs. You can get it online, or just do as we did and ask the hostess next door for help.

Day 2

Breakfast: Receiver Coffee

In the pouring rain, we made our way to nearby Victoria Row for breakfast at local roaster Receiver Coffee. Fuel for the day included strong coffee, a particularly good cup of Earl Grey cream tea, and the Ham Slam, a big fluffy cheddar biscuit layered with a runny fried egg, Black Forest ham, Swiss, and Dijon mayo.

Lunch and more: Landmark Oyster House, Island Chocolates

We swung by the shellfish festival (more on that later) but the rain wasn’t welcoming, so we got back in the car and drove out to tiny and charming Victoria-by-the-Sea. When I say tiny, I mean it: The town is half a square mile, with a population under 200. Despite the town’s size, Landmark Oyster House, set there in an old general store with pretty green shingles and a steep gabled roof, thrives.


Our friendly waiter (everyone is abundantly friendly on PEI) suggested Raspberry Point oysters to start. They hit the spot: medium-sized, briny, and sweet, served with lemon wedges and freshly grated horseradish, which is now how I think every oyster should be eaten. We also tried the scallop dog, with buttery sweet scallops, bacon jam, red pepper aioli, and pickled shallots nestled in a toasted bun. It came alongside a very good, eggy potato salad. 

After seeing the cheery lighthouse and visiting a couple of little gift shops, we popped into Island Chocolates, a family-run shop and café where bars and truffles are hand-crafted, often using local ingredients like haskap, a berry that tastes somewhere between a blueberry and raspberry. That fruity haskap truffle had a nice thick shell of milk chocolate.  

Sun out? Check. We spent the afternoon at the festival and got a substantial fix of oysters and clams. The lobster rolls here were stuffed with unadorned lobster meat, then melted butter from a gigantic vat was ladled over the top.

Dinner: North Shore Canteen

For dinner, both our bartender and waiter at Slaymaker recommended North Shore Canteen in French River, a local haunt for sure. (A slew of chefs from the shellfish festival were two tables down from us.) The menu, which changes with the current haul, hit on the day’s best ingredients: halibut, striped bass, the aforementioned shellfish, beef, and potatoes. We opted for a very generous portion of local beef tartare, amazingly tasty with capers, roasted garlic, and freshly grated Parmesan, served with Covered Bridge potato chips. We wanted to try more PEI beef and opted for a juicy hanger steak with chimichurri and black garlic, which showcased it well. Dessert was a must: parsnip cake drizzled with caramel sauce for the win.

Day 3

It was time for me to check Green Gables off my bucket list. This is an article about where to eat, so suffice it to say that if you’re a fan, this is 100 percent worth a visit to see the house, wander the Haunted Wood and Lover’s Lane, and taste some nonalcoholic raspberry cordial (IYKYK).

All that walking made me hungry, which was good because this was a much anticipated two-lunch day.

Lunch x 2: Blue Mussel Café and Richard’s Fresh Seafood

First stop: Blue Mussel Café, a bright and very welcoming, beloved PEI restaurant set just back from the water in Rustico Harbor. I think it’s obvious what to order, and for good reason: These are excellent mussels — plump, fresh, and brought to the table piled high in a large stainless steel bowl. You can have them prepared with rosemary and lemon or beer and wine. I’d rate them equally, but I did like the hit of rosemary. Melted butter comes in a small ramekin on the side; I used it to dip each mussel, and my husband poured it all over the top. I didn’t have it, but the other signature, seafood chowder, looked popular. It’s made with haddock, salmon, scallops, mussels, and PEI potatoes, and served with a giant biscuit. Noted for my next visit.

We hightailed it to our next meal because I had yet to have the perfect lobster roll. Richard’s Fresh Seafood in Covehead Wharf delivered. This is one of the island’s longest-running fish shacks, where you can savor fish and chips and beer on the deck overlooking the fishing boats, or camp out on the nearby beach under the lighthouse with a lobster roll. no mistake: This is the quintessential lobster roll. It was an exercise in abundance, packed with sweet lobster meat that managed to be chunky and fine, bound with lemon and chive mayo and tiny bits of onion, all stuffed in a buttery toasted bun. It came with crunchy coleslaw, crispy fries, and a paper cup of ketchup. There are other menu items, but this lobster roll alone is worth the trip to PEI.

Dinner: Rustico Cannery

For our final dinner, we drove back toward North Rustico Harbor to Rustico Cannery, which opened this past spring. The airy, modern space has giant windows and a big deck that takes in super sunset views.

I needed greens, and Rustico delivered. We had crisp romaine hearts with a creamy, spicy charred jalapeño dressing, lardons, Parmesan, and sourdough crunchies. My husband hit the jackpot with his dinner: panko crusted, pan-fried haddock with pickled beet remoulade, jalapeño relish, confit potatoes, and coleslaw.

Ice Cream: Cows Creamery

I’d be remiss if I didn’t include our multiple visits to Cows Creamery, the island’s local ice cream shop, which — conveniently — has locations across PEI. It sources the milk for the ice cream from small, local dairy farms and the results are winning!  A few standouts were Coffee Cowrunch (English toffee and Oreo), Cownadian Maple (a triple hit of maple ice cream, maple swirl, and maple cookie chunks; when in Rome), and Messie Bessie, chocolate ice cream with Oreo, toffee, and Cow Chips — the thick, ruffled local potato chips covered with rich milk or dark chocolate that are for sale by the bag all over the island.

A final note: Many places are seasonal, so check if they’re open if you’re visiting in cooler months.

‘The Gilded Age’ Is Back! Here’s What to Eat While You Watch the Show

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

I can be persuaded to watch almost anything that promises extravagant set designs and beautiful costumes. If there’s history and a little mischievous social maneuvering involved, even better. My coworkers recently recommended The Gilded Age, and within two weeks, I’d watched all three seasons just in time for season four to drop.

There’s plenty to admire: Bertha Russell’s determination, Peggy Scott’s writing career and rooms furnished with a reassuring disregard for minimalism. I love the history, too, from the railroad fortunes and New York bankers to Lewis Latimer’s work in electric lighting and the woman who helped bring the Brooklyn Bridge to completion. Every episode gave me another reason to keep watching.

My husband is often still at the restaurant in the evenings, so once the kids are asleep, I have a few hours to settle in with a show. While I watched The Gilded Age, I usually poured a glass of wine (some of my favorites are under $25 at Walmart) and enjoyed the fire as Christine Baranski made an art of Agnes’s disapproval. But with the new season arriving on Nov. 1, I’m borrowing a few ideas from the show to make a watch party spread.

Set the Scene

I have a decidedly romantic streak when it comes to my surroundings, so the show’s interiors have my full attention. I’m particularly taken with the glassware and china that can make even a small supper feel indulgent. With a few soft lamps and the fire going, I’m looking forward to borrowing a little of that grandeur for my own evening.

  • A favorite plate and a special glass: Bannister may have reservations about Bertha’s colored glasses, but as a collector of vintage glassware, I find it rather inspiring. Thrift stores are wonderful places to look for floral china, small silver-plated trays and individual glasses to mix and match for a pretty tablescape.
  • A few flowers on the table: A handful of stems from the grocery store in a little pitcher is plenty. They’re equally charming when arranged in tiny bud vases or in one center arrangement. Finding flowers that mirror the colors in your china is a great way to make everything look put together.
  • Something soft beneath the dishes: Because so many watch parties happen in the living room rather than the kitchen, cover your coffee table with a tablecloth. It’ll look pretty, and it’ll catch any crumbs before they end up in the carpet. Add even more Gilded Age elegance with cloth napkins under silverware rather than paper napkins.
  • A little note for company: A handwritten card listing dishes like “Ada’s Clam Chowder” would be a nice addition if you have company for your watch party. Cream card stock and a nice pen are enough to give the food a thoughtful connection to the show.
  • Music before the show: A little opera while crafting vintage cocktails would be perfect for setting the scene, though I’d leave the battle over opera boxes to Mrs. Astor and Bertha.

A Glass for Agnes—or Ada

I’m more than happy to borrow the show’s habit of pouring wine into crystal glasses. Mine usually contains Champagne, but Ada’s season-three temperance campaign gives us a reason to put some thought into an alcohol-free option too.

Agnes’s feelings about wine appear to be settled, but there’s room for both sisters.

  • Champagne or sparkling wine: I’d choose a bottle I enjoy and pour it into a favorite glass. There’s simply no need to improve on a good Champagne.
  • A Champagne cocktail: Brandy and bitters give a Champagne cocktail a little extra ceremony, which is something perfectly befitting of a Gilded Age watch party.
  • An alcohol-free spritz: Sparkling water with cranberry juice and an orange twist would be my contribution to Ada’s cause, but any mocktail would make an equally good toast to the evening. I’d use the same glassware and leave the pledge unsigned.
  • Tea: Some evenings call for a pot of black tea and a little milk. I like bringing the pot into the living room on a tray.

Small Bites for High Society

The Russells may require a meticulously planned succession of courses, but I’d be content with a small selection of savory bites. Oysters, warm crab dip or little sandwiches would make a perfectly elegant dinner by the fire.

  • Oysters: Oysters make frequent appearances at the show’s lavish dinners, and a few with lemon would be enough to make the evening feel special. Our Oysters Rockefeller are baked with butter, spinach and Romano. And with John D. Rockefeller reportedly arriving this season, his namesake oysters certainly deserve a place at the table.
  • Crab: Marian’s visit to the Scotts is difficult to forget, though I’d rather take inspiration from their crab luncheon than her assumptions. A lovely crab meat au gratin would bring some of that indulgence home, served warm with toasted bread or crackers.
  • Asparagus: Few vegetables receive such careful instruction on how to eat them. After Bannister explains the difference between English and American asparagus etiquette, the Russell kitchen decided to avoid the matter altogether. I’d serve our roasted asparagus anyway, with a squeeze of lemon, and trust everyone to choose their own utensils.
  • Tea sandwiches: Mamie Fish’s doll-themed tea party is more commitment than I’m looking for, but I can appreciate the sandwiches. Cucumber and cream cheese, butter with thinly sliced radish or chicken salad would all be good on soft bread.

Dinner Across Fifth Avenue

For something more substantial, you can look to the show’s luncheons as much as its elaborate dinner parties. A bowl of soup or a sandwich can feel quite grand when the ingredients are good and someone else is responsible for the evening’s drama.

  • Clam chowder: A bowl of New England clam chowder is enough to win over Reverend Forte though it does little to impress Agnes. I’m with Ada on this one. Our New England clam chowder has tender potatoes, clams and a creamy broth that sounds perfect for a November season premiere.
  • Lobster rolls: The lobster left behind after Bertha’s disastrous first reception deserves a more appreciative audience. Our lobster rolls put that idea into buttery toasted buns, making them much more convenient for my own seating arrangements.
  • Steak: George’s Arizona business trip includes a steak order in a saloon, a considerable change of scenery from the Russell dining room. A steak seared in a hot skillet and finished with butter and fresh herbs would be a fitting way to bring a little of the show’s indulgence home.
  • Salmon: The exquisite salmon platters at Bertha’s dinners are hard to overlook, even among all the other beautiful dishes. For an easy at-home version, borrow the fish and simplify the presentation: a grilled fillet with lemon and garlic would be perfect for any cozy viewing party.

Dessert in the Drawing Room

A pot of tea and something sweet would be enough for me on an evening when dinner is already behind us. I like having dessert ready before I sit down, whether that means a trifle waiting in the fridge or a few pastries brought home in a paper bag.

  • Trifle: Bannister’s assessment of trifle as a nursery food seems unnecessarily limiting. Layers of cake, cream and fruit have plenty to offer the rest of us. Our strawberry trifle would be a good place to start. A splash of sherry over the cake would move it a little further from the nursery.
  • French pastries: Aurora Fane’s gathering put me in the mood for a plate of French pastries. Buy a few macarons, perhaps something chocolate, and let the bakery determine how ambitious the evening becomes. Arrange the pastries on a pretty plate, and voila!
  • Apple pie: For all the elaborate food in the show, apple pie is an appealing return to something familiar. Armstrong’s visit to her mother casts it in an unhappy light, but I’m prepared to overlook that for a warm slice with a buttery crust.
  • Ice cream: Ice cream has Agnes’s approval, which is no small recommendation. Strawberry would be a fitting choice from the period. A scoop in a small glass bowl with a crisp cookie would be all the dessert I’d need.

Sam O’Brien

On a fall afternoon in Halachó, a small town in the Mexican state of Yucatán, Wilson Alonzo stood before a smoldering mound of dirt and began digging. Slowly clearing away branches, leaves, and soil, the chef unearthed a large disc enclosed in banana leaves. He peeled the wrapping back and examined the golden edges of the round tamale inside. He nodded. It was ready for the altar.

Every October and November, Yucatecans like Alonzo gather at the edges of underground ovens and cook mukbil pollo (“buried chicken”), a sacred tamale made only for the Yucatán Peninsula’s Day of the Dead. Known as Janal Pixán (“Food for the Souls” in Yucatec Maya) or Los Finados, the holiday welcomes returning spirits: children on October 31, adults on November 1, and all spirits on November 2, followed by a farewell feast eight—or in some cases, 30—days later. Above anything else that might grace an ofrenda, which could include anything from hot chocolate to relleno negro to beer, mukbil pollo is the star that occupies every altar’s center.

“It is the absolute highlight of the offering,” Alonzo said. One bite confirms why: The crispy, caramelized crust of lard-enriched masa gives way to a smoky, savory mouthful of chicken bathed in a brothy crimson sauce of achiote, tomato, sweet and spicy peppers, epazote, onions, and garlic.

Sam O’Brien

But Alonzo—whose restaurant, Ya’axche, specializes in traditional Yucatecan dishes—said it’s not mukbil pollo’s flavor that nourishes spirits; instead of eating it, they consume the tamale through its aroma. And a crucial part of that aroma, he added, comes from the píib. Dating back to the ancient Maya, this underground cooking technique uses wood, stones, and leaves to impart a slightly herbaceous, smoky succulence to dishes like mukbil pollo—also known as pibipollo or pib—and cochinita pibil.

“Cooking it in a gas oven simply isn’t the same,” Alonzo said. “You lose the unique aromas and flavors imparted by the earth itself.”

Not everyone sees the píib as essential. In cities, where outdoor space is limited, most tamales are baked indoors. When Steffan Igor Ayora-Díaz, a retired anthropologist, was growing up in the city of Valladolid, his mother made mukbil pollo at home, then brought it to a local bakery to cook in an industrial oven. “Nobody said, ‘Oh, it has to be in a píib,’ ” he explained.

Ayora-Díaz now lives in the Yucatán state capital of Mérida, where he said beliefs surrounding Janal Pixán lean more secular. Not only do most eschew the píib, but he and many of his friends no longer build ofrendas, instead celebrating the holiday by gathering with family over a meal. Mukbil pollo is still important, but it plays a role more analogous to turkey on Thanksgiving. “We’re still going to eat it because that’s the food that you eat on these days, but you’re not really thinking of dead people when you’re eating it,” he said.

Sam O’Brien

While purists decry these nontraditional approaches to the holiday, Ayora-Díaz points out that mukbil pollo is a blend of ancient and modern. “It’s a ‘traditional’ dish, but I’m putting ‘traditional’ in quotes because it’s relatively new,” he said. The Maya have been cooking in píibs and eating tamales for millennia, but ingredients like garlic, onions, and lard arrived with the Spanish conquistadors in the 1500s. And, according to Ayora-Díaz, mukbil pollo only started appearing in written records in the 1900s.

Janal Pixán, too, is a hybrid of sorts, blending Christian rituals for All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day with Maya beliefs surrounding death and ancestor veneration. In Xocén, a small village of largely Yucatec Maya residents about 20 minutes outside Valladolid, honoring the dead can mean not only praying for their souls at Catholic mass but welcoming them into one’s home as guests and offering them the same hospitality as the living.

Sam O’Brien

“We are dealing with spirits who require respect and specific observances, and who actually come to visit us,” Miguel Tun, a Yucatec Maya tour guide, said. “Even the yards look clean during this time. You walk past someone’s house and see that everything is spotless because visitors are coming.”

Tun lives in a modern home next to his parents’ palapa in Xocén. The Tuns, like many Yucatec Maya, make their tamales only for the biix, or eighth day of Janal Pixán, when souls are believed to return to purgatory. In Xocén, the local version of mukbil pollo is perfectly suited for this journey. Called chachak waaj (“red tamale” in Yucatec Maya), it’s like a small hand pie.

“The idea is that the souls can easily carry the food with them,” Tun said. “It’s about seeing them off. It’s like when people travel—say, from abroad—to visit, and we give them a gift.” 

This gift isn’t just the tamale itself but the time spent creating it. I visited the Tuns to learn how to make chachak waaj from scratch. We began in the morning. After shredding chicken, slow-cooking sauce, mixing manteca (rendered pork lard) into masa, assembling each tamale, and wrapping them in banana leaves, we needed headlamps to navigate the píib in the darkness. 

Sam O’Brien

“If you are going to make an offering as part of a spiritual practice, it has to be something into which you have poured your effort, you have sacrificed your time,” he said. “That holds significant meaning within the Maya worldview.”

The hours spent making chachak waaj also provide an opportunity to reminisce about the dead. “It’s a moment where the whole family is together, remembering things while cooking, making preparations, or kneading the dough,” Tun said. “Sometimes the conversation is about what’s happening in the community, but there are moments where someone makes a comment about something the deceased person used to do or say.”

Sam O’Brien

At 9:30 p.m., Tun and his father returned from the backyard píib carrying several trays of tamales. It was time for the final step. The Tuns, several cousins, and an aunt gathered on plastic chairs and tree stumps and started unwrapping. They inspected each crust, making sure it was sufficiently crispy (the ones that weren’t would head back to the píib). As they worked, scattering charred leaves at their feet, they talked about an upcoming dance recital for one of their cousins.

Later Tun told me this is, for him, the most important part of Janal Pixán. “Even though a loved one is gone, you demonstrate strength, you show that the family remains united,” he said. “You want that person, when they arrive, to see everyone together, to see that bond. That’s what matters most. I believe that’s the true essence of the Day of the Dead.”

We’re in the Era of the $200 Cup of Coffee

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →
Sey | Sey

A version of this post originally appeared in Eater Today, which spotlights the freshest news and stories from across the food world every day. Subscribe now.

These days, Bushwick is perhaps best known for its graffiti art, ecuavolley, and queer bars; the Brooklyn neighborhood is famous for its family-owned taquerias, American-Italian pastry shops, and the hallowed grounds of the Girls episode where Shoshanna accidentally does meth and runs around pantsless at a warehouse rave. Situate yourself at the heart of its largest park, Maria Hernandez, and you’ll see a kaleidoscopic blend of ravers, Latino families, and the baklava guy.

Starting this week, it’s also home to a $200 cup of coffee. 

Bushwick’s Sey Coffee recently announced that it would be touring a rare Panamanian geisha from Finca Sophia, a coffee farm in Panama’s Chiriquí highlands. Sey scooped up the geisha at auction for $44,275, which comes to $6,325 per kilogram, or roughly $200 per cup. The geisha is billed as extraordinarily aromatic, floral, and juicy; Doug Cohen, Sey’s director of brand strategy, advises enjoying it with a clean palate, completely black, and taking your time with it. “As the coffee cools, different aspects of its character become apparent, and that evolution is part of the experience,” he says. 

Even among fourth-wave coffee shops where prices continue to climb higher, Sey’s $200 cup is an outlier. “Coffee as a premium product is relatively new,” says Cohen. “For so long, coffee has been treated as a commodity, and its value has been constrained by that perception.” He says that both before and right after COVID, folks were becoming more curious about coffee, and educating themselves on platforms like Youtube, Discord, and online forums about how to appreciate it and “understand just how high the ceiling of quality can be.” The arrival of the $200 cup of coffee from a cafe in a neighborhood at the epicenter of conversations about both culinary innovation and gentrification feels ripe for closer examination.

So, how did this coffee end up at this price? “We have been purchasing coffee from Finca Sophia for several years,” Cohen says. “This release really grew out of that existing relationship.” When Finca’s general manager told Cohen that the farm’s yields were down by approximately 95 percent, Cohen says a bigger question was raised: “What does it mean to be a good partner when the people you have built relationships with are facing a difficult year?” Buying up the geisha beans felt like a way to both support Sey’s producer, and further develop what Cohen calls “elevated coffee experiences.” 

In the 2010s, the artisanal coffeehouse wave washed over the country, and especially Brooklyn, with hipster gusto. Sey Coffee was an integral part of that boom. Initially called Lofted Coffee, it operated out of its founders’ Bushwick loft until it rebranded to Sey in 2017, and expanded operations to its present Bushwick location on Grattan Street. That space has become something of a local icon since; Sprudge called its aesthetic “shoebox crossed with an airplane hangar”; the minimal-industrial, cream-hued interiors hit a very Axel Vervoordt-with-a-cyber-sigil-tattoo note (positive). The shop’s cachet has drawn coffee lovers, artists, yuppies, and artist-yuppies; customers have snacked on the gluten-free pastries from L’imprimerie (and now, post-L’imprimerie closure, pastries from Smør), and often post up with their dogs thanks to the semi-indoor-outdoor layout. 

Sey is hoping to elevate the coffee shop model to something more premium, with a stated focus on ethics and sustainability. Thus far, it’s earned acclaim for those efforts. Food & Wine named it the best coffee shop in New York and received positive media attention for opting out of tipping, choosing instead to pay its staff up to more than 80 percent above the NYC hourly minimum wage; full-time baristas can make up to $31 per hour in addition to receiving benefits and a share of the cafe’s revenue. 

Increasingly, coffee is getting the same treatment as high-end dining and tasting experiences. There’s the omakase-style menu at NYC’s Driftaway Coffee, or San Francisco’s Komakase coffee tasting room, both of which steer coffee away from quick consumption and toward a world of degustation that appears to have more in common with pricier wine and spirit tastings. The expansion of “espresso house” chain Watch House stateside is also an indicator: The London-based specialty roaster expanded to a fourth location in New York City this summer, raising eyebrows for its $32 cup of coffee while also reportedly scouting for more locations in Austin, Chicago, and Miami. 

The emerging demand may be just much about luxury as it is about craft. I can’t help but wonder if the $200 cup of coffee is happening in Bushwick because the Girls-era Bushwickian has grown up and wants more exclusive experiences. Is this what happens when, as culture writer Raven Smith said on his new BBC podcast last week, the indie outgrows its sleaze? “For us, this is not about pushing prices higher for the sake of it,” Cohen says. “It is about challenging how we assign value to coffee in the first place. What is an extraordinary coffee actually worth?” 

As much as we may lament climbing prices for specialty coffee and espresso, why wouldn’t this happen to the category when so many other aspects of food and beverage — from cocktails to sushi to bread — are following the same trajectory? Higher consumer costs notwithstanding, the marketing of specialty beans does steer U.S. coffee culture away from its productivity-focused consumption and the work-optimized “coffee break.”

“Why do we accept that certain wines can command thousands of dollars, yet question whether coffee can have comparable value?” Cohen asks. It’s a fair question, albeit one that I’d answer with another: Once we acknowledge that coffee should command a higher price, who still gets to drink it?

If you’re looking for a filling, comforting dinner that hits all the right marks without leaving you with a sink full of dishes, this Creamy Chicken and Tortellini Skillet is about to become your new weeknight obsession! Tender, seasoned chicken, plump cheese tortellini, sweet onions, and aromatic garlic come together in a velvety Parmesan cream sauce. Packed with lean protein and a healthy dose of spinach, it’s a complete, satisfying meal that goes from skillet to table in under 30 minutes. My husband even asked me to make an extra batch so he could bring some to his buddies at work!

From the Freezer to Creamy Comfort Food

The real beauty of this creamy chicken tortellini is its one-skillet convenience and how it delivers maximum flavor with hardly any effort (minimal cleanup? Count me in!). I season the chicken before coating it in a little flour, which helps thicken the broth and pan juices into a creamy sauce without making a separate roux. Even the frozen tortellini goes straight into the skillet without precooking. I add the cream when the broth is a little cooler to keep the sauce smooth, and I leave it slightly loose because the tortellini keeps soaking it up as it sits (which makes it even better for leftovers!).

Frozen spinach is another budget-friendly shortcut I love here. It goes straight from the freezer into the skillet with no chopping, making this a restaurant-worthy dinner without the expensive ingredients or extra prep! Prefer a meat-free alternative? Just leave the chicken out of this dish and swap the chicken broth, or try our spinach tortellini skillet next.

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Butter and olive oil are better together here! I use both because the olive oil helps coat the skillet for sautéing, while the butter adds a rich flavor that carries through into the creamy sauce. Keep the heat at medium, though, as butter can still burn when mixed with oil.
  2. Don’t cut the chicken too small! Aim for evenly sized, generous bite-sized pieces (check out my step-by-step photos below for reference). Chicken shrinks as it cooks, and tiny pieces can dry out quickly. Giving the chicken some room to make contact with the hot skillet also helps develop a little golden color and extra flavor. Just make sure the chicken reaches 165°F internally before serving.
  3. Keep the tortellini FROZEN until you’re ready to use it. No thawing or boiling beforehand! This recipe is designed for tortellini straight from the freezer, so thawing it first can throw off the cooking time and leave you with mushy pasta. Different brands may cook a little faster or slower, so check that the tortellini is tender all the way through before serving.
  4. A gentle simmer is your friend. Once the heavy cream and Parmesan go in, I keep the heat at medium-low. You only want small and gentle bubbles. Too much heat can reduce the sauce too quickly or cause the cheese to turn grainy instead of melting smoothly. Stir everything gently, too, so those little cheese-filled tortellini stay intact!
  5. Customize your skillet! I think adding peas or substituting them instead of spinach would be delicious. Asparagus or broccoli florets would also be delicious choices for substitutes or additions.

Creamy Chicken and Tortellini Skillet

This Creamy Chicken and Tortellini Skillet cooks everything together in a garlicky Parmesan sauce with spinach. No need to boil the pasta separately! Course Main Course Cuisine American Total Cost $13.94 recipe / $2.32 serving Prep Time 10 minutes minutes Cook Time 20 minutes minutes Total Time 30 minutes minutes Servings 6 servings (1.5 cups each) Calories 595kcal Author Jess Rice

Equipment

  • Large Sauté Pan

Ingredients

  • 1 lb. chicken breast I used just over that- 1.31lbs, $6.31*
  • 1 small yellow onion diced, (5.79 oz) $0.42
  • 3 garlic cloves minced, $0.18
  • ½ tsp salt $0.01
  • ½ tsp black pepper freshly cracked, $0.03
  • ½ tsp garlic powder $0.03
  • ½ tsp Italian seasoning $0.04
  • ¼ cup all-purpose flour $0.05
  • 1 Tbsp olive oil $0.09
  • 2 Tbsp salted butter $0.20
  • 2 cups chicken broth $0.26**
  • 19 oz. frozen cheese tortellini not thawed, $3.75***
  • ¾ cup heavy cream room temp, $1.11
  • 1 cup frozen spinach $0.32
  • ½ cup Parmesan cheese $1.14

Instructions

  • Gather ingredients.
  • Dice chicken breast into bite-size pieces. They will shrink up a little as they cook.
  • Mince onion and garlic. Set aside for now.
  • Add salt, black pepper, garlic powder, and Italian seasoning to the diced chicken. Toss so every piece of chicken is seasoned.
  • Then, add the flour to the seasoned chicken. Toss until fully coated on all sides.
  • In a large sauté pan, add olive oil and salted butter over medium heat.
  • Once the butter has melted, add the diced onion. Cook the onions until glossy and softened, about 4 minutes.
  • Nudge the onions to the edge of the sauté pan and pour the chicken into the pan, giving each piece of chicken some surface contact with the hot pan.
  • After 5 minutes, flip the chicken and sear on the opposite side, cooking for an additional 5 minutes.
  • Add the minced garlic and deglaze the pan with the chicken broth, stirring the onions and the chicken together. Let that simmer on medium-low for 4 minutes.
  • Add the entire package of frozen cheese tortellini to the simmering chicken and broth mixture. Stir the frozen tortellini until well-combined.
  • Then, add the heavy cream. The frozen tortellini has lowered the temperature of the broth mixture which will lessen the chances of a curdled sauce.
  • Stir everything together and add the shredded Parmesan and frozen spinach. Stir to combine and simmer over medium-low heat for 2 more minutes.
  • The sauce will remain loose but will thicken slightly due to the starch from the cooked tortellini and the shredded cheese. I like the sauce to remain a little loose, because the tortellini will continue to soak up the sauce as it sits. (And I find it’s better for leftovers!) It should lightly coat the back of a spoon.
  • Taste and adjust the salt and pepper to your liking. This is delicious hot, warm, or cold!

Notes

*Boneless, skinless chicken thighs should work well here too. For food safety, check that the chicken reaches an internal temperature of 165°F (74°C), measuring the thickest pieces. **To keep costs low, we use Better Than Bouillon to make our broth. If they are defrosted, they will likely overcook and become mushy. The amount of time required to cook them in this recipe from a frozen state results in perfectly cooked tortellini.

how to make Creamy Chicken and Tortellini Skillet step-by-step photos

Gather and prep your fresh ingredients. Keep the frozen tortellini in the freezer until you’re ready to add it.

Prep the chicken: Dice 1 lb. chicken breast into even, bite-sized pieces, keeping them slightly larger than you might think since they’ll shrink as they cook.

Prep the aromatics: Now dice 1 small yellow onion and mince 3 cloves of garlic.

Season and coat the chicken: Add ½ tsp salt, ½ tsp black pepper, ½ tsp garlic powder, and ½ tsp Italian seasoning to the diced chicken. Toss until every piece is evenly seasoned.

Then sprinkle over ¼ cup all-purpose flour.

Toss again until the chicken is coated on all sides with the flour.

Sauté the onion: Heat 1 Tbsp olive oil and 2 Tbsp salted butter in a large sauté pan over medium heat.

Once the butter has melted and begins to gently bubble, add the diced onion. Cook for about 4 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onion looks glossy, softened, and slightly translucent.

Sear the chicken: Push the onions to the edges of the pan, then add the flour-coated chicken, spreading the pieces out so they contact the hot surface. Cook for about 5 minutes, until the undersides are lightly golden, then flip and cook for another 5 minutes.

Avoid moving the chicken around too much while it sears. Let it sit against the hot pan to develop color and flavor.

Add the broth and garlic: Pour 2 cups chicken broth into the skillet along with the 3 cloves of minced garlic, stirring to combine everything with the chicken and onions. Gently scrape the bottom of the pan to release any flavorful browned bits. Reduce the heat to medium-low and let everything simmer gently for about 4 minutes. The broth should start to look slightly thickened as the flour coating incorporates into the liquid.

Add the frozen tortellini: Pour the entire 19 oz. package of frozen cheese tortellini directly into the simmering chicken and broth mixture. There’s no need to thaw or precook it. Gently stir to distribute the tortellini throughout the skillet without breaking the delicate pasta.

Make it creamy: Pour in ¾ cup room-temperature heavy cream and stir gently to combine. Adding the cream after the frozen tortellini helps because the pasta has slightly cooled the broth, reducing the chance of the sauce curdling. Keep the heat at medium-low so the sauce simmers gently rather than boiling vigorously.

Add the cheese and spinach: Stir in ½ cup shredded Parmesan cheese and 1 cup frozen spinach. Continue simmering gently over medium-low heat for about 2 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the cheese has melted into the sauce and the spinach is heated through. The sauce should look smooth and creamy, with the spinach evenly distributed. Check that the tortellini is tender all the way through, simmering for longer if needed. Make sure the chicken has reached an internal temperature of 165°F.

Check the sauce and serve: The sauce should remain slightly loose but still lightly coat the back of a spoon. Don’t worry if it looks a little thinner than a typical Alfredo sauce! The tortellini will continue absorbing the liquid as it sits, thickening the sauce naturally.

Taste and adjust the salt and pepper to your liking. Serve and enjoy!

Storage & Reheating

Let any leftovers cool, then transfer them to an airtight container and refrigerate within two hours of cooking. They’ll keep for up to 3-4 days. The tortellini will soak up the creamy sauce as it sits (which is why I especially like the sauce to be looser when I know I’ll have leftovers!), so expect it to thicken in the fridge.

Reheating

Warm leftovers in a skillet over medium-low heat. You may need to add a splash of chicken broth or milk to loosen the sauce if the pasta has absorbed a lot. Stir gently until heated through to 165°F. You can also microwave individual portions in short intervals, stirring between each one for even heating.

Freezing

I wouldn’t recommend freezing this one. The cheese-filled tortellini can lose its tender texture, and the cream sauce may separate once thawed. You’ll get much better results keeping any leftovers in the fridge and enjoying them over the next few days.
