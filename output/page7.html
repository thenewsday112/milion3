Brown Butter Financiers

📅 Oct 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Of all the pastries in France, one of my favorites is the financier. Enriched with nuts, and moistened with butter, every bakery in France offers their own version of them. They come in different sizes, shapes, and even flavors; almond is the most popular, but you’ll sometimes come across financiers made with ground hazelnuts or pistachios. I like them all. There are a few theories how this mini-gâteau got its curious name.

One is that, traditionally, they’re baked in small, rectangular molds. Once baked and unmolded, the little cakes resemble bars of gold, which appeals to people in the financial world. Another is that people in France are known to indulge in an afternoon sweet stop at their local bakery, called a goûter. Because people who work in the financial industry normally wear nice outfits or suits, something that’s neat to eat is appreciated, so they can stay presentable when heading back to the office.

I don’t have to worry about that, nor has anything come out of my oven ever turned to gold. (Quelle dommage!) But when I found myself with some leftover brown butter from infusing it in bourbon for Brown Butter Old Fashioneds, since I treat butter like gold, I repurposed it as a base for a batch of financiers.

Using browned butter keeps the butter flavor even more in focus. Some people get a little anxious when they see dark specks in pastries and desserts; I once had a waiter ask me what the dark flecks in the vanilla ice cream were. When I told him they were vanilla beans, and asked him (incredulously) what he thought they were, he replied, “I thought they were dirt.”

So after you brown the butter, when pouring it out of the pan, you don’t want to scrape up too much of the dark bits at the bottom of the pan, but I don’t mind a few in my cakes (in fact, I prefer them). So no need to fret about having a few in your batter. These cakes are also very forgiving, which is why so many bakeries in France offer them. They’re easy to make, keep well, and are the perfect afternoon snack – or as the French say, un snack.

Browned Butter Financiers

For financiers, I use mini-muffin tins, which are easily available. I’m not a fan of silicone bakeware but know that some people like it. This batter is pretty forgiving so can be baked in madeleine molds or even in larger muffin tins, filling them only about halfway. If you use another size mold, you’ll likely need to adjust the baking time; bake them until browned on top, and the feel just set in the center when you touch them. As mentioned, I made these with leftover brown butter from the Brown Butter Old Fashioned recipe. I started with 4 ounces (8 tablespoons/115g) of butter, which yielded the amount called for in the recipe. If starting from scratch, and making your own brown butter, start with that amount of brown butter, then you can measure it out when it’s browned and cooled. (If you need a bit more butter, you can simply add a bit of melted butter to it, to reach the 2 1/2 oz/75g amount. There are links at the end of the post with detailed instructions on making brown butter. Course Dessert Cuisine French Keyword almonds, financiers, french pastry Servings 24 financiers

Ingredients

  • 1 cup (140g) almond or hazelnut flour
  • 3/4 cup plus 2 tablespoons (180g) sugar
  • 5 tablespoons (45g) flour
  • generous pinch salt
  • 4 large egg whites at room temperature
  • 1/2 teaspoon pure vanilla or almond extract
  • 2 1/2 ounces (75g) brown butter slightly warm (liquified)

Instructions

  • Preheat the oven to 375ºF/180ºC and butter the insides of 24 mini muffin tins generously with softened, not melted, butter, making sure to butter the upper rims of the indentations.
  • In a medium bowl, mix the almond or hazelnut powder, sugar, flour and salt. Stir in the egg whites and vanilla or almond extract, then the browned butter.
  • Fill each indentation of the mini muffin tins almost to the top. Rap the tins sharply on the counter to level the tops, then bake for 13 minutes, until nicely browned. Let the financiers cool in the tins, then remove them, using a sharp knife to help release them, if necessary.

Notes

Storage: The financiers can be stored in an air-tight container for up to one week. They can be frozen for up to two months.

This Stuffed Pepper Soup has all the cozy flavors of classic stuffed peppers, with browned, savory ground beef, tender bell peppers, onions, and garlic simmered in a rich, generously seasoned tomato broth. I honestly could not stop eating it! It’s easy, hearty, satisfying, and you get all that familiar flavor without the extra steps of stuffing peppers. I serve it over fluffy rice and, don’t worry, I didn’t forget the cheese! A sprinkle of mozzarella gives you that irresistible cheese pull. Better yet, it’s ready in just 45 minutes and costs only $1.97 per serving. Perfect for soup season, or any season!

Big Flavor from a Simple Shopping List

I already love classic stuffed bell peppers, but this soup makes enjoying those same flavors so much easier on a weeknight. In most stuffed pepper recipes, the rice goes straight into the filling. For this soup, though, I prefer to keep it separate until serving. Rice soaks up liquid like a sponge, and I found that stirring it straight into the pot made the rice mushy. And by the next day, there was barely any broth left! Instead, I like to spoon the rice into individual bowls and ladle the soup over the top. It’s such a simple step, but it makes a big difference…especially when I’m saving leftovers.

I also make the most of my cooking time by getting the rice going while I prepare the soup. Once the ground beef and veggies (bell peppers, onion, and garlic) are cooked, I simmer everything for 15 minutes in a rich tomato broth packed with herbs. And, of course, I wanted to keep this soup easy on the grocery budget, too. I use green bell peppers (which are usually cheaper than red, yellow, or orange), canned tomatoes, dried herbs, and Better Than Bouillon for the broth. Nothing fancy or expensive, but plenty of flavor! These simple ingredients help me stretch just one pound of ground beef into six hearty servings. 😋

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Let those peppers stand out! I cut my bell peppers and onion into a medium dice because I like them to have some texture and really stand out in the finished soup. Keep the pieces roughly the same size so they cook evenly. After the sauté and 15-minute simmer, the peppers should be tender enough to pierce with a fork but still hold their shape. If they’re a little firm, give the soup a few more minutes.
  2. Remove the excess grease. Depending on the type of ground beef you use (I use 27% fat), quite a bit of grease can collect in the pot as it cooks. Make sure to carefully blot up the excess with a paper towel so your soup doesn’t end up oily. You can also spoon the grease into a heatproof container, let it cool, and discard it in the trash. Just don’t pour it down the sink!
  3. Make it as brothy as you like. Prefer a slightly thinner soup? Stir in a splash of extra beef broth or water until you’re happy with the consistency. If there’s more liquid than you’d like, let it simmer uncovered for a few extra minutes, stirring occasionally. I recommend tasting and adjusting the seasoning before serving.
  4. Got extra veggies? Throw them in! This soup is a great way to use up whatever you’ve got. Diced carrots or celery can go in with the peppers and onion, although carrots may need a little longer to soften. Corn can be added with the tomatoes and broth, and fresh spinach only needs a minute or two at the end. You can even add diced potatoes (I’d dice them small, like in our vegetable beef soup), but they’ll need a longer simmer and possibly some extra broth!

Budget-Saving Tip

When I make stuffed peppers at home, I typically use ground beef. However, the ground beef makes up about half the cost of this entire pot of soup, so it’s also a great place to save some money. Feel free to use ground chicken, turkey, pork, or sausage instead. (You can even try lentils!)

Higher-fat ground beef is often cheaper than lean, and it’s totally fine to use here. Just drain off the excess fat after browning so your soup doesn’t end up oily. If you still want some beef in your soup but need to stretch your budget further, try ½ lb. ground beef with 1 cup cooked brown or green lentils. Brown the beef as directed, then stir in the cooked lentils with the tomatoes and broth to heat through during the final simmer. We use a similar meat-stretching trick in our lentil and sausage stew, and it’s a great way to make a little meat go further.

how to make Stuffed Pepper Soup step-by-step photos

Gather all of your ingredients: Cut 2 green bell peppers into a medium dice, finely dice 1 onion, and mince 2 garlic cloves. I like to get the chopping done first since the rice and soup cook at the same time.

Cook the rice: Add 1 cup uncooked long-grain white rice and 2 cups water to a medium saucepan with a lid. Bring to a boil, then reduce the heat to a gentle simmer. Cover and cook for 20 minutes, or until the rice is tender and the water has been absorbed. Turn off the heat and set aside. (This is the same technique we use in our ‘how to cook rice’ guide).

Brown the ground beef: While the rice simmers, heat 1 Tbsp vegetable oil in a large pot over medium heat. Once the oil is hot, add 1 lb. ground beef and cook for about 5 minutes, breaking it into small crumbles as it cooks. The beef should be cooked through, with no pink remaining.

Remove the excess grease: Carefully blot up any excess grease from the beef using paper towels. This helps prevent an oily layer from forming on top of the finished soup.

Soften the vegetables: Add the 2 diced green bell peppers, 1 diced onion, 2 minced garlic cloves, 1½ tsp salt, and 1 tsp black pepper to the pot with the beef. Cook for 5 minutes, stirring occasionally. The onions and peppers should start to soften but still have some texture.

Add the tomatoes and seasonings: Pour in one 14.5 oz. can of diced tomatoes (including the juices), one 15 oz. can of tomato sauce, and 2 cups beef broth. Add 1 tsp dried oregano, ½ tsp dried basil, ¼ tsp dried parsley, and ¼ tsp paprika. Stir everything together until the seasonings are evenly distributed throughout the tomato broth.

Simmer the soup: Bring the mixture to a gentle simmer and cook for 15 minutes to allow the flavors to develop and the broth to thicken slightly. The bell peppers should be tender enough to pierce easily with a fork while still holding their shape. If they’re still too firm, let the soup simmer a little longer.

Serve with rice and cheese: Fluff the cooked rice with a fork and divide it evenly among six serving bowls (about ½ cup per bowl).

Ladle a heaping cup of stuffed pepper soup over each portion of rice, then sprinkle with the shredded mozzarella (1 cup total). Serve warm and enjoy! (For a bonus, I sprinkled fresh parsley on top. Most fresh herbs would be a great garnish, like basil, thyme, or chives, or you can enjoy the recipe as is!)

Storage & Reheating

I recommend storing any leftover rice and soup separately because the rice will continue absorbing the broth as it sits. Let everything cool promptly, then transfer to separate airtight containers and refrigerate for up to 3-4 days.

Reheating

Reheat the soup in a saucepan over medium heat, stirring occasionally, or in the microwave until heated through. Warm the rice separately in the microwave (with a splash of water, stirring every 15-30 seconds until hot), then spoon the soup over the top. If you’ve already mixed the rice into the soup and it’s become too thick, add a splash of beef broth or water to thin it out as it reheats.

Freezing

You can freeze leftover stuffed pepper soup for up to 3 months in freezer-safe containers. I recommend freezing the rice separately to keep it from getting mushy. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator, or use the thaw setting on your microwave, then reheat thoroughly before serving.

Has Substitution Culture Gone Too Far?

📅 Oct 22, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

This excerpt was originally published in Pre Shift, our newsletter for the hospitality industry. Subscribe for more first-person accounts, advice, and interviews.

When Chanita Chayaluk decided to open her restaurant and wine bar Taan, she hoped “to showcase Thai food in Chicago in a different light,” she says. “The first [wave of] immigration of Thai people in Chicago was in the 1970s, and a lot of [Thai] food here still feels like it’s from that time.” In September, she opened with a menu including miang, a Northern Thai dish made with fermented tea leaves, and a whole, deep-fried fish in a fish-sauce caramel. 

Another quirk of operating in Chicago? “We’re still pretty ‘Midwest nice,’” says Chayaluk. “We try to overaccommodate and appease the masses.” In the early days of service, guests made frequent requests for substitutions or accommodations due to preferences and dietary restrictions. “It would slow down service and speed, and that was not just affecting us, but the guest experience in many ways,” she says. With a small kitchen and staff, it was hard to keep up. “It just felt like we were Chipotle.” When a guest asked for green curry mussels “but without the mussels,” it was a turning point. 

Chayaluk implemented a limited substitution policy at Taan, stating on Instagram that ingredients like fish sauce and shrimp paste are “the fabric that defines our cuisine,” and therefore, could not be removed from any dishes that include it. 

A decade or so ago, substitution culture was generally frowned upon and derided. Recently, though, some restaurants report it bubbling back up—and in full force. There’s a swirl of factors that got us here: Lunch slop bowl spots primed customers to expect a detailed level of customization with their orders. Unreasonable Hospitality pushed an intensified “customer is always right” mentality. And influencers entering the food-review space have made criticism of anything—including a restaurant choosing not to honor certain dietary preferences—ripe for an immediate, viral takedown. Couple all of that with the fact that it is hard to keep a restaurant afloat these days, and you’ve got several operators giving in, whether to specific requests, or to altering their menus altogether (see also: the great de-spicification of menus.)

At the same time, restaurants like Taan and New York’s Ha’s Snack Bar—which proudly announces in its Instagram bio that everything contains fish sauce—are standing their ground.

For Lom Wong in Phoenix, Arizona, substitution and modification requests have ramped up after receiving national attention. Chef Yotaka “Sunny” Martin was recognized by the James Beard Awards last year, and the restaurant was named a Michelin Bib Gourmand in August. “[After the award,] we had more guests than we were used to picking things apart. They were asking us to modify dishes or not understanding our service style and why we weren’t coursing things in a European fashion,” says chef and co-owner Alex Martin. “[There is a] European distinction of what an award-winning restaurant looks like, so people equated that recognition with a white-tablecloth restaurant. We were just like, ‘Well, that’s not who we’ve ever been, and it’s never what our restaurant will ever be.’”

Like Taan, Lom Wong took to social media to set expectations, including that the restaurant would not “make substitutions simply to make a dish more familiar,” and that certain dishes might differ from Western expectations of them; beef might have gristle, fishes will be whole, and both choices are fully intentional. “Sunny is our guiding light of cultural context,” says Martin. “[She advises, considering if] changing something about a dish compromises the integrity of the dish.” For example, the mango salad can be ordered with the shrimp on the side, but the restaurant won’t remove beef or shrimp paste from dishes that necessitate them. 

Chayaluk takes a similar approach at Taan; there is always a vegan, gluten-free option (currently a fried rice) that she describes as a “catchall” for those who can’t have other items on the menu. “We make sure that that dish is also really, really good and substantial; it’s not just an appetizer,” she says. “It’s not an afterthought.” 

Staff at both restaurants are encouraged to uphold the boundaries, which both Chayaluk and Martin say are as much in place for protecting their staff’s peace as they are for ensuring quality control for guests. While there has been some backlash, the expectation-setting at the start of the meal (and before, thanks to the social media posts) has for the most part been taken well. “The way we explain [our policy] to guests is kindly saying we respect your preferences, but they’re your preferences,” says Martin, “and steering them toward dishes on the menu that might already align with those.”

Cooking polenta in modern times doesn’t have much in common with the way it was prepared for centuries in the regions that now make up Northern Italy. Polenta was traditionally cooked on a grand scale in copper cauldrons over open fire. There was a lot of stirring, ladling, and creative, resourceful uses of leftovers. Historically it was considered one of the humblest foods and when I came across this line about polenta in Honey From a Weed by Patience Gray (1986), it stuck with me. “The idea of polenta, planted firmly in the north Italian imagination, is the antithesis of modern aspirations. It therefore intrudes on modern life like a vague emotional regret.”

But, polenta is wonderful. And similar to other foods that were cast off in exchange for industrialized, refined counterparts – brown rice, naturally leavened breads, etc. — many people find their way back to the “old ways” eventually.

What is Polenta?

Think of polenta as a cornmeal porridge of sorts. In The Classic Italian Cook Book (1973), Marcella Hazan asserts the importance of polenta by saying, “…to call polenta a cornmeal mush is a most indelicate use of language. In country kitchens, polenta was more than food, it was a rite. It was made daily in an unlined copper kettle, the paiolo, which was always kept hanging at the ready on a hook in the center of the fireplace.” She goes on to describe a wonderful scene where, “…there was a moment of joy as it [polenta] was poured out in a steaming golden circle on the beechwood top of the madia…”

About This Polenta Recipe

Polenta is a regular preparation in my kitchen, and it can be cooked a number of ways. Not just in a cauldron lol.  I jump back and forth between stovetop polenta and oven-baked polenta depending on a number of factors. Today I’m going to talk through my technique for making beautifully creamy polenta on the stovetop. We’ll get to the oven-baked version in a future post. That one is great if you just want to combine ingredients in a baking dish, push it into the oven, and forget about it until the timer goes off. But it’s not as creamy. In either case, the ingredient list is short, and most of your attention should be focused on sourcing some great cornmeal / polenta. Beyond that, a bit of patience, and technique is all you need. I love it served topped generously with this mushroom ragù as you’ll see down below. 

Which Polenta (a.k.a. cornmeal or grits) Should I Use?

Look for stone-ground, whole grain, whole kernel polenta without any additives – preferably organic. Store-bought, quick-cooking, refined polenta is the starchy endosperm of the corn kernel, and much of the flavor from the bran and germ is stripped out in the milling process. These are a few favorites to look for.

  • Hayden Flour Mills Yellow Corn Polenta:  My local market carries Hayden and this is the polenta I purchase most often. It is coarse grind, so it takes a bit longer to cook, but the flavor and texture is wonderful. (Pictured above right)
  • Bob’s Red Mill Organic Medium Grind Cornmeal: Stone-ground, whole grain. Readily available and a solid choice. If I’m looking for a polenta that will cook a bit faster, this is the one to grab because the corn is medium coarse and a bit finer. (Pictured above left)
  • Rancho Gordo Fine Yellow Polenta:  If you like a finer grind, this is your choice. Organic, heirloom corn grown & ground by Anson Mills for RG. IYKYK.

Whole polenta is quite perishable. Use it quickly or store it in the freezer.

Do I Have to Stir Polenta with a Wooden Spoon?

If you read through old polenta recipes, many will mandate stirring with a wooden spoon. It’s a good suggestion, but your polenta will be just fine if you use a stainless steel spoon instead. That said, wood spoons are kinder to your pots, less likely to scratch (especially if your pots have any sort of coating). And, some metal spoons (especially very old ones) can react to acidic ingredients resulting in a metallic taste. If you’re making tomato sauce or sourdough, for example.

Polenta Variations

Knowing how to make a great pot of polenta sets you up with a foundation to keep it simple and straight-forward (like the creamy polenta recipe below), or to add your own twists and flavors. Here are a few ways you can switch things up.

  • Use broth: Instead of water as the base liquid in your polenta experiment with broths, teas, or other infusions. I often make mine with a favorite mushroom tea (topping the polenta with these baked mushrooms.
  • Make it herby: Polenta has a real affinity for certain herbs. My favorite is chives. Double down with a couple handfuls of snipped chives folded in along with some chive compound butter, near the end of cooking so the chives retain their brightness.

What to Do with Leftover Polenta

There are many things to do with leftover polenta. Patience Gray includes this wonderful paragraph (Honey From A Weed, 1986) that exemplifies the cycle of a pot of polenta, “…made one day to serve hot, what is left is sliced and fried in hot oil till golden (polenta fritta), or brushed with oil and grilled (polenta ai ferri), to eat the next day. If chopped salame, parmesan cheese, butter and egg yolks are added to the polenta once it is stiff, and, when it has cooled, the beaten egg whites are folded in, and the preparation put into an oiled oven dish, what emerges after 45 minutes in a hot oven is a torta di polenta.”

Here are a couple other ideas:

  • Baked Polenta:  Slice into strips, brush with olive oil and pan-fry or bake at 350°F into golden, tasty slabs. Serve topped with mushroom ragù.
  • Polenta in Sourdough: This is a favorite polenta-centric baking tip. Crumble cooked (and cooled) polenta into sourdough prior to baking. I tend to do this after my second set of folds. Alternately, you can fold into quick-bread batters.

The Eggs Scene in &#8216;You Can See Everything&#8217; Scrambles Reality

📅 Oct 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This review contains spoilers for the film You Can See Everything.

A version of this post originally appeared in Eater Today, which spotlights the freshest news and stories from across the food world every day. Subscribe now.

I saw You Can See Everything — the new documentary about disgraced Theranos founder Elizabeth Holmes, co-directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim — on Thursday, and I still feel like my brain is trying to punch its way out of a bag, or like I’ve just awakened from an Inception-esque dream within a dream. It’s difficult to describe the winding, twisting, nearly three-hour film to someone unfamiliar with its many layers of context, from Fielder’s Dadaist, boundaryless approach to his subjects to Holmes’s hypnotic affect, which is either highly effective gaslighting or blinding, self-deluded earnestness. (Which is it? I still don’t know.)

What happens when you take two unreliable narrators — make that three, if you include Holmes’s partner, Billy Evans, whose on-camera persona morphs from protective, naive tech bro to unscrupulous grifter — put them in a jar, and shake them? The result is that the more questions are asked, the further the truth drifts out of reach. It’s like staring at a Magic Eye image, slightly crossing your eyes, desperate to make out the hidden 3D subject but never quite managing to see it. Eventually, you start worrying your eyes might get stuck that way.

The discourse surrounding the film is already abundant, but we’re here to talk about the eggs.

If you’re familiar with the hallmarks of Fielderian programming, you know that Fielder is prone to hyperfixating on seemingly mundane details that later return to completely upend viewers’ expectations. In You Can See Everything, one such detail is observed in an exchange between Holmes and Evans as they make eggs. How much can be gleaned from watching someone make breakfast? A lot, apparently.

In the first half of the film, Fielder is fully embedded in the rental home of Holmes and Evans, who have invited the filmmakers to document the final weeks before Holmes reports to federal prison. (How and why they agreed to this arrangement is incomprehensible if they knew anything about Fielder’s work.) During this time, the narrative takes a surprisingly sympathetic turn for Holmes, who seems intent on convincing the filmmakers — and Fielder specifically — that she is actually a sweet, misunderstood victim of circumstance, not a Machiavellian con woman who defrauded investors. Frankly, aspects of this claim feel convincing at times. Viewers are invited to witness her ostensibly less guarded moments, including a scene in which she makes breakfast with Evans. As Fielder interviews her, Holmes somewhat unenthusiastically and limply beats a bowl of eggs with a fork. The camera lingers on her feeble whisking technique. Then, after a few seconds, another set of hands enters the frame: those of Evans. Without a word, he takes the bowl and fork from Holmes and begins whipping the eggs with considerably greater fervor.

Now, backseat driving in the kitchen is hardly uncommon among couples, though it feels like a fast track to bickering. (Evans tells Fielder that he and Holmes “never fight”; Holmes is also emphatic that she’s never lied in her life. Again, unreliable narrators.) What’s less common — or at least generally considered pretty rude and aggressive — is grabbing something out of your partner’s hands mid-task to take over. Fielder points this out, asking Evans why he felt the need to grab the eggs; Holmes explains that Evans doesn’t like the way she mixes them and thinks he can do it better. 

It’s a tiny interaction, but a remarkably revealing one. Evans feels comfortable taking control of something Holmes is doing, without so much as asking, and seems largely unaware of how his behavior might come across on camera. Holmes, meanwhile, appears accustomed to the intrusion, offering an explanation rather than an objection. (Granted, unlike sustained and unblinking eye contact, beating eggs does not appear to be one of Ms. Holmes’s strong suits. Typically, one does so vigorously to aerate them and make them fluffier for an omelet or scramble.)

But with this being Elizabeth Holmes, we must also ask: Could it be a case of weaponized incompetence? Is it possible that, not wanting the task of beating the eggs, she intentionally does so insufficiently to make Evans take over? Is she being controlled, or is she getting someone else to do her work for her? Or is this evidence of her “different” way of thinking, that she truly doesn’t know the “right” way to beat them? 

In interviews in the film, Holmes portrays the Theranos years as a period during which she was manipulated by her former boyfriend and business partner, Sunny Balwani, and maintains that her intentions were ultimately good for humanity, regardless of the consequences for investors and patients who received unreliable test results. Whether she actually believes this account of events, or simply knows how to sell it, remains one of the film’s central mysteries — as does the extent to which her romantic relationships lead her to take the fall for the actions of bad worse men.

These questions reverberate in the second half of the film, when — with Holmes now in prison — we learn some unsettling things about Evans. He has power of attorney over Holmes, and has been using her X account to post odd, profanity-laced remarks that seem entirely incongruent with her own manner of speaking. Suddenly, the eggs scene looks like an early indication that Evans is far more domineering than the protective-partner persona he’s been presenting. His ostensible vigilance toward Holmes may be genuine on some level, but it’s difficult to reconcile with his willingness to license her image for billboards promoting another (bizarre-seeming) entrepreneur’s Theranos obsession, or his apparent acceptance of the possibility that her AI-generated likeness could be used to create pornography or other content antithetical to her interests. 

And yet, even as Evans emerges as an increasingly unsavory character, Holmes remains inscrutable. Does she know what he’s doing? Does she approve? Is he exploiting her circumstances, or are they working toward a shared objective — reputational, monetary, or otherwise? The film offers kaleidoscopic evidence for competing interpretations, including that even if Holmes is being dominated, she may be content with that dynamic. 

What’s extraordinary about Fielder and Oppenheim’s documentary is its optical-illusion-like layers of “truth.” Despite the film’s many harrowing close-ups of microexpressions, there is what Holmes and Evans say, then there is what they do, or have done, or plan to do (launch a Theranos 2.0 called Haemanthus, apparently). There is also Fielder’s own history as a provocateur, a faux-earnest magician who will go to virtually any lengths to actualize a concept, and who seems remarkably capable of keeping a stone-cold straight face through any scenario. 

We know enough about Fielder to question his sincerity, too. There are several moments in the film where I — and the several friends I went to see the film with — wondered if there was palpable sexual chemistry between him and Holmes, and whether Fielder would act on it. If he did, we couldn’t agree on whether it would be genuine or an act of performance art, or whether he has any barriers between those things. 

The film’s most revealing moments are the ones that feel off-script for everyone involved: a fleeting facial expression, an odd word choice in an otherwise emphatic statement, a series of gestures exchanged over a bowl of scrambled eggs. The subjects, who have invited themselves to be watched, have few moments when they seem to forget that; those instances are when we ostensibly have the clearest insight into their reality. Frankly, it’s still murky, and that seems to be both the point and the irony in the film’s title.

You Can See Everything elevates Nathan Fielder’s reputation far beyond simply a master of cringe humor; there is, here, a glimmer of genius. This story feels almost fateful in its ability to showcase his gifts, illuminating the bottomless strangeness, the infinite complexities, of how humans interact with one another. Even when an idea is big, it’s the little things that matter the most. And in a documentary that promises you can see everything, we learn that it’s up to us to look closely — and even then, what we see might look different in another light.

I love pork chops, and I feel like they’re under appreciated. Some people think pork chops are dry and boring. Let me tell you, I completely disagree! These Smothered Pork Chops are a Southern staple, packed with flavor and seared to perfection. And the best part? Grab some mashed potatoes because you’re going to want every last drop of that gravy!

A Good Sear Makes an Even Better Gravy

Everyone has their own way of making Southern smothered pork chops, and this is mine! My secret is seasoning, dredging, and searing the pork chops over medium-high heat first. The light flour coating helps create a delicious, slightly crisp golden crust, and all those tasty browned bits left behind in the skillet add even more flavor to the homemade gravy. I always set the chops aside while I make the gravy, too. Those lean cuts can overcook quickly, and nobody wants tough pork chops for dinner!

Then, don’t even get me started on the gravy. I make it from scratch with onions and garlic (the backbone of any good sauce, if you ask me! 😉). I give the onions time to soften and start browning, bringing out their natural sweetness and adding another layer of flavor to the sauce. A little flour helps thicken everything up, then chicken broth (I use Better Than Bouillon to save money) and a splash of heavy cream give the gravy a rich, luxurious finish. Comfort on a plate without spending a fortune! If you’re a gravy lover like me, you’ll also want to try our Salisbury steak next. It’s another budget-friendly favorite with plenty of homemade gravy.

Melissa Nolan serving her smothered pork chops in our Nashville test kitchen.

Recipe Success Tips

  1. Keep those pork chops juicy! I use boneless pork chops in this recipe, which are fairly lean, so they can dry out quickly. I recommend using an instant-read thermometer to check the thickest part of each chop, especially if yours are thicker than the ones I’ve used. Pork is safely cooked at 145°F, followed by a 3-minute rest. Set the chops aside while you make the gravy, then return them to the skillet briefly to warm through so they don’t overcook.
  2. Give the pork chops room to brown. Let the chops sit at room temperature for 10-15 minutes to help take the chill off. Then, pat them dry with paper towels before seasoning and dredging, and make sure the skillet is hot before adding them. If your chops don’t fit comfortably in one layer, cook them in batches! Crowding the pan traps moisture, making it harder to develop that lovely golden crust. Watch the heat, too, as the flour coating can burn if the pan gets too hot.
  3. Get the gravy consistency right. The butter and reserved flour form a roux, a mixture of roughly equal parts fat and flour that thickens the gravy. Cook the flour for about a minute to get rid of its raw taste. Then stir in the chicken broth, scraping up those flavorful browned bits from the pan as you go. Let the gravy simmer until it’s thick enough to coat the back of a spoon. Too thick? Add a splash more broth. Too thin? Let it simmer a little longer before returning the pork.
  4. Taste before adding more salt. I season my pork with salt, pepper, and paprika, but garlic powder or onion powder are great additions, too! Remember that chicken broths can vary a lot in saltiness. I always taste the gravy after it has thickened before deciding whether it needs more seasoning.
  5. Add some extras! I kept my recipe simple with onions and garlic, but sliced mushrooms are a delicious addition, as I use in my creamy pork chops recipe. Cook them with the onions until the excess moisture evaporates. You can even add bacon!

how to make Smothered Pork Chops step-by-step photos

Gather and prepare your ingredients. Let the pork chops sit out at room temperature for 10-15 minutes to take the chill off. Slice 1 large yellow onion (about 2 cups) and mince 2 garlic cloves (about 1 Tbsp). Measure out the remaining ingredients so everything is ready to go.

Season the pork chops: Heat 1 Tbsp vegetable oil in a large cast-iron skillet over medium-high heat. Meanwhile, pat 4 boneless center-cut pork chops dry with paper towels. Season both sides evenly with ¼ tsp salt, ¼ tsp black pepper, and ½ tsp paprika.

Dredge the pork chops: Spread ¼ cup all-purpose flour on a plate. Lightly coat each pork chop on both sides, shaking off any excess flour. Reserve the remaining flour to help thicken the gravy later.

Sear the pork chops: Once the oil is hot, carefully add the chops to the skillet in a single layer. Cook undisturbed for 3-4 minutes per side, until a golden-brown crust develops. Avoid moving them too much so the coating can brown nicely. Check that the thickest part of each chop reaches an internal temperature of 145°F. Transfer the chops to a plate and set aside while you make the gravy.

Sauté the onion: Reduce the heat to medium and add the sliced yellow onion to the same skillet. Cook for about 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the onion softens and turns golden around the edges.

Add the butter and garlic: Stir in 2 Tbsp butter and the minced 2 cloves of garlic. Cook for about 1 minute, stirring frequently, until the butter has melted and the garlic smells fragrant. Be careful not to let the garlic burn, as it can make the gravy taste bitter.

Make the roux: Sprinkle 2 Tbsp of the reserved flour over the onion mixture. Stir continuously for about 1 minute, until the flour is fully incorporated.

Make the creamy onion gravy: Add 1 cup chicken broth, ¼ cup heavy cream, ¼ tsp dried thyme, and the remaining ½ tsp salt and ¼ tsp black pepper. Stir well, scraping up any browned bits from the bottom of the skillet. Let the gravy simmer for about 5 minutes, stirring occasionally, until it’s smooth and thick enough to coat the back of a spoon.

Smother the pork chops! Return the pork chops and any juices collected on the plate to the skillet. Spoon the creamy onion gravy over the top and heat briefly until warmed through. Enjoy!

What to Serve with Smothered Pork Chops

All this creamy onion gravy deserves something delicious to soak it up! My first choice is a big scoop of mashed potatoes. They’re fluffy, buttery, and perfect for catching every last spoonful of gravy. I’d start the potatoes before cooking the pork chops, since they take about 25 minutes to prepare. For an even cheaper option, serve the chops over white rice. It takes about 25 minutes, too, and can simmer away with the lid on while you work on the pork. Or, for a Southern-inspired dinner, try our cheese grits. They’re cheesy and ready in just 15 minutes, so you can get them going on another burner once you’ve seared the chops.

To round out the meal, I recommend some sautéed green beans. Their garlicky flavor and tender-crisp texture are lovely alongside the creamy gravy, and they add some extra veggies to your plate. They only take about 10 minutes to cook once prepped, so you can sauté them in a separate skillet while the onions and gravy come together. That way, everything is ready to serve at roughly the same time.

Storage & Reheating

Store leftover smothered pork chops in an airtight container in the fridge for up to 3 days. I recommend keeping the chops covered with plenty of gravy to help prevent them from drying out. Refrigerate within 2 hours of cooking.

Reheating

I prefer reheating these pork chops in a covered skillet over medium-low heat to help keep them tender. The gravy will thicken as it cools, so add a splash of chicken broth or milk to loosen it up. Heat the gravy thoroughly and warm the pork chops until they reach an internal temperature of 165°F. Try not to heat them any longer than necessary, as boneless pork chops can become tough when reheated.

Freezing

I wouldn’t freeze these smothered pork chops. The heavy cream in the gravy can separate or turn grainy after thawing, and the pork chops may lose some tenderness.
