Steal My Food Itinerary: 3 Days on Prince Edward Island

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →
Raspberry Point oysters at Landmark Oyster House on Prince Edward Island. | Kate Heddings

Recently I spent a few days exploring Prince Edward Island, a place that embraces its local foodways with gusto. There’s a complex history here, with roots in Indigenous Mi’kmaq, Acadian, Irish, Scottish, and other cultures. And if one thing unites these histories, it’s the island’s abundance of seafood and produce, which have helped earn it the nickname Canada’s Food Island. How can you not want to visit Canada’s Food Island?

On a stretch of land only about 140 miles long, you can eat oysters pulled out of nearby waters, potatoes grown on local farms, dig into a juicy burger made with PEI beef, and lick ice cream made with milk from the cows you pass along those famous red-dirt roads. That’s a lot of good eating packed into a place you can drive across in an afternoon.

I had to structure my visit, therefore, around where to dine, but also include a bucket-list visit to Green Gables Heritage Place. (I am a complete freak for L.M. Montgomery’s novel Anne of Green Gables, set on PEI around the turn of the last century.) I also had to schedule time at the PEI International Shellfish Festival, which promised endless trays of freshly shucked oysters and clams, hot buttered lobster rolls, steaming bowls of mussels, and plenty of entertainment, from shucking competitions and cooking demos to live music and dancing.

My husband and I rented a car and stationed ourselves in Charlottetown, the island’s main hub. Prince Edward Island is situated in the Gulf of St. Lawrence, just a hop away from Nova Scotia. You’re never far from the ocean here, and on a sunny day, the air seems to sparkle. The island is known for its red-sand cliffs and beaches, rolling landscapes, fishing harbors, and lighthouses. If you’ve read Anne of Green Gables, you’ll know just how pretty PEI is.

We landed late Thursday afternoon, ready to hit the ground running. With just shy of three days, I had carefully mapped out the places I wanted to visit, using Charlottetown as my base. 

Day 1

Drinks and snacks: Slaymaker & Nichols

Charlottetown isn’t big, and most things here are walkable within 20 minutes or so. First stop in PEI: Slaymaker & Nichols, a gastropub and inn with the feel of an old-fashioned supper club. It takes its name from the 19th-century circus that once performed on the site. They take cocktail making seriously here. The summery feel of the day prompted me to try the watermelon mule, with hibiscus-infused vodka and chunks of pickled watermelon. I liked it, especially alongside the creamy lobster dip with tomatoes and harissa, and the buttery shrimp pil pil, with lots of tasty little spears of asparagus.

More drinks: Reddins

I was saving myself for my first lobster roll, which we grabbed up the street in an unremarkable spot. It was pretty mediocre, so I won’t go on about it. But after eating, we stopped into Reddins, a speakeasy that channels PEI’s Prohibition history in a dark, intimate third-floor bar with vintage furniture, good jazz, and whimsical cocktails. Our excellent bartender, AB, told us the space had once been a pharmacy where folks came during Prohibition to get their “medicine” — drugstores could legally dispense alcohol by prescription. We tried a few of the signature cocktails, and the one that stood out was the Pink Canary, with vodka, rose, lychee, citrus, and rosé bubbles, served in a small bathtub. Yup, you heard that right. It was sweet and awfully fun to sip. 

Tip: You need a secret password to get in when you buzz from downstairs. You can get it online, or just do as we did and ask the hostess next door for help.

Day 2

Breakfast: Receiver Coffee

In the pouring rain, we made our way to nearby Victoria Row for breakfast at local roaster Receiver Coffee. Fuel for the day included strong coffee, a particularly good cup of Earl Grey cream tea, and the Ham Slam, a big fluffy cheddar biscuit layered with a runny fried egg, Black Forest ham, Swiss, and Dijon mayo.

Lunch and more: Landmark Oyster House, Island Chocolates

We swung by the shellfish festival (more on that later) but the rain wasn’t welcoming, so we got back in the car and drove out to tiny and charming Victoria-by-the-Sea. When I say tiny, I mean it: The town is half a square mile, with a population under 200. Despite the town’s size, Landmark Oyster House, set there in an old general store with pretty green shingles and a steep gabled roof, thrives.


Our friendly waiter (everyone is abundantly friendly on PEI) suggested Raspberry Point oysters to start. They hit the spot: medium-sized, briny, and sweet, served with lemon wedges and freshly grated horseradish, which is now how I think every oyster should be eaten. We also tried the scallop dog, with buttery sweet scallops, bacon jam, red pepper aioli, and pickled shallots nestled in a toasted bun. It came alongside a very good, eggy potato salad. 

After seeing the cheery lighthouse and visiting a couple of little gift shops, we popped into Island Chocolates, a family-run shop and café where bars and truffles are hand-crafted, often using local ingredients like haskap, a berry that tastes somewhere between a blueberry and raspberry. That fruity haskap truffle had a nice thick shell of milk chocolate.  

Sun out? Check. We spent the afternoon at the festival and got a substantial fix of oysters and clams. The lobster rolls here were stuffed with unadorned lobster meat, then melted butter from a gigantic vat was ladled over the top.

Dinner: North Shore Canteen

For dinner, both our bartender and waiter at Slaymaker recommended North Shore Canteen in French River, a local haunt for sure. (A slew of chefs from the shellfish festival were two tables down from us.) The menu, which changes with the current haul, hit on the day’s best ingredients: halibut, striped bass, the aforementioned shellfish, beef, and potatoes. We opted for a very generous portion of local beef tartare, amazingly tasty with capers, roasted garlic, and freshly grated Parmesan, served with Covered Bridge potato chips. We wanted to try more PEI beef and opted for a juicy hanger steak with chimichurri and black garlic, which showcased it well. Dessert was a must: parsnip cake drizzled with caramel sauce for the win.

Day 3

It was time for me to check Green Gables off my bucket list. This is an article about where to eat, so suffice it to say that if you’re a fan, this is 100 percent worth a visit to see the house, wander the Haunted Wood and Lover’s Lane, and taste some nonalcoholic raspberry cordial (IYKYK).

All that walking made me hungry, which was good because this was a much anticipated two-lunch day.

Lunch x 2: Blue Mussel Café and Richard’s Fresh Seafood

First stop: Blue Mussel Café, a bright and very welcoming, beloved PEI restaurant set just back from the water in Rustico Harbor. I think it’s obvious what to order, and for good reason: These are excellent mussels — plump, fresh, and brought to the table piled high in a large stainless steel bowl. You can have them prepared with rosemary and lemon or beer and wine. I’d rate them equally, but I did like the hit of rosemary. Melted butter comes in a small ramekin on the side; I used it to dip each mussel, and my husband poured it all over the top. I didn’t have it, but the other signature, seafood chowder, looked popular. It’s made with haddock, salmon, scallops, mussels, and PEI potatoes, and served with a giant biscuit. Noted for my next visit.

We hightailed it to our next meal because I had yet to have the perfect lobster roll. Richard’s Fresh Seafood in Covehead Wharf delivered. This is one of the island’s longest-running fish shacks, where you can savor fish and chips and beer on the deck overlooking the fishing boats, or camp out on the nearby beach under the lighthouse with a lobster roll. no mistake: This is the quintessential lobster roll. It was an exercise in abundance, packed with sweet lobster meat that managed to be chunky and fine, bound with lemon and chive mayo and tiny bits of onion, all stuffed in a buttery toasted bun. It came with crunchy coleslaw, crispy fries, and a paper cup of ketchup. There are other menu items, but this lobster roll alone is worth the trip to PEI.

Dinner: Rustico Cannery

For our final dinner, we drove back toward North Rustico Harbor to Rustico Cannery, which opened this past spring. The airy, modern space has giant windows and a big deck that takes in super sunset views.

I needed greens, and Rustico delivered. We had crisp romaine hearts with a creamy, spicy charred jalapeño dressing, lardons, Parmesan, and sourdough crunchies. My husband hit the jackpot with his dinner: panko crusted, pan-fried haddock with pickled beet remoulade, jalapeño relish, confit potatoes, and coleslaw.

Ice Cream: Cows Creamery

I’d be remiss if I didn’t include our multiple visits to Cows Creamery, the island’s local ice cream shop, which — conveniently — has locations across PEI. It sources the milk for the ice cream from small, local dairy farms and the results are winning!  A few standouts were Coffee Cowrunch (English toffee and Oreo), Cownadian Maple (a triple hit of maple ice cream, maple swirl, and maple cookie chunks; when in Rome), and Messie Bessie, chocolate ice cream with Oreo, toffee, and Cow Chips — the thick, ruffled local potato chips covered with rich milk or dark chocolate that are for sale by the bag all over the island.

A final note: Many places are seasonal, so check if they’re open if you’re visiting in cooler months.

Lamb Tagine recipe

📅 Oct 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the first lessons I learned, right after I moved to Paris, was to never, ever be on time for a dinner party. When i got my first invitation to a dinner party, my hosts told me, “Come at 8pm…which in Paris, means to come at 8:20 or 8:30pm.”

Subsequently, when I have guests for dinner, I expect them to be at least 20 minutes late, although there’s much debate on how late you’re actually supposed to be. But if you’re on time, or early, your host likely won’t not be ready and you’ll probably catch them by surprise.

It’s a tricky balance when you’ve inviting people for dinner, since you want make sure when everyone’s at the table, dinner will be ready and perfectly cooked. If your friends show up very late, and you’ve made something like pork roast, which can take a while to bake, but will dry out if overcooked, you’re sunk.

So one technique I rely on is braising. Braising is cooking something long and slow, which softens cuts of meat and poultry, and infuses whatever you’re cooking with flavor. In the case of a tagine, a North African dish that benefits from braising, your cooking the meat in a broth of onions, aromatics, and spices, which all come together after a stint in the over to make a perfect one-pot meal, that’s can be kept warm in the oven until everyone is at the table.

In Paris, with so many Arabic and North African butchers around, it’s easy to find cuts of meat that lend themselves to slow-braising, and tagines are quite popular in France. When I wrote my book, My Paris Kitchen, someone wrote to me, wondering why I had non-French recipes in my book. Just like people in other countries, such as Americans who make Mexican and Asian foods at home, or Dutch people who eat rijsttafel, an Indonesian meal composed of many side dishes, French people regularly eat a variety of foods from other cultures, especially in Paris, which is a melting pot, where you can enjoy couscous in Belleville, dosas in the Indian quarter, and Vietnamese noodles in the thirteenth arrondissement. But really, you can get all those foods in just about every corner of the city.

Tagines are simple to make, and once you’ve made one, you’ll wonder what took you so long to make one? You’re basically just browning the meat in a Dutch oven or large pot, then sautéing an onion with spices and seasonings, adding a few spoonfuls of tomato paste or a can of tomatoes, some stock or water, then braising it in the over for a few hours.

This is my basic recipe that I make often. It works great with a lamb shoulder, which you can have your butcher cut into pieces for you. If you can only get a whole lamb shoulder, you can skip the sautéing step and just braise the lamb shoulder whole. If you can get lamb shanks, this recipe works well with four of them.

I like to serve it with pearl couscous, which you can see above. It’s basically pasta pearls, and goes by several different names. Regular couscous is also an option, as is rice-shaped pasta, known as orzo

Lamb Tagine

You can substitute a cut up chicken for the lamb. If using chicken, reduce the oven time to 1 to 1½ hours. I also like to add prunes, raisins or dried apricots or cranberries to the tagine, during the last 20 minutes of cooking. You can vary the spices depending on what you have or can get. The saffron is optional since that can be more difficult (and expensive) to obtain, but I like the flavor of it in a tagine. Sometimes I use tomato paste in my tagines, and other times I add a can of crushed tomatoes. I put a jar of harissa, North African hot sauce, on the table, for guests who like a little spice with their tagine. (And hosts like me, who like it too!) A favorite brand of harissa in the U.S. is made by NY Shuk. Course Main Course Cuisine Mediterranean Keyword tagine Servings 6 servings

Ingredients

  • 1 lamb shoulder cut into 4 pieces
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons kosher or sea salt plus more, to taste
  • 1 medium onion minced
  • 2 1/2 cups (600ml) chicken stock or water
  • 1 teaspoon ground ginger
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 2 teaspoons paprika
  • 1 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • freshly-ground black pepper
  • 1 small bunch fresh cilantro tied with a string, plus a few sprigs for garnish
  • a pinch of saffron threads (optional)
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste or one 14 ounce (400g) can crushed tomatoes
  • Harissa for serving

Instructions

  • Massage the pieces of lamb shoulder with the salt.
  • Heat a few tablespoons of oil in a Dutch oven over medium-high heat, and sear the lamb pieces very well, turning them only after they’re nicely browned (this step adds flavor to the tagine.) As you cook the lamb pieces, don’t crowd them in. If your Dutch oven isn’t big enough to cook them all in a single layer at the same time, brown the lamb pieces in batches.
  • Preheat the oven to 350ºF (175ºC). Once the lamb is browned, remove the pieces from the pot and set them on a plate. (If there is a lot of fat in the pan, you can remove some of it, but leave a film of it in the bottom of the pan.) Add the chopped onion to the pot and some of the stock (or water), then scrape the bottom of the pan with a flat wooden spatula to release the flavorful browned bits. Add the spices, the rest of the water, the bunch of cilantro, the saffron, if using, and the tomato paste or crushed tomatoes. Give everything a quick stir to mix everything together.
  • Cover the pot and bake in the oven for 2 1/2 to 3 hours, turning the lamb over in the liquid a few times during baking. The liquid should just be simmering gently. If it’s boiling, turn down the heat. Add more liquid if necessary while it's cooking so the meat is half submerged in liquid. When the meat starts to fall off the bone, that’s when it’s ready. Taste the liquid and add more salt if desired.
  • Pull the meat off the bones. Spoon the meat and some juices into bowls over a bed of couscous or pearl couscous (pasta). Garnish with a sprigs of fresh cilantro.

Notes

To serve, remove the cilantro and discard. Squeeze some lemon juice into the liquid and add more salt, if desired. Serve with rice or bulghur, along with juices from the tagine. At the table, make sure you have a tube of harissa handy, the fiery Moroccan hot sauce.

This is Amused Bouche, SAVEUR’s food questionnaire that explores the culinary curiosities of some of our favorite people. This interview series dives deep into their food routines, including dinner party strategies, cherished cookbooks, and the memorable bites they’d hop on a flight for.

Comedian and actor Hasan Minhaj is known for quippy questions, biting comebacks, and colorful commentary, but his most delightful soundbites are about food. During a recent  hour-long chat, he named a wide range of favorites, including bananas and oranges (“they come prepackaged and are great for traveling”); macaroni and cheese (“I love every category and version of it, from the box of Kraft to anyone’s Thanksgiving mac ’n’ cheese”); and dal chawal (specifically his mom’s version of the lentils and rice dish, “pure comfort food and my love language”).

These are the best-of-the-best bites for Minhaj, which is coincidentally the name of his new movie—Best of the Best, streaming now on Netflix—that he cowrote and produced with Prashanth Venkataramanujam and stars in as a professor and former dancer. The film pays homage to real-life Bollywood competitions, following collegiate dancers Maya and Anjali (played by Maitreyi Ramakrishnan and Priyanka Kedia, respectively), whose characters were inspired by “deeply passionate college kids in the biggest world that a lot of people have never heard of.”

Fiery passion is at the center of Minhaj’s life, especially when it comes to actual spice. “Some people want to drive at 85, some like a nice even 65, and some take it five miles below the limit,” he explains in a speedy analogy. “My spice level? I want to feel things in all caps. Life is to be lived. So I’m like, ‘Give it to me!’ Give me the full robustness of flavor that’s possible. I am a maximalist and love it all!”

His other spice of life is chai, “a form of commiseration, community, and love in Indian households—and an entry point to gossiping.” He loves that everyone does chai differently (he and his wife, Beena, prefer a more ginger-forward profile, while his parents like it sweeter). That’s why he became an investor and partner in the New York City-based Kolkata Chai in 2024, working with brothers Ani and Ayan Sanyal to expand the reach of a drink so engrained in their lives and culture. “It’s a welcome change to a lot of people’s only chai experience being at Starbucks in Middle America. It’s really amazing how you can change the complete perspective of an entire culture because of a drink that people can explore and make their own.”

Read on for Minhaj’s answers to our Amused Bouche questionnaire—it’s no surprise they’re chock-full of equally heartwarming and appetizing soundbites.

If you could only eat one thing 24/7/365, what would it be?

America is celebrating its 250th-year anniversary, and I think as a country, America’s greatest accomplishment is what we brought to cereal. I have been caught many a time red-handed with my full paw inside of some Golden Grahams, going to town. I will kill half a box, straight up, no milk. It is a snacking cereal. I’ll be watching a prestige drama and stress-eating Golden Grahams. Sometimes I’ll even drizzle honey over a bowl. I’ll also do the same with Honey Bunches of Oats. There’s a Montagues-and-Capulets debate here of blue box or orange box. I’m a blue box guy, because I love the almonds, extra crunch, and sweetness. It was my granola mix before I started eating granola, and it’s like trail mix without dastardly raisins coming in and f—ing shit up!

What’s the first thing you learned how to cook?

A strawberry Toaster Strudel, one of my all-time favorites. I popped it in the toaster as a kid, and it was an eye-opening experience. When I came home from elementary school, it felt like I was a master chef making myself this crisp little strudel cake and frosting it. I felt like a confectionery master. It was incredible. I toasted it on a level three and would sometimes pop it up and flip it midway through.

How about your latest kitchen adventure?

I actually didn’t hit my growth spurt until much later in high school, so I was quite undersized and malnourished-looking when I was younger. I was bullied a lot. My mother would make me eat three hard-boiled eggs on the way to school. And I hated hard-boiled eggs. Now that I’m a parent of small children, I understand the efficacy of hard-boiled eggs. But now I will pan-fry hard-boiled eggs, because really my hatred of them came from texture and temperature issues. But if you cut them in half and fry them yolk side down, there’s a crispness that makes it turn into a crunchy, almost taco-like topping that you can put on top of bread and butter. 

It’s a really delicious, warm-and-gooey snack that I started making when I tried to give my daughter a hard-boiled egg and she immediately spit it out. I’m like, “Yeah, the cycle of torture continues! There needs to be a better way! Let me figure out a way to make this delicious.” The secret was making a quesadilla as the on-ramp she needs to get eggs in her. It was an experiment I worked backward from, kind of like writing a joke, telling a story, or working on a screenplay. My daughter loves quesadillas but hates eggs, so by making it into crunchiness that would blend right in, it wouldn’t feel like a squishy thing that I’m force-feeding her.

What’s your treat-yourself splurge?

Growing up, we were never allowed to order dessert from restaurants. It is my greatest joy to order dessert from restaurants. I’ll tell the missus to order whatever she wants. Sometimes she’ll be indecisive. She always wants the chocolate lava cake, but she’ll be eyeing a berry tart. It’s a distant second, but I’ll take the bullet and order both for us to share. Sometimes when I do it, I’ll look over my shoulder to be like, “Are mom and dad going to find out?” But now I’m dad. We’re mom and dad!

What’s your most cherished cookbook?

I do not cook from cookbooks, but I will cook from YouTube Shorts. There’s just something about the looping nature of it breaking down the recipe. It’s tickling something in my mind and letting me know, “Hey, it’s not that hard. You can totally do it! You’ll get to completion, and it’s going to look pretty good.” And then as soon as doubt kicks in, the video starts playing again and snaps me out of it. It’s like the feeling of playing a song that you love over and over again, like “This Is How We Do It” by Montell Jordan. Play it again! “Men in Black”—play it again! I want to listen to it again, and that’s the feeling I get when I search “chicken tinga recipe” on YouTube Shorts.

Is there a cooking disaster that made you swear off a dish forever?

My wife does most of the cooking, but I want to put on the record that I try. I do cook, but a lot of the reaction is like, “Oh, you tried. I appreciate the effort. And that’s great for you. Why don’t you enjoy the omelet that you’ve made?” But I think I make a great soft-scramble, and what I bring to the breakfast table is pretty solid. I used to burn all of my food because I got impatient and cranked up the heat too fast. I learned you can’t bring microwave energy to a stove. Once I understood that foundational element in not burning your eggs, it was a huge unlock. Then I got to experience the full variety of eggs that aren’t charred.

Which nostalgic foods from childhood bring you the most comfort?

Dal chalwal: just lentils and rice. It has always been the first thing my mom makes for me when I get home to Sacramento, from college until now. It’s this beautiful gesture of love that I’m eating dal chawal with my mother, and her grandchildren are eating it with her at the same time. It’s pure comfort food and my love language.

When you’re playing dinner-party DJ, what’s spinning?

It’s a seasonal thing. Beena is really the entertainer and has been an incredible host since we met. For a holiday party, she’ll queue up a Pentatonix holiday playlist. Thematic, but a little left of center and unexpected.

What is your biggest entertaining flex to impress guests?

Beena loves a theme. We host Halloween on our block every year and call it Boo Bash. We go all out with a Halloween-themed live band, fog machines on the dance floor—aka the driveway—children’s face painting, bounce house, fake blood as drinks in IV bags. Her level of joy and imagination is unparalleled. She’s incredible. I add pizzazz, but I’m like, “Go off, queen!” She is the show. She’s the show creator, and I’m the showrunner.

Tell me about a meal so good you would hop on a flight to relive it.

My grandparents on both sides of my family have passed away, but I’m lucky enough that I was raised by my grandmother on my mother’s side from third to fifth grade. There’s a specific memory of when my grandmother made biryani. When the lid came off the pot and the steam and aroma of all the flavors escaped for the first time, she would put a ladle in and scoop it out for me, with meat that is so tender because it’s been cooking for so long. That’s the feeling that I would chase and book a flight immediately for, but it’s just a feeling. A feeling of love from it taking all day to make biriyani and that she loved me enough to put in that time and energy. Goat biriyani especially was a special-occasion dish, like when a cousin was getting married, and I associate it with being with family and something happy happening. In Hindi you call it ghar kha khana, the food from home. Biryani is the crème de la crème and the chef’s kiss of Indian cuisine for me. Especially my grandmother’s.

Loch Katrine Cake, at first glance, would seem to be a traditional Scottish bake; after all, the name sounds like it’s straight out of the Highlands.

Perfect for us here at Scottish Scran. Excellent.

But hold on to your mixing bowls; the recipe’s origins are a surprise and couldn’t have been further from Scotland if it tried!

However, first, let us explain precisely what we’re talking about.

What is Loch Katrine Cake?

Loch Katrine cake is a delightfully simple layered tray bake featuring a thin pastry base that’s easy to make, accompanied by a layer of light sponge filled with currants.

The cake is all topped with a thin layer of lemon icing that helps break up the sweetness and add a wee zing.

Where is Loch Katrine Cake From?

This deliciously simple cake actually hails from New Zealand, not Scotland! It’s a recipe that Sonja and her family have grown up with, made for its deliciousness as much as for its simplicity.

So, how did it come to have such an obviously Scottish name?

Why is it Called Loch Katrine Cake?

Let’s start on home soil before we head to the Southern Hemisphere. Loch Katrine in Scotland is a loch in the Trossachs, famously associated with Sir Walter Scott’s The Lady of the Lake and a source of water to Glasgow since 1859.

This makes Loch Katrine a pretty well-known loch, considering Scotland has 30’000 of them!

So, you would expect this to be where the cake has gained its name. And in a second-hand way, you would be right.

Sort of.

What you need to know is that nestled in North Canterbury, in New Zealand’s South Island, is a remote alpine lake, Loch Katrine, named by Scottish settlers who brought a piece of home with them across the world when they first began more organised migrating to New Zealand in the mid-1800s.

Around the same time, give or take a decade, the Scottish Loch Katrine would have been in the news about its new supply to Glasgow.

It should be noted that the lake is also known by its original Maori name, Waitetemoroiti. It is also worth noting that the Gaelic name for Loch Katrine is Loch Ceiteirein. Katrine is the modern English version and the name used in Scott’s poem.

After all that information about the name of the loch and, by default, the cake, there is no information as to why this tasty treat was named after Loch Katrine.

It first appears in New Zealand cookbooks; however, we can take a leap of faith to assume its origin and naming are due to the New Zealand body of water.

This all being the case, you could say that Sir Walter Scott and his popular poem were the reason Loch Katrine Cake got its name.

Why Make Loch Katrine Cake?

Why make Loch Katrine Cake? Because it is so deliciously tasty! The zing the lemon provides and the juice of the currants balance the sweetness of the sponge and icing.

Our kids thought this was one of the best things we’ve made, just sweet enough and wonderfully moreish.

Things You’ll Need to Make Loch Katrine Cake

The beauty of Loch Katrine Cake is in its simplicity. You don’t need anything fancy, just basic baking staples you already have in your kitchen. Here’s what you’ll need:

  • Baking tray 30x22cm
  • Sifter
  • Bowl
  • Clingfilm
  • Wooden spoon
  • Baking paper
  • A little butter to grease the tin

Ingredients for Loch Katrine Cake

Yield: 18 pieces approx
Prep time: 40 minutes
Baking time: 30 minutes

Base:

  • 190g Plain Flour (1.5 cups)
  • 2 tsp Baking Powder
  • 90g Unsalted Butter, cold and cubed
  • 4-5 tbsp Milk
  • 3-4 tbsp Raspberry Jam
  • 135g Currants (1 cup)

Sponge:

  • 90g Unsalted Butter, room temp
  • 150g Caster Sugar (3/4 cup)
  • 2 tsp Baking Powder
  • 190g Plain Flour (1.5 cups)
  • 3 Medium Eggs
  • 5-6 tbsp Milk

Icing:

  • 350g Icing Sugar
  • 3.5 tbsp Water/Lemon Juice (water for plain icing, lemon for lemon icing)
  • 1 tsp Vanilla Essence (if you are not doing lemon icing)

How to Make Loch Katrine Cake – Step-by-step Method

To start making Loch Katrine cake, we need to begin by making the pastry, which forms the base of the cake.

Sift the flour and baking powder into a mixing bowl.

Take your cold, cubed butter and rub it into the flour and baking powder mix slowly, using your fingers, until it resembles breadcrumbs.

Add milk gradually to make a stiff dough. You may need more or less milk depending on the mix. Work it slowly into the mix, stirring the milk and mixing together with a spoon or your hands. This will help avoid adding too much milk and making your dough too wet.

Once your dough is well mixed, your next step is to let it rest.

Wrap it in cling film and pop it in the fridge for 30 minutes. The reason you wrap the pastry is to prevent it from drying out.

Chilling the pastry allows the gluten in the flour to relax, and the fat to remain separate and firm when rolling it. This will help have a flaky and soft pastry once baked.

Grease and line your baking tin with baking paper, ensuring that you focus on the sides of the tin as well as the bottom.

Roll out your pastry after it’s chilled for 30 minutes, then use it to line the bottom of your greased tin. Cut off any excess.

Spread your raspberry jam evenly over the pastry, lightly covering the entire surface.

Sprinkle the currants onto the now jam-covered pastry, again trying to ensure an even spread.

Preheat your oven to 180°C / 160°C for fan-assisted (365°F/320°F).

In a new bowl, cream the room-temperature butter and caster sugar. Once the mix is creamed into a paste-like consistency, beat in the eggs one at a time.

Add the flour and baking powder to the bowl and mix them together gently.

Pour this mixture into the baking tin and spread it evenly over the pastry.

Bake for 30-35 minutes; all ovens vary slightly. Test the sponge by inserting a knife into it after you think it’s cooked and looking to see that the knife comes out clean when removed.

Allow to cool fully in the baking tin, before icing.

For lemon icing, mix lemon and water with the icing sugar; for vanilla icing, replace lemon with vanilla essence. Do try it with the lemon icing, it really does add to the taste of the cake.

Once well mixed together, spread the icing over the sponge thinly while still in the baking tin.

Allow to set before lifting the cake out of the tin. We used a fish slice very gently to move the cake from the tin to a plate.

Slice into rectangles or squares depending on your preference.

Then enjoy your Loch Katrine cake, we’d suggest with a cup of tea!

Yield: 18 approx

When Christmas is on the horizon, you start to look for typical Christmas flavours. There’s something comforting about them that helps to get you in the mood for the big day!

When thinking about a sweet treat that sums up the season, we created our Christmas Tiffin -and not just any Christmas Tiffin, but a boozy Christmas Tiffin! (Optional, of course…).

Not only does our Christmas Tiffin have the tastes and flavours that capture the time of year as well as any hallmark card, but it also has a kick to it that is reminiscent of a Christmas pudding or cake.

Ok, so it may not be a mince pie or a Christmas pud, but enjoying a square of boozy Christmas Tiffin will undoubtedly put you in the mood, make you yearn for a carol or two and start the debate about the best Christmas movie.

In our house, it’s Love Actually, in case you’re curious.

What is Tiffin?

Before we help you make a Christmas Tiffin with a kick, we thought we’d explain what exactly Tiffin is. For Scots, it’s very simple; Tiffin is a homemade treat enjoyed in cafes and homes across the country.

I myself didn’t know the actual name of this treat, but I’d been eating it for years in England and New Zealand. I’d always heard it called Fridge Cake, which is a blunt name, to say the least.

It was a happy day when I discovered its real name!

So, why do we call it Tiffin, and why is it Scottish? When you search for the term, you might find it refers to a lunch in India or even a container to hold it.

However, in Scotland, it specifically refers to a tasty traybake made from crushed biscuits, butter, sugar, and raisins or cherries, all topped with a layer of melted chocolate.

Chocolate Tiffin is said, by Wikipedia at least, to have originated in Troon, Scotland, in the early 1900s, though I haven’t found any concrete evidence to support this claim.

We do know that it has been enjoyed and accepted here as Scottish for as long as anyone can remember, and that’s good enough for us!

We’ve shared our classic Chocolate Tiffin recipe before, as well as a variation in our Biscoff Tiffin. With the Christmas season upon us, we thought it was time for a Christmas version!

What is boozy Christmas Tiffin?

Boozy Christmas Tiffin is a simple invention, taking our own delicious Chocolate Tiffin recipe and mixing it up a little. We have added spices to make sure it has that Christmassy flavour, plus dried cranberries, and on top of all of that, we’ve added a good measure of spiced rum.

We chose spiced rum for this recipe because of the extra flavours it provides on top of the slight taste of the alcohol itself. You can, of course, use whisky, and this works just as well. But if you’re a fan of spiced rum, then pick the one whose flavour works best for you and enjoy!

We used Red Leg Rum in this batch due to its Vanilla and Ginger flavouring, but Dark Matter, a Scottish Spiced rum, also works well with its peppercorn, ginger, and allspice berry flavouring.

We soak the raisins in the rum so that they absorb the flavour and then tip them all into the mix.

If you’d prefer to leave out the alcohol, then that’s no problem! The spices added still help to give it the flavour of Christmas.

And that, as they say, is that. Our Christmas Tiffin is the treat with a kick that, for many, sums up the holiday period perfectly!

It’s the gift that keeps on giving. Enjoy!

Things you’ll need to make Boozy Christmas Tiffin

  • 8x8inch cake tin (or 9inch for a thinner slice) – we like this one
  • Microwave safe jug
  • Ziplock bag and rolling pin for crushing the biscuits
  • Baking paper for lining the tin
  • Large saucepan
  • Wooden spoon

Ingredients for Boozy Christmas Tiffin

  • 300g Gingernuts (approx 30 biscuits)
  • 150g Unsalted Butter (2/3 Cup or 5.3oz)
  • 50g Golden Syrup (2.5 tbsp)
  • 40g Cocoa Powder (5 tbsp)
  • 40g Caster Sugar (3 tbsp)
  • 100g Raisins (approx 3/4 Cup)
  • 100g Dried Cranberries (approx 3/4 Cup)
  • 200g Milk Chocolate (7oz)
  • 50g White Chocolate (1.7oz)
  • 3-4tbsp Spiced Rum
  • 1 tbsp Mixed Spice (usually includes cinnamon, nutmeg, cloves, and ginger if not available to you)

How to make Boozy Christmas Tiffin – Step-by-step Method

Place the raisins in a wide bowl with the spiced rum, cover with cling film and place in the fridge overnight or for a couple of hours before. If you don’t have time for this, then you can add them both to the recipe, but it helps to not make the cake too wet if the raisins have had some time to absorb the rum.

Grease and line a 20cm/8″ square tin with baking paper.

Put the gingernuts in a ziplock bag and crush them with the rolling pin, leaving some bigger chunks.

Melt the butter, sugar, golden syrup, mixed spice and cocoa together in a large saucepan on the stovetop.

Once the mixture is melted together, remove from the heat and add the biscuits, raisin/rum mix and cranberries, ensuring they are well coated.

Transfer the mixture to your prepared tin and press it down into a compact, even layer with the back of a spoon.

Melt the chocolate in the microwave in 30-second bursts on medium heat, stirring in between. Pour the melted chocolate over the top of the tiffin base, smoothing it out into an even layer by tilting the tray.

Melt the white chocolate and pour in 3-4 lines over the top, then take a skewer or toothpick and use it to swirl the white chocolate into the milk chocolate (optionally, just use all milk chocolate).

Place in the fridge for at least two hours to set.

Slice with a sharp knife and enjoy!

Yield: 16 pieces
